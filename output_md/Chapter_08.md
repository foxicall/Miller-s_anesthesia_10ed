# CONSCIOUSNESS, MEMORY, AND ANESTHESIA

<!-- PDF page 1 -->


GEORGE A. MASHOUR and KANE O. PRYOR

### KEY POINTS

- Mechanisms of consciousness and memory, and their interruption by general anesthetics, are important scientific problems that have clinical relevance for the practice of anesthesiology.

- Consciousness, and its assessment, is a multidimensional phenomenon characterized by wakefulness, awareness, and behavioral expression.

- Anesthetics act at structures in the brainstem, hypothalamus, and basal forebrain that regulate sleep–wake states, which may account for loss of wakefulness.

- Anesthetics disrupt connectivity, communication, and flexible dynamics across cortical and thalamocortical networks, which may account for loss of awareness.

- Memory can be subdivided into explicit (conscious) and implicit (unconscious) recall; an example of explicit episodic recall is remembering a surgical event.

- Suppression of explicit episodic recall is one of the most potent effects of most general anesthetics.

- Effects on the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex—as well as the connectivity of these structures—may account for anesthetic- induced amnesia, even before loss of consciousness.

## Introduction

### SCIENTIFIC AND CLINICAL IMPORTANCE

Consciousness and memory are among the most fascinating and complex subjects in all of science. The richness of human consciousness and memory—and the ability to express this richness in language—is a defining characteristic of _Homo sapiens_ . Consciousness and memory also have clinical relevance for the anesthesiologist: together, the experience and explicit episodic recall of surgical events is known as the problem of “intraoperative awareness.” When formally assessed, this complication occurs in approximately one to two cases per 1000<sup>1–3</sup> and is associated with a high incidence of posttraumatic stress disorder.<sup>4,5</sup> The incidence of intraoperative consciousness without recall is substantially higher.<sup>6</sup> To advance the field of perioperative brain monitoring, a detailed understanding of the neurobiology of consciousness, memory, and anesthesia is required.

## Consciousness

### DEFINITIONS

The field of consciousness studies has been plagued by the indiscriminate use of the term “consciousness.” When we refer to consciousness, we mean subjective experience. In simple terms, it is what we lose when we have dreamless sleep and what we regain again in the morning upon awakening. However, there are several important definitions and distinctions to consider.

1. **Awareness:** Cognitive neuroscientists and philosophers use the term “awareness” to mean only subjective experience. In clinical anesthesiology, we (inaccurately) use the term “awareness” to include both consciousness and explicit episodic memory.<sup>7</sup> The taxonomy of memory will be discussed in the next major section of the chapter.

2. **Connected vs. Disconnected Consciousness:** Connected consciousness is the experience of environmental stimuli (such as surgery), whereas disconnected consciousness is an endogenous experience (such as a dream state).<sup>8</sup>

3. **Consciousness vs. Responsiveness:** An individual may fully experience a stimulus (such as the command “Open your eyes!”) but not be able to respond (as when a patient is paralyzed but conscious during surgery).<sup>9</sup>

4. **Conscious Level vs. Content:** Level of consciousness is the global state of arousal (e.g., awake or anesthetized) and content of consciousness is the qualitative aspect of experience (e.g., the redness of the rose).

Advances in neuroscience have enabled us to move beyond speculative frameworks and focus on a systemsbased approach to anesthetic- induced unconsciousness.<sup>10</sup> The remainder of this section on consciousness adopts a systems approach by discussing: (1) brainstem and hypothalamic nuclei regulating the sleep–wake cycle (and therefore arousal states) (Figs. 8.1 and 8.2); (2) the role of the thalamus in consciousness and anesthesia;

### SUBCORTICAL NUCLEI REGULATING AROUSAL

It was hypothesized in the mid- 1990s that anesthetics suppress consciousness by actions at the subcortical nuclei that evolved to control sleep–wake cycles.<sup>11</sup> The past decades have supported this hypothesis with correlative

evidence,<sup>12,13</sup> although recent data suggest that there is no trivial relationship between, for example, a single sleeppromoting nucleus and the anesthetized state.<sup>14</sup> The following is a description of select subcortical nuclei and regions that mediate arousal states<sup>15</sup> and, potentially, some traits of anesthesia.

### Brainstem

**Locus Coeruleus (LC).** Norepinephrine is synthesized in the LC, which is located in the pons and projects widely throughout the cortex.<sup>16</sup> Like other monoaminergic

<!-- PDF page 3 -->

neuronal populations, LC activity is highest during waking consciousness, decreased during non- rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, and at its nadir during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep.<sup>17,18</sup> Thus the LC is associated with cortical arousal only during wakefulness and not with the cortical activation during REM sleep. Norepinephrine transmission in the basal forebrain may be of particular relevance to anesthetic depth.<sup>19</sup> It has been found that LC noradrenergic neurons modulate the state of isoflurane anesthesia as well as emergence therefrom.<sup>20</sup> Of note, administration of ketamine is associated with an increase of activity in the LC,<sup>21</sup> which appears to contribute to its anesthetic effects.<sup>22</sup> The LC and the role of norepinephrine in hypnosis are of particular interest due to the use of the alpha- 2 agonist dexmedetomidine in clinical care. Microinjection of dexmedetomidine in the LC results in reduced levels of consciousness that can be prevented by coadministration of the alpha- 2 antagonist atipamezole.<sup>23</sup> After exposure to dexmedetomidine, brain changes somewhat mimic NREM sleep, in that the LC and tuberomammillary nucleus (TMN) are deactivated, while the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO) is activated.<sup>24</sup> Data in dopamine- beta- hydroxylase knockout mice (which lack the ability to synthesize norepinephrine) demonstrate a hypersensitivity to dexmedetomidine, suggesting alternative mechanisms of action.<sup>25</sup> However, selective knockdown of alpha- 2A adrenergic receptors from the LC prevent dexmedetomidine- induced loss of righting reflex,<sup>26</sup> a marker of general anesthesia in rodents.

**Laterodorsal/pedunculopontine tegmentum (LDT/ PPT).** Along with the basal forebrain, the LDT and PPT in the pons are the brain’s source of acetylcholine.<sup>27</sup> As with the noradrenergic LC, activity of the LDT/PPT is high during waking consciousness and decreases during NREM sleep.<sup>16</sup> However, in contrast to the LC and other monoaminergic neurons, the cholinergic LDT/PPT are also active during REM sleep, during which the cortex is aroused. Furthermore, activation of cholinergic neurons in LDT or PPT induces REM sleep.<sup>28</sup> Thus both states of cortical activation across the sleep–wake cycle are associated with high cholinergic tone. General anesthetics modulate cholinergic projections from the LDT/PPT. Sleep spindles occur during halothane anesthesia and are associated with decreased cholinergic transmission to the medial pontine reticular formation (PRF).<sup>29,30</sup> There is evidence that synaptic and extrasynaptic gamma- aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors play a role in modulating LDT neurons,<sup>31</sup> which could provide a direct link to molecular mechanisms of numerous general anesthetics.

**Pontine Reticular Formation (PRF).** The PRF is part of the reticular activating system, which plays an important role in cortical arousal. Although GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain, the actions of GABA in the PRF are associated with cortical arousal.<sup>32</sup> For example, there is increased time spent in the waking state when the GABAA receptor agonist muscimol is microinjected in the PRF.<sup>33</sup> When the GABAA antagonist bicuculline is microinjected, wakefulness is suppressed, but REM sleep (another state of cortical arousal) is triggered. Vanini and colleagues found that decreased levels of GABA in the PRF correlated with isoflurane- induced unconsciousness,

muscular hypotonia, and decreased respiratory rate.<sup>34</sup> Since the effects of anesthetics are normally associated with a potentiation of GABA activity, these findings highlight that a specific neuroanatomic and neurochemical milieu can play a unique and unexpected role in the mechanisms of consciousness and anesthesia. In addition, Vanini and colleagues found that GABA- ergic transmission in the rat PRF modulates the loss of consciousness induced by isoflurane but does not appear to affect emergence,<sup>35</sup> providing evidence for asymmetry between the two processes.

The mesopontine tegmental anesthesia area is in the PRF. When pentobarbital is microinjected in this area, a reversible state with anesthetic traits is induced.<sup>36</sup> This role of this area has been defined with greater spatial resolution, with the identification of around 1900 neurons that can induce general anesthesia.<sup>37</sup> Recent evidence has further supported and characterized the mesopontine tegmental anesthesia area as a mediator of general anesthesia.<sup>38,39</sup>

**Ventral tegmental area (VTA).** Dopaminergic neurons of the ventral tegmental area (VTA) in the midbrain were not classically considered key mediators of the sleep–wake cycle because of relatively less evidence of state- dependent changes compared with neurons in other brainstem nuclei. This view has been challenged in sleep neurobiology.<sup>40</sup> A dopaminergic pathway regulating sleep–wake states has been identified in _Drosophila_<sup>41</sup> and dopaminergic neurons of the VTA have more recently been found to play a role in mammalian sleep.<sup>42</sup> There has been renewed interest in the ability of dopaminergic activity to reverse or accelerate recovery from general anesthesia. Studies of the dopamine agonist methylphenidate have revealed an ability to reverse the sedative effects of both isoflurane and propofol in animal models.<sup>43,44</sup> VTA appears to be the source of the dopaminergic transmission mediating arousal during exposure to anesthesia, as evidenced by the fact that electrical stimulation of the VTA or selective stimulation of VTA dopaminergic neurons can reverse anesthetic- induced unconsciousness.<sup>45,46</sup>

### Hypothalamus

**Ventrolateral and Median Preoptic Nuclei (VLPO, MNPO).** The anterior hypothalamus has long been hypothesized to play a role in sleep–wake regulation.<sup>47</sup> VLPO is a structure in this region that transmits GABA and galanin.<sup>48</sup> Neurons in VLPO are maximally active during NREM and REM sleep<sup>49,50</sup> ; the median preoptic nucleus (MnPO) is also active during sleep. Of note, the activity profile of GABA- ergic neurons in VLPO correlates with sleep amount, whereas the activity of GABA- ergic neurons in MnPO correlates with homeostatic sleep pressure or propensity.<sup>51</sup> Importantly, activity of the VLPO during sleep is correlated with inhibition of other arousal centers in the brainstem and hypothalamus.<sup>49,52</sup> Given its potentially central role as a mediator of sleep, VLPO became an attractive candidate as a mediator of anesthetic- induced unconsciousness. Nelson and colleagues demonstrated activation of VLPO after systemic administration of propofol or pentothal<sup>53</sup> ; additional studies have examined the mechanistic significance of these findings. Eikermann and colleagues conducted studies of rats with chronic lesions of VLPO, finding that ablation of VLPO resulted in sleep

deprivation (as expected) but conferred increased sensitivity to the effects of isoflurane.<sup>54</sup> Moore and colleagues demonstrated that acute lesions of VLPO conferred resistance to the effects of isoflurane, an effect that appeared to be mediated specifically through the sleep- active neurons in VLPO.<sup>55</sup> These neurons are actually depolarized (i.e., activated) by isoflurane. However, more recent data have demonstrated that chemogenetic stimulation of GABAergic neurons in this region affects sleep architecture but not anesthetic state transitions.<sup>14</sup> In fact, there are newly identified populations of neurons in this region that can promote wakefulness.<sup>56,57</sup> Furthermore, there has been a shift of attention to the supraoptic nucleus of the hypothalamus (located posterior to the VLPO), with a subpopulation of neuroendocrine cells that appear to influence both slow- wave sleep and general anesthesia induced by diverse drugs.<sup>58</sup>

**Orexinergic Neurons.** Orexinergic neurons are found in the lateral hypothalamus and provide an important arousal stimulus for the cortex. Orexinergic neurons, which innervate other arousal centers in the brainstem and basal forebrain and fire maximally in the waking state, are suppressed during NREM sleep, and show occasional bursts during phasic REM sleep.<sup>59,60</sup> Dysfunction of the orexinergic system is associated with narcolepsy in both humans and animal models.<sup>61,62</sup> The often- dramatic delay of anesthetic emergence in narcoleptic patients<sup>63</sup> has motivated the study of orexin in anesthetic mechanism. Orexins attenuate the effects of isoflurane,<sup>64</sup> propofol,<sup>65</sup> ketamine,<sup>66</sup> and barbiturates,<sup>67</sup> using various measures. Local infusion of orexin in the basal forebrain is associated with electroencephalographic (EEG) arousal and decreased emergence time in animals anesthetized with sevoflurane<sup>68</sup> and isoflurane.<sup>69</sup> Microinjection of propofol in the perifornical region of the hypothalamus (the locus of orexinergic neurons) is associated with a decrease in cortical acetylcholine, an important mediator of arousal.<sup>70</sup> Importantly, both genetic and pharmacologic studies have demonstrated that orexins play an important role in emergence from sevoflurane and isoflurane anesthesia, but not induction.<sup>71</sup> This seminal study suggested that there is a distinct neurobiology of induction and emergence, and formed the basis for a theory of “neural inertia” across state transitions.<sup>72</sup> Of note, halothane did not show an effect on orexinergic neurons and emergence time was not altered in orexin knockout mice<sup>73</sup> but differential effects on induction and emergence have been confirmed for propofol anesthesia.<sup>74</sup> Orexins might facilitate emergence from anesthesia through actions in the basal forebrain.<sup>75</sup>

**Basal Forebrain.** The basal forebrain is a collection of nuclei with diverse neurotransmitter systems that regulate wakefulness and sleep.<sup>76–78</sup> There has been a particular focus on cholinergic neurons in the reversal of the anesthetized state or acceleration of emergence from general anesthesia. Basal forebrain cholinergic activity appears to be important for isoflurane, propofol, and ketamine anesthesia; activation of these neurons can accelerate emergence from GABA- ergic anesthetics.<sup>79–81</sup> Electrical stimulation of the basal forebrain in rats can reverse the traits of isoflurane

anesthesia and promote wakefulness; importantly, this effect appears to be mediated through the prefrontal cortex.<sup>82</sup>

### ROLE OF THE THALAMUS

The thalamus is composed of more than 50 nuclei and subnuclei that can be grossly classified as relays for sensory input from the periphery or multimodal, integrative regions that receive input from the cortex. Additionally, the thalamus is critical for transmitting arousal signals from the brainstem and for regulating cortical communication. The involvement of the thalamus in arousal, sensory processing, and cortical computation is likely critical for normal consciousness. As such, the thalamus has been of continued interest to those investigating mechanisms of anesthetic- induced unconsciousness.

The thalamus has long been proposed as an ON/OFF switch for anesthetic state transitions.<sup>83</sup> This theory was generated based on the consistent metabolic depression of the thalamus by a number of inhaled and intravenous anesthetics<sup>84–86</sup> (with the exception of ketamine),<sup>87</sup> suggesting that it could serve as an effective OFF switch. The hyperpolarization of the thalamus would shift tonic firing to burst firing that—as with sleep—would prevent afferent sensory stimuli from arousing the cortex. Importantly, however, sensory nuclei in the thalamus (and their connectivity to the cortex) appear to be less involved in anesthetic- induced unconsciousness compared to the higher- order or “nonspecific” nuclei.<sup>88,89</sup>

Evidence for the thalamus as an ON switch has been derived primarily from animal experiments, in which stimulation of the centromedial thalamus by either nicotine or antibodies blocking voltage- gated potassium channels could reverse the effects of inhaled anesthetics.<sup>90,91</sup> Although microinjection of large doses of nicotine into the thalamus could precipitate anesthetic emergence, antagonism of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in the same location did not appear to contribute to anesthetic- induced unconsciousness. Central thalamic activation results in behavioral improvement in humans with traumatic brain injury.<sup>92</sup> A study in humans demonstrated that (spontaneous) activation of the thalamus along with other subcortical structures is correlated with recovery from anesthesia, suggesting the involvement of the thalamus in the primitive or “core” consciousness observed at emergence.<sup>93</sup> Recent studies in nonhuman primates have demonstrated that electrical stimulation of the thalamus can reverse the traits of anesthesia and induce neurophysiologic signatures of consciousness and arousal.<sup>94,95</sup>

Entrainment of the thalamus might also play an active role in anesthetic- induced unconsciousness. A computational study using human EEG data and modeling suggested that the action of propofol on GABA receptors in the nucleus reticularis generates a hypersynchronous alpha rhythm (8–13 Hz) with the frontal cortex.<sup>96</sup> Hypersynchrony of alpha may block the flexible corticocortical communication required for normal consciousness.<sup>97</sup> An animal study identified alpha synchronization between thalamus and medial prefrontal cortex during propofol induction.<sup>98</sup> The potential role of thalamocortical

### CORTICAL–SUBCORTICAL CONNECTIVITY

Studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have refined the role of thalamocortical connectivity in anesthesia. One study identified a propofol- induced disruption of connectivity between the thalamus and lateral frontal–parietal networks.<sup>99</sup> Similarly, a study of the specific nuclei (linked to particular sensory modalities) and nonspecific nuclei (linked to integrative functions) found that disrupted connectivity between the nonspecific nuclei and the cortex best accounted for a reduction in the level of consciousness by propofol.<sup>88</sup> The inhaled anesthetic sevoflurane has been shown to functionally disconnect the thalamus and cortex, especially frontal cortex.<sup>100,101</sup> A consistent finding of neuroimaging studies is that the thalamocortical connectivity of primary sensory networks is relatively well preserved despite anesthetic- induced unconsciousness.

The finding of impaired thalamocortical connectivity in association with anesthetic- induced unconsciousness has not been universal. A fMRI study of propofol revealed more profound functional disconnections between the cortex and putamen, a subcortical structure in the basal ganglia.<sup>102</sup> In contrast, thalamic connectivity was relatively well preserved. The potential role of the striatum (composed of the putamen and caudate) in anesthetic- induced unconsciousness has been demonstrated in a study of rats undergoing isoflurane anesthesia.<sup>103</sup> This study was conducted with fMRI and found that the functional connection between the frontal cortex and the basal ganglia was disrupted during general anesthesia. Functional disconnection of association cortex and subcortical structures has also been shown by fMRI in a study of propofol- induced unconsciousness in humans.<sup>104</sup> Recent work has identified a lateralization of subcortical connectivity (thalamus, ascending reticular activating system) during sevoflurane anesthesia.<sup>105</sup>

### CORTICAL CONNECTIVITY AND DYNAMICS

The last three sections were organized according to a bottom- up approach to consciousness and anesthesia, starting with the brainstem, and then moving to the diencephalon and thalamocortical system. Sleep is clearly generated through such bottom- up mechanisms<sup>15</sup> ; however, anesthetics may suppress the level of consciousness through bottom- up mechanisms and content of consciousness through top- down (i.e., cortical) mechanisms.<sup>12</sup>

Early studies using positron emission tomography (PET) demonstrated regional depression in cortical areas, including lateral and medial frontal–parietal networks.<sup>106</sup> Disruption of fMRI- based functional connectivity in frontal–parietal networks has been demonstrated during anesthetic- induced unconsciousness induced by drugs with distinct molecular mechanisms, including propofol,<sup>99,107</sup> sevoflurane,<sup>100</sup> and ketamine.<sup>108</sup> However, the cortex is amenable not only to fMRI but also neurophysiological techniques of assessing connectivity, enabling data on anesthetic- induced unconsciousness with improved

temporal resolution. EEG can be used to measure functional connectivity (the statistical covariation of the activities of brain regions), and directional or effective connectivity (the presumed causal influence of one brain region on another).<sup>109</sup> Using such techniques applied to EEG (in some cases with concomitant fMRI), disruption of frontal–parietal connectivity and surrogates of information exchange in humans has been consistently observed after administration of a variety of anesthetics with diverse molecular targets.<sup>101,110–113</sup> It has been suggested that such disruption of frontal–parietal connectivity, with implications for impaired information transfer of relevance to consciousness, might be a common mediator and proximate cause of anesthetic- induced unconsciousness.<sup>114</sup> However, it is also becoming clear that corticocortical connectivity during the anesthetized state fluctuates dynamically, even during pharmacokinetically stable periods.<sup>115,116</sup>

On a more global brain level, high- density EEG and transcranial magnetic stimulation revealed an inhibition of cortical effective connectivity after midazolam- induced unconsciousness.<sup>117</sup> After administration of the benzodiazepine, local cortical activation could be observed at the site of magnetic stimulation, but robust evoked potentials were terminated at less than 100 milliseconds, and cortical communication was limited. Of note, this finding is consistent with findings in NREM sleep.<sup>118</sup> This perturbational approach determined that the complexity of cortical response to a stimulus decreased during sleep, general anesthesia, and disorders of consciousness.<sup>119</sup> The concordant findings across physiologic (sleep) and pharmacologic (anesthesia) states of unconsciousness<sup>120</sup> may reflect a common neurophysiologic mechanism of disrupted cortical connectivity through slow oscillations, which share a number of characteristics in NREM sleep and general anesthesia.<sup>121</sup> A study of three epilepsy patients with intracranial neurophysiologic monitoring<sup>122</sup> revealed that, within 5 seconds of propofol- induced unconsciousness, there was a dramatic increase in the power of slow oscillations. Although single- unit neuronal activity was initially suppressed, it returned to baseline (or above baseline) but was fragmented into highly active and quiescent periods. Neural firing became coupled with the slow oscillation. However, the slow oscillations themselves demonstrated decay in phase coupling with increased distance across the cortex. Thus, neuronal spike activity became fragmented into “on” and “off” periods, which became temporally uncoordinated across the cortex. These neurophysiologic conditions dramatically reduce the probability of meaningful corticocortical communication.

More recent trends of analyzing cortical changes during states of unconsciousness take a dynamic approach that reflects not only static connectivity configurations but also the repertoire of states that can be accessed during general anesthesia. For example, there is a contraction of dynamic repertoire and neural signal diversity during propofol- induced unconsciousness<sup>123,124</sup> that would preclude the kind of flexibility required for normal conscious experience. Complexity and criticality have emerged as analytical tools to capture dynamic changes during anesthetic state transitions. In the neurophysiologic context, complexity is a surrogate for the diversity of neural signals and the differentiation of conscious processing. Complexity could be regarded as a complement to connectivity, which is a surrogate for integration of conscious processing. Differentiation and integration represent two key components of both conscious experience and the neurobiology that underlies it. Complexity decreases during propofol- and sevofluraneinduced general anesthesia.<sup>130–132</sup> The effects of ketamine on complexity are dose- dependent, with increases at subanesthetic dosing consistent with psychedelic effects; with anesthetic dosing, there is a dynamic toggling between normal levels and depressed levels consistent with a GABA- ergic anesthetic.<sup>133</sup> Complexity is also reduced during general anesthesia in children and appears to increase with development throughout adolescence.<sup>134</sup> Criticality describes a state of the system poised on the border of phase transitions, such as order and disorder. Criticality creates a platform for brain networks to shift dynamically in response to external or internal demands.<sup>135</sup> It has been demonstrated that the

dynamics of the anesthetized brain deviate from criticality,<sup>136,137</sup> which likely constrains the flexibility and repertoire of brain states required for conscious experience.

## Memory

### SIGNIFICANCE OF AMNESIA IN MEMORY RESEARCH

Modern understanding of the organization and neuroscientific basis of human memory owes much to the study of amnesia. The most famed example of this occurred in 1957, when Brenda Milner of the Montreal Neurological Institute reported the remarkable case of Henry Gustav Molaison (1926–2008),<sup>138</sup> an amnesiac known only as H.M. until after his death and the single most influential case study in the history of cognitive neuroscience. In a procedure intended to treat a refractory seizure disorder, significant portions of H.M.’s medial temporal lobe (MTL) were removed bilaterally, including the hippocampus, amygdala, and parahippocampal gyrus. He developed profound and enduring anterograde amnesia and was unable to establish any new conscious (explicit) memory, irrespective of the sensory modality. He also developed a temporally graded window of retrograde amnesia, with impaired recall of events occurring during the three years preceding his surgery. However, he could remember more distant events,

<!-- PDF page 7 -->

learn procedural tasks (even though he could not remember the learning session), and retained largely or entirely intact cognitive functions, including perceptual processing, language, attention, access to semantic knowledge, and the capacity to retain small packages of information in constant rehearsal (a form of working memory). Prior to the description of H.M., the prevailing theory—articulated by Karl Lashley and Donald Hebb—was that there was no brain region specifically dedicated to memory function. Instead, memory processes were thought to be distributed and integrated into region- specific perceptual and cognitive functions. For example, the visual attribute of a memory would be wholly served within the striate and extrastriate cortical regions responsible for visual perception. The description of H.M. immediately disproved this model. It became clear that the MTL was a specialized and obligatory structure for the establishment and early maintenance of all conscious memory.

The trajectory of memory research was profoundly transformed. Early branches focused on the structural–functional organization of the MTL (Fig. 8.4A) and the nature of cellular- level neuroplastic processes – the latter notably marked by the description of long- term potentiation (LTP) by Timothy Bliss and Terje Lømo in 1973.<sup>139</sup> Subsequently, emerging technologies enabled the investigation of systemslevel constructs. Examples include the use of EEG and magnetoencephalography to assess the role of oscillatory phase synchronization in neuronal assemblies,<sup>140</sup> fMRI to identify large- scale networks associated with specific memory functions,<sup>141</sup> and machine learning to classify complex neural network patterns predictive of memory.<sup>142</sup>

### TERMINOLOGY

Amnesia is the term used to describe one of the cardinal properties of general anesthesia. As understood by most anesthesiologists and laypeople, this description is phenomenological: it states that patients do not recall events that occur while receiving anesthesia. However, this usage confuses a critical mechanistic and semantic distinction. Patients in the deepest states of anesthesia are unable to process and bind perceptual elements into an integrated conscious experience. From the perspective of cognitive neuroscience, the “amnesia” of general anesthesia is not a primary failure of memory, but rather a failure of consciousness. It simply reflects that a conscious experience cannot be reconstructed by memory processes when it never existed in the first place. Further confusion is added by the frequent use of the term “awareness”—a synonym for conscious perception in the present—to describe the case in which a patient consciously recalls events occurring during the administration of an anesthetic. This ignores the fundamental principle that memory is functionally dissociable from consciousness. Awareness is necessary for the establishment of memory under anesthesia, but it is not sufficient. Conscious recall can occur only if awareness is accompanied by memory processes in the MTL and elsewhere that establish and preserve a representation that can be reconstructed later. The term “amnesia” accurately refers to a failure of these memory- specific processes such that a conscious event is not transformed into an enduring memory.

It is axiomatic that patients who form memories while under anesthesia cannot have been in a state of true unconscious oblivion; they must have possessed some conscious substrate from which the memory derives. However, the converse inference is not always true: the existence of consciousness will only rarely lead to the existence of memory if an anesthetic drug is present. Evidence of this is unambiguously encountered in everyday anesthetic practice: in patients receiving a low dose of propofol or midazolam who engage in a cogent conversation that they are later unable to recall, or in those emerging from general anesthesia who follow commands to demonstrate that extubation can proceed safely, yet later cannot recall anything related to this clearly conscious event. Anesthetic drugs must therefore have direct effects on memory processes that are dissociable from those on consciousness—in other words, act as true amnestic agents—and it is this observation that provides a framework for the systematic study of how anesthetic drugs affect memory.

## Organization and Function of Normal Memory

### THE DECLARATIVE MEMORY SYSTEM

When the term “memory” is used in everyday language, it almost always refers to declarative (or explicit) memory. Declarative memory is a representation of prior events and knowledge that is accessible to consciousness and can be manipulated by attention and executive function. It is the form of memory of greatest interest in the context of anesthetic amnesia.

Further important organizational structure exists within declarative memory. The first is the distinction between episodic and semantic memory. Episodic memory is the recollection of events with a clear spatiotemporal context (as when recalling autobiographical events with a distinct sense of personal experience, time, and place), whereas semantic memory is the capacity to recall and apply meaning, facts, and knowledge without spatiotemporal context (as when recalling that Mount Everest is the highest mountain in the world without any sense of time and place for the acquisition of that knowledge). The episodic memory system is fast- mapping and highly dependent on the MTL as well as frontal, parietal, and anterior thalamic structures,<sup>143–145</sup> whereas the semantic memory system is slower and involves distributed cortical regions that closely map to the default mode network, a large- scale system that is active during the resting state and flow of spontaneous cognition.<sup>146,147</sup>

The second organizational structure within episodic memory is the distinction between recollection and familiarity. Recollection involves remembering specific qualitative contextual details about a prior event, whereas in a familiarity judgment, there is a sense that an item has been encountered previously, but beyond that there are no added contextual details. Over the last two decades the prevailing understanding has been Aggleton and Brown’s “dualprocess” model,<sup>148</sup> in which recollection and familiarity arise from distinct processes and neural architecture within the MTL. ### NON- DECLARATIVE MEMORY SYSTEMS

Findings that H.M. and other amnesiacs could learn a hand–eye coordination skill even while possessing no memory of the task led to the distinction between declarative and procedural memory,<sup>150</sup> which is dependent on the caudate nucleus. Memory- impaired patients were also found to have intact priming,<sup>151</sup> which is a nonconscious process in which exposure to a stimulus influences the response to a later stimulus – for example, amnesiacs can name pictures 100 milliseconds faster if they have seen them previously, despite having no declarative memory of the exposure.<sup>152</sup> The neural bases for priming effects involve regions serving perceptual and contextual processing and so vary with the nature of the task,<sup>153</sup> but recent evidence also implicates the involvement of common processing regions.<sup>154</sup> Lastly, a large body of work has extensively elucidated the emotional memory system. The classic experimental model is Pavlovian fear conditioning and its variants, wherein an emotionally neutral conditioned stimulus is paired with an aversive unconditioned stimulus, leading to an involuntary associative physiologic and/or behavioral response to the conditioned stimulus. The circuitry involves convergence of afferent information from all sensory modalities onto the lateral nucleus of the amygdala, with efferents from the basolateral and central nuclei projecting widely to modulate processes in cortical and subcortical regions, including those involved in memory (Fig 8.4B).<sup>155,156</sup> With the progressive description of these and other functional distinctions, the framework for understanding memory eventually shifted to models that contained multiple memory systems, distributed functionally and anatomically.

### WORKING MEMORY

Working memory refers to the capacity to maintain limited amounts of information in the stream of consciousness, which can be manipulated to perform complex cognitive tasks such as reasoning, comprehension, and learning.<sup>157,158</sup> The concept evolved from, and has largely replaced, earlier ideas about short- term memory, but the terms should not be used interchangeably, as working memory implies both a short- term memory store and the capacity for volitional manipulation. The most influential current model, first proposed by Baddeley and Hitch in 1974,<sup>159</sup> divides working memory into capacity- limited component subsystems: a phonological loop that maintains information through vocal or subvocal rehearsal, such as when one holds a telephone number in mind; a visuospatial sketchpad, which holds and manipulates spatial, visual, and kinesthetic information; and a central executive, which is responsible for regulating selective attention and inhibition. A fourth subsystem, the episodic buffer, was later added to the model and is responsible for binding and integrating multimodal representations.<sup>160</sup> The MTL was long thought to have no role in working memory, but it is now recognized that the hippocampus is involved in visuospatial processing.<sup>161</sup> Current understanding of working memory suggests that it is dominantly served by persistent activity and flexible resource allocation in a distributed cortical network: a critical hub in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) is interconnected with

parietal cortex, thalamus, caudate, and globus pallidus.<sup>162</sup> The functional and structural distinction between working memory networks and the MTL does not mean that working memory and declarative memory systems do not interact. Working memory depends on declarative memory representations to provide semantic meaning and context. During working memory tasks, both hippocampus and cortical perceptual areas associated with representations of declarative memory become activated and show increased synchrony with prefrontal regions.<sup>163,164</sup> Reciprocally, encoding of declarative memory is strongly influenced by the nature of processing occurring in working memory, with deeper levels of executive processing resulting in better learning.<sup>163,165</sup> The transition of memory from short- term stores through to stable long- term stores is experienced as continuous and was assumed to reflect sequential transfer across systems. However, this model is challenged by rare case examples of patients who have a selective short- term memory deficit but intact declarative memory function, and recent studies have reinforced the view that memories are formed in multiple systems in parallel.<sup>166</sup>

### LONG- TERM POTENTIATION (LTP), SYNAPTIC TAGGING, AND THE CONSOLIDATION MODEL OF MEMORY

The consolidation hypothesis of memory<sup>167</sup> was first proposed by Müller and Pilzecker in 1900. They noted that memory for new information could be disrupted by learning other information shortly after the initial training. This effect, called retroactive interference, is temporally graded such that the susceptibility of the memory is greatest immediately after learning and decreases with time. Müller and Pilzecker proposed that the memory trace must initially exist in a fragile state, but subsequently becomes stable through the process of consolidation. Although continually refined, the consolidation hypothesis remains the framework for understanding the temporal course of memory processes and behavior.

For a memory trace to be consolidated, it must of course be created. The term used to describe this process is encoding. Encoding implies that the networks mediating the neural representation of an event as it is experienced do not immediately return to their previous state and are modified in such a way that potentiates reactivation of that representation. The synaptic plasticity and memory hypothesis states that activity- induced synaptic plasticity is both necessary and sufficient for the information storage underlying memory,<sup>168</sup> and within this framework encoding implies that some form of synaptic plasticity has been initiated. However, encoding cannot assure the propagation of a memory trace. Encoding creates the potential for the formation of a long- term memory.

The minimal events that constitute the neural correlates of encoding are incompletely understood. Cellular models demonstrate that functional changes in synaptic strength can occur in the absence of any structural change in dendritic spines.<sup>169</sup> The perpetuation of these initial changes through structural and functional remodeling represents the neural correlate of memory consolidation. The prevailing cellular model for this is LTP,<sup>170</sup> which describes a durable increase in synaptic transmission efficiency

<!-- PDF page 10 -->

following a stimulation protocol. It is now recognized that LTP occurs richly throughout the hippocampus, as well as in other afferent pathways. LTP can be induced by nonphysiologic high- frequency stimulation, but also by stimulation protocols that resemble physiologic activity, the most important being bursts in the theta range (4–8 Hz).<sup>171</sup> This is highly relevant to memory, as synchronized hippocampal theta oscillations appear critical to successful memory behaviors.<sup>140</sup>

The breadth and depth of literature on the mechanisms of LTP are far too voluminous to summarize here. Nonetheless, certain principles are essential and relevant to anesthesia studies and can be stated succinctly. The induction of most forms of LTP requires activation of postsynaptic voltagedependent _N_ - methyl- d- aspartate (NMDA) receptors,<sup>172</sup> followed by influx of Na<sup>+</sup> and Ca<sup>2+</sup> . This rise in intracellular Ca<sup>2+</sup> is the critical trigger for LTP. Calcium- calmodulindependent kinase II (CaMKII) is then activated<sup>173</sup> and autophosphorylated,<sup>174</sup> leading to cytoskeletal reconfiguration. Activation of several other cell- signaling cascades also contributes to LTP. The terminal expression of LTP is protein synthesis, occurring in both the soma and local dendrites, and resulting in enduring structural changes at the synapse.<sup>175</sup> Protein synthesis inhibitors have been demonstrated to consistently prevent sustained LTP _in vitro_ and learning _in vivo_ .<sup>176</sup>

LTP thus proceeds in two phases: early LTP (E- LTP) is independent of protein synthesis and can be sustained across an interval of minutes to a short number of hours, while late LTP (L- LTP) is dependent on intracellular signaling and protein synthesis and can be sustained across many days. The synaptic tagging and capture hypothesis provides a mechanistic explanation for the observation that the persistence of synaptic potentiation (and memory) is influenced by events surrounding encoding.<sup>177</sup> In this model, synapses activated during E- LTP become tagged via a protein synthesis- independent mechanism. This tag establishes the potential for sustained L- LTP, but for the cascade to continue the tags must capture plasticity- related proteins (PRPs) synthesized in the soma or dendrites in response to neural activity. The synaptic tagging model explains how the thousands of dendrites of a single neuron can be engaged in memory stabilization processes in various states of evolution, because the tagging and PRP capture need not occur as a singular event.

### RECONSOLIDATION

In 2000 Nader et al. reported that an old memory for auditory fear conditioning, which would normally not be sensitive to protein inhibitors, can be made newly sensitive if it is retrieved.<sup>178</sup> The implication is that retrieval of a memory renders it transiently plastic, after which it restabilizes. This process is termed reconsolidation, and the period of plasticity is termed the reconsolidation window. The mechanism shares many of the LTP processes that are associated with initial consolidation, but also has features that are quite distinct at both the cellular and systems level.<sup>179</sup> Reconsolidation serves as a modulatory process that enables the strengthening of an existing memory, but it is also clear that it provides a window within which an existing memory is malleable and can be updated with the addition of novel

information, or diminished through interruption of restabilization processes.<sup>180</sup> These latter properties have generated significant translational interest because of repeated demonstrations of the ability to modify or even eliminate fear memory in animals through pharmacologic and behavioral interventions that interrupt reconsolidation.<sup>181</sup> Indeed, the erasure of fear memory in humans can be demonstrated through performance of a behavioral extinction procedure during the reconsolidation window.<sup>182</sup>

### PHASE SYNCHRONIZATION AND COUPLING

The neurons in assemblies and networks undergo oscillatory activation and inhibition. Phase synchronization of these oscillations supports neural communication by creating transient and dynamic associations between different functional brain regions. Phase synchronization appears to be fundamental to neural plasticity and memory,<sup>140</sup> and numerous studies have demonstrated that dynamics of synchrony during memory tasks can be related to both long- term<sup>143</sup> and working memory<sup>183</sup> performance. Gamma- band (30–100 Hz) synchrony is believed to support an important form of Hebbian plasticity, termed spiketiming- dependent plasticity, in the hippocampus. Both computational models and experimental data provide evidence that rapid changes in the frequency and phase of gamma rhythms modulate this form of plasticity through coordination of presynaptic spikes in assemblies of neurons, effectively classifying which assemblies are interpreted as a single event.<sup>184</sup>

Another property of relevance to memory coding is the coupling of gamma oscillations to the phase of slower, synchronized oscillations within the theta band (4–8 Hz).<sup>185</sup> Theta oscillations, which are prominent in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, undergo phase resetting in response to a stimulus. The phase reset involves widely distributed regions, and is thought to serve as an efficient mechanism for the optimization of interregional communication.<sup>186</sup> Theta phase resetting and synchrony in the hippocampal–entorhinal system are implicated in declarative memory,<sup>187</sup> and in the amygdala–hippocampal system in fear- based memory.<sup>188</sup> Coupling between theta phase and gamma amplitude is connected to declarative memory formation,<sup>187</sup> and gamma subcycles nested within theta cycles are proposed to form a code for representing multiple items and spatial representations in an ordered way.<sup>189</sup> Coupling between theta phase and gamma phase is a control mechanism that enables a more precise temporal coordination of neuronal spikes, and may code for separated representations of multiple items in working memory.<sup>190</sup>

## Effects of Anesthetic Drugs on Declarative Memory Function in Humans

There are multiple potential pathways by which anesthetic drugs could interfere with memory and cause amnesia. Many of these cannot be directly assessed in humans, but the plausibility of candidate mechanisms can be indirectly examined by designing experiments that are informed by

<!-- PDF page 11 -->

the characteristics of known memory processes. Studies of anesthetic effects on memory are most informative when conceptualized and designed with reference to robust methods and findings taken from the larger body of memory research.

### BEHAVIORAL STUDIES OF RETROGRADE MEMORY EFFECTS

Notwithstanding some isolated case reports, systematic investigations have found little evidence that anesthetic drugs predictably cause retrograde amnesia in humans. Studies have found no retrograde amnesia associated with induction doses of thiopental,<sup>191</sup> methohexital,<sup>191</sup> or propofol.<sup>192</sup> A randomized, double- blind trial found that adult patients have normal memory for visual stimuli presented 4 minutes before administration of midazolam at doses of 2, 5, and 10 mg.<sup>193</sup> Similarly, memory is normal for word lists learned in the preoperative holding area or operating room 2–4 minutes before induction.<sup>194</sup> One recent study in parturients reported a retrograde effect for pictures shown 1 minute prior to midazolam 0.1 mg/kg, but this was a cohort study in which the subjects self- selected whether or not to receive midazolam.<sup>195</sup> Another recent study suggested a narrow window of retrograde inhibition of free recall but not recognition,<sup>196</sup> which is difficult to interpret. Studies in pediatric patients have shown normal memory for pictures presented immediately before sedation with midazolam,<sup>197</sup> propofol,<sup>198</sup> and dexmedetomidine.<sup>199</sup> In controlled laboratory settings using human volunteers and target- controlled infusions, studies have found no impairment of memory for pictures<sup>200</sup> or words<sup>201,202</sup> presented before sedative infusions of propofol, midazolam, thiopental, or dexmedetomidine.

There is some evidence from studies in highly controlled settings that anesthetic drugs may cause the opposite effect, in which memory for material presented prior to drug administration is enhanced – a phenomenon termed retrograde facilitation. In one study using a mildly sedative dose of propofol,<sup>203</sup> memory for words learned immediately prior to drug administration was enhanced throughout the testing period of 24 hours. Similar effects are described for midazolam and other benzodiazepines in the psychopharmacology literature.<sup>204</sup> The mechanistic explanation for retrograde facilitation is that it results from the pharmacologic suppression of retrograde interference, an active process in which the encoding of new memories inhibits the consolidation of recently formed ones, with the newest memories being most vulnerable.<sup>205</sup> The cellular correlate is that induction of new LTP interferes with recently formed LTP and memory performance, even when the tasks are unrelated.<sup>206</sup> However, when the induction of new LTP is blocked by administration of a selective NMDA antagonist after learning, interference with recently formed LTP does not occur, and memory performance improves.<sup>207</sup> A parsimonious explanation for the retrograde facilitation seen with propofol and benzodiazepines is that they similarly modulate the induction of new LTP via a GABA- ergic pathway, freeing consolidation resources that enhance survival of recently formed memory.

Findings of retrograde facilitation and absence of retrograde amnesia suggest that the key mechanisms of

GABA- ergic anesthetic amnesia, at least in humans, involve very early processes in the consolidation cascade. If processes further downstream were the principal targets, the expected effect would be that administration of an anesthetic drug would interfere with ongoing consolidation of memory for events occurring in the past, creating a reliable window of retrograde amnesia. However, rodent studies have demonstrated that GABA- ergic anesthetics can exert a direct effect on downstream protein transcription processes,<sup>208</sup> so there remains a gap in resolving the findings from animal studies with behavioral observations in humans.

The absence of pharmacologically induced retrograde amnesia does not dismiss the repeated finding that, in the clinical setting, some patients will not recall the immediate preanesthetic period – an effect that increases with age.<sup>209</sup> Although not systematically studied, the complex noradrenergic- mediated effects of acute stress and anxiety on memory may be contributory,<sup>210</sup> as could effects on attention.

### ANESTHETIC EFFECTS ON ATTENTION AND AROUSAL AS MODULATORS OF ENCODING

Anesthetic effects on encoding are mediated by changes in attentional processes. Selective attention is necessary for the successful establishment of declarative memory,<sup>212</sup> is governed by distinct networks mediating alertness, target orientation, and executive control,<sup>213</sup> and involves modulation of neuronal phase synchrony across short- and long- range connections.<sup>214</sup> Most anesthetic drugs impair

attention via decreased arousal, with the notable exception being the NMDA antagonist ketamine, which has dominant effects on orienting and selection.<sup>215</sup> Mathematical modeling demonstrates that the decrease in arousal predicts the effect of the drug on encoding.<sup>201</sup> The level of arousal almost fully predicts the memory effects of thiopental and dexmedetomidine, while propofol causes a significantly greater loss of memory than would be predicted by modulation of arousal alone, consistent with an additional effect on consolidation.<sup>202</sup> In neuroimaging correlates, low doses of thiopental mimic the effect of an attentional challenge task in decreasing activation in the left inferior prefrontal cortex (LIPFC), an effect not seen with propofol.<sup>216</sup>

Two recent studies have evaluated the effect of propofol on attention networks defined by an individual’s functional connectome – their unique pattern of static functional brain connectivity. At deep levels of sedation, propofol modulates the strength of individual sustained attention networks more than that of resting state networks<sup>217,218</sup> ; sustained attention networks also are sensitive to modulation by subanesthetic levels of sevoflurane.<sup>217</sup> No memory paradigms were performed in these experiments.

### NEUROIMAGING STUDIES OF CORTICAL MEMORY PROCESSES

A limited number of functional neuroimaging studies have evaluated the effect of anesthetics on cortical activation patterns during memory encoding. An early PET study investigating sedative doses of propofol using a word memory task identified that activation in LIPFC—a region associated with encoding and subsequent memory for language tasks—was conserved, suggesting that propofol did not block the processes required to support successful encoding.<sup>219</sup> These findings were replicated in a subsequent investigation using an auditory depth of processing task.<sup>216</sup> In contrast, propofol decreases activation in DLPFC – a region most associated with executive control functions and cognitive control of motor planning.<sup>219</sup> However, other studies have identified decreased activation in encoding areas. An fMRI study of sentence comprehension during light sedation with propofol found that decreased memory was related to decreased activation in the left inferior frontal gyrus (LIFG, within the LIPFC) and the middle temporal gyrus.<sup>220</sup> Another study of propofol using a word encoding task found decreased activation in LIFG associated with memory loss, but also found intact connectivity between LIFG and a number of frontoparietal and temporal regions associated with verbal processing and memory tasks, including the middle temporal gyrus and precuneus.<sup>221</sup>

Some functional neuroimaging studies have used experimental tasks similar to memory encoding tasks while evaluating sedative doses of anesthetic drugs but have not assessed memory encoding as a primary aim; these are informative but must be interpreted with caution. An early PET study of sedative doses of midazolam using a tone detection paradigm reported a dose- dependent activation decrease in Brodmann areas 9, 10, and 46, which overlap regions of both DLPFC and LIPFC.<sup>222</sup> An fMRI study of semantic word processing during propofol sedation found a dose- dependent decrease in activation of the LIFG, despite intact behavioral responses.<sup>223</sup> An fMRI study

of dexmedetomidine reported a generalized suppression of bilateral prefrontal activation in Brodmann areas 9 and 10 during an emotional picture memory task, but encoding performance was not specifically analyzed.<sup>224</sup> A study using music stimuli found decreased activation in primary and secondary auditory processing areas at sedative doses of dexmedetomidine and midazolam, but not with propofol.<sup>225</sup> Low doses of ketamine disrupt some theta- linked working memory strategies, but leave the frontal–midline theta/default mode network intact.<sup>226</sup>

### NEUROIMAGING STUDIES OF MEDIAL TEMPORAL LOBE FUNCTION

The degree of amnesia caused by the effects of propofol on consolidation processes can be linearly related to decreased activation in the hippocampus bilaterally at the time of initial encoding.<sup>227</sup> In contrast, despite a clear memory effect, dexmedetomidine does not reduce the overall level of hippocampal activity, and dynamics of hippocampal activation are less predictive of subsequent memory<sup>224</sup> ; this likely reflects a downstream effect from weakened cortical encoding processes rather than a primary consolidation effect. An earlier study of the benzodiazepine lorazepam and the cholinergic antagonist scopolamine found decreased memory to be correlated with decreased activation in the anterior hippocampus, accompanied by decreased activation in the fusiform gyrus and inferior frontal cortex.<sup>228</sup> In an exploratory investigation, 0.25% sevoflurane was found to reduce hippocampal activation in response to auditory and visual stimuli, but no memory performance was assessed.<sup>187</sup>

A few studies have evaluated anesthetic effects on the hippocampus using electrocorticography and depth electrodes implanted in epileptic patients, but none have been dedicated to memory function. One notable study evaluating the effects of mildly sedative concentrations of propofol reported a significant increase in hippocampal–rhinal spontaneous coherence in the delta band, but minimal changes in other bands; corticohippocampal coherence was not assessed.<sup>229</sup>

### STUDIES OF CORTICAL EVENT- RELATED POTENTIALS

Several anesthesia memory studies have used the eventrelated potential (ERP) – a small but stereotypic positive and negative signal fluctuation in the EEG time- locked to a stimulus that can be isolated through summation over multiple identical trials. Competing models explain the generation of ERPs through either the induction of new oscillations<sup>230</sup> or through stimulus- induced phase resetting of ongoing oscillations.<sup>231</sup>

An early ERP study of scopolamine, lorazepam, and diphenhydramine suggested that drug- induced effects on arousal and memory may be electrophysiologically dissociable, although memory was not assessed directly.<sup>232</sup> Changes in the P1N1 and N1P2 early complexes, associated with arousal, were seen for all three drugs. However, changes in later complexes associated with memory, notably the P3 and N2P3, were seen only with scopolamine and lorazepam, and not with diphenhydramine, an antihistamine that causes sedation but not amnesia.

This work was extended by a series of studies evaluating intravenous anesthetics and using memory- specific experimental paradigms. In verbal memory tasks performed at equivalent levels of sedation, the true amnestic drugs with memory effects independent of sedation—propofol and midazolam—caused decreased amplitude in the P3 and N2P3 components,<sup>233,234</sup> with the latter being the best predictor of subsequent memory performance. Across all drugs studied, the N2 latency was related to reaction time, a surrogate measure of sedation.

Another study examined early- and mid- latency ERPs originating from the midline parietal precuneal region and related them to the extent of encoding and consolidation failure.<sup>201</sup> Across multiple drug classes and doses, consolidation failure and reaction time were closely correlated with the P2 amplitude and N2 latency observed at the time of encoding. As the visual P2N2 complex is known to originate from synchronous theta oscillations,<sup>235</sup> and reaction time can be related to interregional synchrony,<sup>236</sup> it is plausible that changes in theta oscillations across a distributed corticohippocampal network are significant in mediating anesthetic effects on consolidation. No direct measures of cortico hippocampal theta synchrony have yet been performed in studies of pharmacologic amnesia in humans. Another analysis<sup>237</sup> examined the old–new effect, a robust phenomenon in the parietal ERP that distinguishes the response to initial item exposure from subsequent exposures as a marker of memory strength. Propofol and midazolam caused a significant decrease in the old–new effect at 27 seconds, even though memory performance was maintained, demonstrating that neurophysiologic markers of impaired memory processes can precede behavioral detection. Although not a direct evaluation of memory, other studies using auditory ERPs to music signatures found that sedative doses of propofol cause a decrease in mismatch negativity and early right anterior negativity, which are known to relate to associative memory operations but in contrast do not affect the P1 complex, which derives from primary auditory processing.

## Nonhuman Studies of Anesthetic Effects on Memory Processes and Behavior in the Medial Temporal Lobe

### ANESTHETIC EFFECTS ON MEDIAL TEMPORAL LOBE PLASTICITY

GABA- ergic interneurons project within and across subregions of the hippocampus,<sup>238</sup> providing an abundant density of plausible targets for anesthetic drugs. In an early study using tetanic stimulation of the Schaffer collateralcommissural pathway, isoflurane blocked the induction of LTP and long- term depression (LTD) by low- frequency stimulation.<sup>239</sup> These effects were reversed by the addition of the GABAA receptor antagonist picrotoxin, providing strong evidence that the effect of isoflurane on LTP is GABA- ergically mediated. In a similar protocol, amnestic concentrations of sevoflurane caused a failure of LTP, which was prevented by the addition of the GABAA

antagonist bicuculline.<sup>240</sup> Other studies have replicated a dose- dependent suppression of LTP by both isoflurane and sevoflurane.<sup>241</sup> The effects of volatile anesthetics may not be exclusively GABA- ergically mediated: one study using the agonist epibatidine and antagonist dihydro- β- erythroidine implicated the α4β2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor in isoflurane suppression of LTP.<sup>242</sup>

A series of investigations have demonstrated that propofol inhibits the induction, but not the maintenance, of LTP, and has no effect on LTD. The effect is blocked by the addition of picrotoxin, implicating a GABAA receptormediated mechanism.<sup>243,244</sup> Notably, one study found that propofol inhibited LTP only at anesthetic, and not at amnestic concentrations.<sup>244</sup> Propofol has also been demonstrated to inhibit a number of component subprocesses of LTP.<sup>245–247</sup> Propofol reduces expression of activity- related cytoskeleton- associated protein (Arc) in the hippocampus in response to inhibitory avoidance training but does not appear to reduce Arc mRNA,<sup>208</sup> which is notable because it suggests a posttranscriptional mechanism.

The hippocampal GABAA- ergic interneuron population is notable for a high density of the α5- subunit subtype, which is virtually absent elsewhere in the cortex. The α5GABAA receptor mediates tonic inhibition in hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons,<sup>248</sup> and regulates the induction of LTP in response to a narrow range of frequencies in the theta range.<sup>249</sup> These observations have informed a series of anesthesia studies using α5- GABAA- knockout mice and inverse agonists. Etomidate blocks LTP measured in CA1 neurons in wild- type mice, but not in α5 −/− mutants, and behaviorally the α5 −/− mutants are resistant to the amnestic effects of etomidate, but not to its general anesthetic effects.<sup>250</sup> Furthermore, the effects of etomidate on LTP and memory behaviors are reversed by the addition of the α5- GABAA selective inverse agonist L- 655,708.<sup>251</sup> Similarly, tonic inhibitory conductance in hippocampal pyramidal neurons is enhanced by low (amnestic) concentrations of isoflurane and sevoflurane, but is not observed in α5 −/− mutants,<sup>252</sup> and is reversed by the addition of L- 655,708 and MRK- 016, another inverse agonist.<sup>253</sup> In rodents, impaired memory performance that extends for 24–48 hours after exposure to isoflurane also appears to be mediated by the α5- GABAA receptor.<sup>254,255</sup>

GABAA- ergic receptor subtypes other than α5 may be associated with anesthetic amnesia. α4- GABAA receptors are concentrated in the dentate gyrus and dorsal thalamus, and α4 knockouts are resistant to the amnestic effects of isoflurane but only minimally to its general anesthetic effects.<sup>256</sup> Forebrain- selective β3- GABAA receptor knockout mice are resistant to the amnestic effects of isoflurane,<sup>257</sup> but in contrast the amnestic effects of etomidate appear to not involve β3- subunit receptor subtypes.<sup>258</sup> The α1- and α2GABAA subunit subtypes also do not appear to be involved in the amnestic effects of volatile anesthetics.<sup>259,260</sup> The hyperpolarization- activated, cyclic nucleotide- gated subtype 1 (HCN1) channel may also be involved in mediating anesthetic amnesia: HCN1<sup>−/−</sup> knockout mice are resistant to the amnestic effects of sevoflurane.<sup>261</sup>

## Human and Nonhuman Studies of Anesthetic Effects on Fear Memory Systems

### THE AMYGDALA- DEPENDENT FEAR SYSTEM

The amygdala is a cluster of interconnected nuclei sitting immediately anterior to the hippocampus, with afferent and efferent projections to a wide distribution of cortical and subcortical structures. It is critical to fear learning and memory, and the systematic study of amygdala- dependent classical (Pavlovian) fear conditioning has revealed much information regarding the underlying circuitry and mechanisms.<sup>155,156</sup> The basolateral nucleus of the amygdala (BLA) modulates the encoding and consolidation of memory in the hippocampus and elsewhere in response to emotion, arousal, and stress.<sup>267</sup> The mechanism is dependent on noradrenergic projections terminating on both α and β receptors within the BLA,<sup>268</sup> and can be triggered by systemic stress mediators, notably glucocorticoids<sup>269</sup> and epinephrine.<sup>270</sup> Amygdalo hippocampal connectivity occurs via direct and indirect projections, and is dependent on synchronous oscillations in the theta and gamma bands.<sup>188,271</sup> The amygdala contains a number of rational targets for GABA- ergic anesthetic drugs.

### STUDIES OF ANESTHETIC EFFECTS ON FEAR SYSTEMS IN RODENT MODELS

As various forms of fear conditioning represent the dominant experimental method for studying memory in animal models, almost all studies of anesthetic effects on memory offer some insight into their effects on fear systems. There is substantial evidence that anesthetic effects on fear memory are mediated by GABA- ergic mechanisms in the amygdala. Selective injection of the BLA with midazolam blocks acquisition and stress enhancement of fear memory,<sup>272,273</sup> while lesions of the BLA block anterograde amnesia for an inhibitory avoidance task in rats receiving diazepam,<sup>274</sup> propofol,<sup>275</sup> and sevoflurane.<sup>276</sup> Further, injection of the BLA with the selective GABAA antagonist bicuculline blocks the effect of propofol<sup>208</sup> and midazolam.<sup>277</sup> Midazolam administered at the time of reactivation of a fear memory can attenuate reconsolidation and lead to a weakening of the fear memory<sup>278</sup> ; this is of considerable translational interest as a potential foundation for treating fear- based psychopathologies.

There are certain conditions under which anesthetic drugs can enhance fear memory. When propofol or ketamine is given immediately following conditioning, retention is enhanced,<sup>279,280</sup> which may represent a form of retrograde facilitation as described earlier, but retention is reduced when dexmedetomidine is similarly administered. In a rat model of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), propofol and ketamine administered immediately after fear learning enhances long- term fear behavior, while dexmedetomidine has a neutral effect.<sup>280</sup> Midazolam given during extinction training, which normally inhibits learned fear expression, blocks the effect of extinction,<sup>272</sup> and also blocks fear- reducing effects when given during preconditioning contextual learning.<sup>281</sup> When sevoflurane is administered at a very low, nonsedating dose (0.11%), fear conditioning is enhanced, likely via the extracellular signal- regulated protein kinase signaling pathway.<sup>282,283</sup>

### STUDIES OF ANESTHETIC EFFECTS ON FEAR SYSTEMS IN HUMANS AND NONHUMAN PRIMATES

A limited number of studies in humans have directly addressed anesthetic effects on emotional memory. An early behavioral study that established dose–response curves for amnesia demonstrated that emotionally negative material was more resistant to the memory effects of thiopental than was neutral material.<sup>284</sup> As the superior memorability of emotional material is known to be mediated by the amygdalo hippocampal axis, neuroimaging studies have focused on anesthetic effects in these regions. A PET study of sevoflurane (Fig. 8.5, lower panel) demonstrated that the superior memorability of emotionally negative material was preserved at concentrations of 0.1% and 0.2%, but was lost at 0.25%<sup>285</sup> ; this loss was associated with decreased effective connectivity projecting from the right amygdala to hippocampus, and from the right nucleus basalis to hippocampus. An fMRI study of propofol demonstrated that a sedative dose does not suppress activation of the amygdala in response to negatively arousing items, but does markedly attenuate activation of the hippocampus (Fig. 8.5, upper panel), which correlates strongly with amnesia and loss of the superior memorability of emotional material.<sup>227</sup> This implies that cortical and subcortical processes serving emotional interpretative functions and afferent amygdala activation are relatively spared by amnestic levels of propofol, while efferent processes underlying amygdala- dependent modulation of hippocampal plasticity are interrupted. An fMRI study of dexmedetomidine also reported no effect on activation of the amygdala in response to negatively arousing items, but in contrast to propofol, emotional items retained superior memorability, and activation in the left amygdala and hippocampus were correlated with subsequent memory.<sup>224</sup> In contrast, ketamine suppresses activation of the amygdala in response to negative emotional pictures,<sup>286</sup> potentially via alterations in emotional perception. ### RELEVANCE TO PERIOPERATIVE PSYCHOLOGICAL MORBIDITY

Amygdala reactivity is of mechanistic importance to a number of archetypal fear- based psychopathologies, including PTSD, anxiety, phobia, and panic disorder.<sup>290</sup> The neurohumoral stress response to surgery and critical illness— emotionally stressful events at baseline—causes complex changes in levels of catecholamines and glucocorticoids, which are known promoters of plasticity in amygdaladependent fear processes. It is unclear under what conditions systemically elevated levels of these mediators might contribute to long- term neuropsychological changes, although multiple studies in the critical care setting relate

<!-- PDF page 16 -->

stress exposure and exogenous administration of catecholamines to negative long- term outcomes.<sup>291</sup> In a broad surgical population, the incidence of PTSD- complex symptoms related to surgery was reported to be 16%<sup>5</sup> – similar to that observed in victims of violence. There is a theoretical potential for anesthetic drugs to impact long- term psychological sequelae, both positively and negatively. Recent pilot data pose questions for further investigation: a randomized study found that trauma patients anesthetized with propofol were significantly more likely to develop PTSD than those anesthetized with sevoflurane.<sup>292</sup> However, there is presently insufficient human data to inform specific recommendations.

Implicit (nondeclarative) memory processes do not axiomatically require a conscious substrate and are not dependent on hippocampal plasticity. The effect of anesthetic drugs on implicit memory functions may therefore be distinct from those on declarative memory, and accordingly several investigations have sought evidence for implicit processing in unconscious subjects. This literature is notable for heterogeneity and challenges in experimental methodology, which must be highly rigorous in order to detect implicit memory traces and dissociate them from explicit memory. Many studies have applied the word stem completion task, but the gold standard is the process dissociation procedure, in which subjects are asked to exclude items that have been presented.

Reflecting this heterogeneity and the difficulty in interpreting this literature, two recent meta- analyses reported evidence of implicit memory in ∼36% of all patient cohorts studied<sup>293</sup> and in 41% of a subset of studies using auditory stimuli and complete blinding.<sup>294</sup> With the caveat of caution in interpretation, implicit memory is more likely to be reported in patients with American Society of Anesthesiologists’ physical status 3–4 and in general anesthesia cases as compared with deep sedation cases. Another interesting conclusion from the meta- analyses is that intraoperative music may exert beneficial effects on pain and opioid requirement, but positive suggestion does not. This is not clearly a memory- related phenomenon but implies some element of perceptual processing.
