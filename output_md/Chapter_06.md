## Introduction

The practice of anesthesia requires extensive knowledge of human anatomy, physiology, and pharmacology. In addition to these essential underpinnings of anesthesia practice are the human factors and performance requirements needed to optimize clinical decision-making and care and ensure patient safety. This chapter reviews some of the human factors that have impact on performance and how to manage them effectively to optimize care and practice. It also provides an

overview of the role for simulation as a way to address some of the limitations of human factors that impact care to both assess and improve individual- and team- based performance.

### SCOPE OF THE PROBLEM

Human factors contribute to patient harm. As an example, a 2021 Canadian review of anesthesiology airway- related medicolegal cases identified provider factors, team factors, and system factors that contributed to patient harm.<sup>1</sup> The peer reviewers identified faulty clinical decision- making in 54% of the cases, procedural violations in 22% of cases, and poor situational awareness in 20% of cases. While the methodology of this study is not ideal for identifying team factors and system factors, they were far outweighed by the provider factors contributing to the errors (Fig. 6.1).

The Anesthesia Closed Claims Project has been instrumental in assessing some of the underlying causes for patient harm. A recent analysis of airway- related claims identified errors in judgment in 73% of claims.<sup>2</sup> These judgment errors included failure to plan appropriately and prospectively for management of a patient with a known difficult airway, failure to utilize a supraglottic airway as a bridge to potentially difficult tracheal intubation, and perseverance with one approach to the airway without identifying alternative and potentially more appropriate options. Another closed claims analysis examined the frequency and type of situational awareness errors in malpractice claims in which patients suffered brain damage or death, and found that situational awareness may have contributed in 74% of these tragic outcomes.<sup>3</sup>

### FACTORS IMPACTING HUMAN PERFORMANCE

### Fatigue

Sleep deprivation is an acknowledged problem for clinicians, including anesthesiologists. While work hour limits are now in place for trainees and, in most practices,

limitations in shift hours have been implemented, fatigue remains a significant issue for anesthesiology because of the high demands of the clinical environment, the call coverage obligations, and the challenges associated with the increasing patient acuity. In addition, clinicians providing care during nighttime hours are challenging their biorhythms by demanding alertness at nonphysiologic times. Resident physicians are also subject to prolonged and chronic sleep deprivation as they balance work and life and fulfill their educational and clinical obligations. To address fatigue in trainees, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) now places restrictions on duty hours, including time spent moonlighting over and above educational program hours. Despite these limitations on work hours, perceived quality of care has not improved.<sup>4</sup> Fatigue among all anesthesia providers is thus a challenging patient safety factor, particularly at a time when there are workforce shortages affecting the specialty. Simulation and experiential learning have been helpful methods to assess and improve practice habits to address the challenges of fatigue on clinical care.

### Distraction

The OR environment is filled with distractions, including but not limited to noise and alarms associated with each monitor, the number of individuals moving into and out of the immediate environment, requests by surgeons to listen to monitoring notifications or to have background music playing during the procedure, and the expectation that the anesthesiologist is providing ongoing oversight of the entire surgical environment while remaining attentive to the needs of the patient, surgeon, and other OR staff.<sup>5–9</sup> Adding to sources of distraction are phone calls regarding other patient needs, including those from the postanesthe sia care unit (PACU), calls related to the next scheduled case, or patients scheduled for surgery at a future date. Other activities that can take attention away from the patient include the need to urgently prepare and administer a medication that is not readily available; calls to the pharmacy or blood bank for drugs or blood products needed during an emergency; evaluating the reason a monitor or other equipment is not functioning properly despite proper preoperative check; and the complexity of documentation on the electronic medical record. While in the past, some anesthesiologists would read the _Wall Street Journal_ during a case, more recent distractions relate to the use of social media and searches on the internet to obtain both clinically and non- clinically related information.<sup>10</sup> Simulation and experiential learning can be helpful in providing clinicians with the tools needed to effectively maintain continual vigilant monitoring of the patient despite the potential deleterious distractions.

### Production Pressure

The time pressures in the operating room, PACU and intensive care units, and other environments also represent a factor inherent to anesthesia practice that can contribute to complications and harm. The emphasis on turnover time in the operating room and other pressures create the potential for error.<sup>11–13</sup> Similarly, trying to work efficiently to meet the needs of the surgeon puts pressure on the anesthesiologist to complete pre- induction procedural tasks quickly to prevent delays and optimize OR utilization.<sup>13</sup> Maximizing

efficiency in the operating room is most realistically accomplished when the team of surgeon, anesthesiologist, circulating nurse, and scrub technician work together regularly on similar, commonly performed procedures (for example consistent subspecialty services such as cardiac, neurosurgery, and transplant) and when patients have low disease burden. Collaboration is not as straightforward for different groups of anesthesia providers who do not regularly work with specific surgical teams or individual surgeons, is more challenging, and can increase production pressure among other concerns. At the same time, while collaborating and cooperating with the multidisciplinary team is a laudable goal, the individual pressures imposed on each member of any team can be difficult to manage even under optimal collaborative environments. Patient safety clearly can and should take precedence over these other obligations, but often the production pressure can contribute to variations in the standard of care and result in unintended complications.

### IMPACT OF ADVERSE EVENTS ON PROVIDERS

Adverse events or errors in clinical decision- making that result in patient harm or death not only have impact on patients and their families, but also on the medical personnel who participate in the care. The impact on the providers is referred to as the “second victim” phenomenon,<sup>14–16</sup> to acknowledge that the providers are also impacted by the adverse events. At the same time, over the past few years, concerns about the use of the term “second victim” have been raised because of its implications for patients and their families who have been directly impacted by the adverse events and might undermine efforts to systematically review the events and identify ways to improve clinical care.<sup>17,18</sup>

While identifying a better terminology for the impact of adverse outcomes on providers is an important next step, identifying the correct way to acknowledge it has been challenging. Providers involved in the care of the patient at the time of an adverse event often “second guess” their decision- making capability, judgment, or even their competence. It is also essential to acknowledge that each event often has impact both on those directly involved in decisionmaking and management, as well as others involved in the overall clinical care of the patient, but less directly involved in the care at the time of the event. While acknowledging the impact of these events on providers is critically important, those efforts cannot substitute for identifying why and how adverse events occurred, and the systems solutions required to reduce the likelihood of recurrence. As a result, each “second victim” often experiences remorse about the outcome and their involvement in it but should also be encouraged to question what went wrong, and why and how the error or outcome could have been avoided.

The likelihood of becoming a second victim during the course of one’s career is high.<sup>19</sup> Although there is limited data to define the likelihood of becoming a second victim and the specific specialties for whom it is more likely, the frequency probably varies depending on the roles and responsibilities as well as the level of participation in the care resulting in the poor outcome. In addition, while the second victim phenomenon can occur in any clinical relationship, it is often most notable when the providers, whether

a physician, nurse or others, have developed a relationship with the patient and/or family members.

The impact of the second victim phenomenon can play a role in the providers’ well- being. It can also affect their self- confidence and, in some cases, their ability to assume responsibilities for similar clinical encounters in the future. The duration of the impact is also not well defined. As a result, identifying the phenomenon is critically important to provide peer and institutional support measures. At the same time, it is important to understand what and when such support is provided or required, since the response of each second victim can be different and require different measures to address it. Some second victims do not think they need support or training to address the phenomenon and might not accept it, at least initially after the event.<sup>20</sup> However, making support available through both peer support, case review to identify whether or how the outcome might have been avoided, or the likelihood of similar outcomes in the future avoided can be very helpful for all involved providers. Some second victims will benefit from support from others, including social workers, psychologists, psychiatrists, or clergy, while others will not. Providing such resources to meet individual need can be valuable and demonstrates to the organization the acknowledgement of the second victim phenomenon and its implications to the entire staff.

### PERSONAL FACTORS IMPACTING PERFORMANCE (COGNITIVE BIAS)

As Pat Croskerry wrote, “Virtually every cognitive error is deemed preventable in hindsight.”<sup>21</sup> A number of other factors specific to each individual impact performance. Perhaps the most significant of these factors is the process by which each person makes clinical decisions and the timing involved in doing so. These internal factors represent cognitive bias and create risk for cognitive errors in decisionmaking. “Cognitive bias” refers to a tendency to think or behave in a certain way, and “cognitive error” is when bias is misleading.<sup>22–24</sup> Some call cognitive bias “cognitive disposition to respond” or “recognition- primed decisionmaking.”<sup>25,26</sup> Systemic cognitive errors are predictable deviations from reality. Cognitive bias can be applied to individuals as well as to situations, and so is relevant to both interpersonal encounters as well as purely clinical decisionmaking. These concepts are critically important since they impact the decision- making process for anesthesiologists, though they have relevance beyond anesthesia and medicine. For a more in- depth discussion, the interested reader is referred to the reference list as well as Daniel Kahneman’s fascinating book, _Thinking Fast and Slow_ .<sup>27</sup>

Another important concept that relates to human decision- making is dual process theory, a framework that has been proposed to explain the different ways in which we process information and draw conclusions.<sup>28–31</sup> Dual process theory describes two types of decision- making. Type 1 processes are thought processes that are rapid, intuitive, automatic, and require little effort. These processes are responsive, capable of parallel processing, and highly context- bound; in other words, strongly influenced by ambient conditions such as the workload of the practitioner, priorities, interruptions, and the practitioner’s affective state.

<!-- PDF page 4 -->

It is a passive, reflexive way of thinking, and tends to rely on heuristics (rules of thumb, or mental shortcuts). The majority of type 1 processes are unconscious (cognitive psychologists estimate that we spend 95% of our time in the intuitive system 1), and so may be more prone to error. However, it’s often both an effective and efficient way of addressing any issue, allowing us to make rapid decisions and reserving effort for the more challenging decisions. Type 1 processes are referred to as the “story” system, represented by the part of the brain that fills in gaps in data, jumps to conclusions, and arranges the information to make sense and create a logical story. In many clinical situations this process of “jumping to conclusions” without understanding the consequences can be deleterious for both patient and provider.

Type 2 processes are more analytical and involve critical thinking. They rely on logic, so are less prone to error. However, the decision- making process is based in large part on experience, so can be a less effective way of making important clinical decisions for novice learners. Type 2 decisionmaking processes can be difficult and require focus and effort. As a result, they can be diminished by inattentiveness, fatigue, and distraction. What is perhaps most interesting and relevant to the clinical setting for anesthesiologists is that as their experience improves, type 2 processes can be transformed into type 1, more intuitive decision- making with repeated exposure to similar situations. As an example, consider the effort required for a medical student faced with a respiratory acidosis to interpret the data, consider a differential diagnosis, recall the components of minute ventilation, decide between increasing respiratory rate or tidal volume, calculate an appropriate weight- or height- based tidal volume, determine which settings on the ventilator to adjust based on the ventilator mode, and finally make a change. In contrast, an experienced anesthesiologist will make the correct adjustments in far less time and with less effort.

Most experienced clinicians spend the majority of time utilizing type 1 decision- making processes; however, depending on the clinical situation being addressed, both forms of thinking are essential. We switch back and forth between the two. For experienced providers, type 1 decision- making might be appropriate based on their background, skills, and expertise. At the same time, in most situations, often for the less experienced provider, there is a tendency to identify the best response quickly, generally using type 1 process (cognitive indolence) rather than expending the energy and time required to analytically assess data and identify the optimal solutions.<sup>31</sup>

## Heuristics, or Rules of Thumb

Heuristics represent shortcuts to decision- making, designed to minimize the cognitive load; they represent a feature of type 1 processing.<sup>32,33</sup> Heuristics are a practical approach to problem- solving that are sufficient for reaching a shortterm goal or approximation, but are not guaranteed to be optimal, rational, or nuanced. Examples of heuristics in anesthesia practice include the following:

- “If a patient comes to the OR intubated, the patient leaves the OR intubated.”

- “Any after- hours case gets an endotracheal tube.”

- “Only cases less than two hours should be eligible for a supraglottic airway.”

- “Always check a preoperative potassium level in patients on hemodialysis.”

While these “rules” are not incorrect, they do not take the specific clinical situation or other relevant information that might support a different decision into account. Using heuristics precludes the need to consider the risk–benefit profile for a particular patient and allows us to preserve our cognitive resources for more difficult decisions but is not always in the patient’s best interest. Some common heuristics provide examples of how this approach impacts clinical decision- making:

**Anchoring** refers to the tendency to overvalue early information, or the credence we give to our “first impression.” As an example, if you are asked to estimate the product of the following line of numbers: 8 × 7 × 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1. You are more likely to respond that this series of numbers has higher estimate than if you were asked to estimate the product of this line of numbers: 1 × 2 × 3 × 4 × 5 × 6 × 7 × 8. Obviously, the products are identical; yet overvaluing the information received first can result in a higher estimate.

**Availability** heuristic is the tendency to answer a question about frequency of events based on the ease with which the conclusion is reached, rather than completing a more thorough assessment. For example, media coverage of childhood deaths due to a school shooting may create the impression that school is more dangerous to children than driving in the car with their parents, yet in 2020 vehicular trauma caused 607 deaths of children under the age of 12 and injured 63,000 more; in contrast 2 children were killed in a school shooting and 9 people were injured.<sup>34,35</sup>

**Affective** heuristic refers to the interplay between our affective state and our decision- making. Strong emotion can quickly and powerfully influence our decision- making; for example, clinical decision- making may be compromised for the patient who is a VIP, whose presentation reminds the clinician of a family member, or from whom the clinician may unconsciously or consciously wish to distance, as when caring for a patient experiencing homelessness or incarceration.

A variety of heuristics relevant to the practice of anesthesiology have been described. ### SYSTEM STRATEGIES

Education and clinical experience are essential methods to optimize clinical decision- making and overcome the inherent biases most clinicians may not even recognize. Training designed to better understand the sources of bias can be very helpful, including teaching about the cognitive theories underlying decision- making and the impact of cognitive biases. However, other methods are also essential, including, but not limited to, team- based learning, implementation of cognitive aids, and teaching inferential rules to combine an analytical approach with a non- analytical approach.

A variety of approaches are used routinely to improve decision- making and minimize the negative impact of cognitive bias. As one example, clinicians teach and practice structured data acquisition (for example, reviewing a chest X- ray or obtaining and interpreting an echocardiographic or other ultrasound examination), approaching each study in the same systematic way and reporting on all structures and findings whether positive or negative, regardless of the study indication or clinical situation. This approach minimizes the likelihood that a significant finding, often unrelated to the reason the study was performed, is missed (e.g., identifying a new lung mass on a study performed to look for a retained foreign body).

Over the past few years, a variety of “slow down” strategies have been used to improve decision- making in these complex situations, including time- outs before a procedure, and debriefing at its completion. The SBAR (Situation– Background–Assessment–Recommendation) provides an opportunity to assess and establish a shared mental model of what was done and its impact for members of the team.<sup>37</sup> Perhaps the most important effective way to optimize the decision- making process, particularly in the perioperative setting, is effective group interactions that ensure group decision strategy, accountability, feedback, and psychological safety. Debriefing, after- action reflection, and metacognition can all create awareness around compromised decision- making. In addition, as was noted previously, implementation of decision support systems can be effective ways of optimizing care based on the collaborative SBAR process. For example, development of cognitive aids or alerts or reminders embedded in the electronic medical record (implemented in a way that does not distract the provider) may provide just- in- time clinical information.<sup>38</sup>

### INDIVIDUAL STRATEGIES

Individual strategies to consciously engage type 2 decisionmaking require that the clinician recognize the potential

bias and overcome it. A variety of strategies can be learned and employed individually and incorporated into teambased approaches to decision- making. Perhaps the most common approach is experiential learning, as takes place regularly in the clinical environment and is embedded in our educational programs. Similarly, the use of simulation models designed to address specific clinical scenarios have been very effective at improving clinical performance and decision- making (see discussion of simulation models, below). Several creative strategies have been adopted by clinicians to improve cognitive approaches to clinical decision- making related to anesthesia practice.<sup>39</sup>

One of the challenges in developing individual strategies to optimize decision- making is that in order to do so, the underlying cause for an unanticipated event occurring during anesthesia and surgery can be difficult to identify and therefore it can be difficult to determine the most appropriate therapeutic option. For example, the patient who becomes hypotensive during a complicated thoracic surgical procedure associated with significant bleeding might initially be presumed to be hypovolemic, requiring blood transfusion; alternative diagnoses also have to be considered, such as pneumothorax, air embolus, cardiac tamponade, or myocardial infarction. Identifying the correct diagnosis can be difficult, but rapid treatment is required. In this situation, the clinician is likely to treat the “most likely” cause for the problem, but must be prepared to rapidly change course, if the expected response is not achieved.<sup>40</sup>

While considering treatment options, the risk of imposing bias cannot be dismissed, since doing so will delay making the correct clinical approach to address the problem. Identifying the bias can be challenging. Graber _et al._ propose questions that may suggest an increased propensity to bias<sup>41</sup> :

1.  Was the diagnosis suggested to me by the patient, nurse, or colleague?

2.  Did I accept this patient as a “hand- off?”

3.  Did I consider organ systems besides the obvious one?

4.  Is this a patient I do not like, or like too much, for any reason?

5.  Have I been interrupted or distracted while caring for this patient?

6.  Am I cognitively overloaded right now?

7.  Am I stereotyping the patient or the presentation?

After acknowledging some of the sources of bias, determining how best to respond to a clinical dilemma can be challenging when many alternative responses might be appropriate. Some other approaches to consider when confronted with this diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma include the following:

### “Prospective Hindsight”

Imagine a future in which the decision was wrong, and ask, “What did I miss?”<sup>27</sup>

### “Rule Out Worst Case”

Based on the signs and symptoms, what is the worst possible outcome that could occur when considering a cause for a clinical problem and initiating a treatment to address it? Rule out that outcome first, before moving on to the next most likely cause.

<!-- PDF page 7 -->

### “Don’t Just Do Something, Stand There”

Sometimes the best initial response is to do nothing. Anesthesiologists and many other physicians are accustomed to taking rapid and decisive action. However, in some cases, the patient may be better served by stepping back and reassessing the clinical information before acting. More experienced providers are often more comfortable delaying reaction when clinically feasible to allow time to reassess before initiating a treatment, particularly when the therapy has its own inherent risks. Simulated clinical scenarios provide opportunities to practice this challenging skill.

### “My Working Diagnosis Is...”

Including the word “working” emphasizes uncertainty and encourages us to maintain a high index of suspicion. This could be a powerful addition to our medical communication and decision- making by continuously acknowledging that we may have missed or misinterpreted pertinent information.

## High-Reliability Organizations

The concept of a high-reliability organization (HRO) was developed in 1987 based on an assessment of how the airline industry and others manage risk and potential for errors.<sup>42–45</sup> The description of the high-reliability organization acknowledged that these organizations operate under challenging and hazardous conditions but have put systems in place to address and potentially minimize them. HROs function in challenging and the organizations represent potentially hazardous environments. They do not prevent all, but rather have the ability and foresight to identify and prioritize how to optimize performance throughout the organization despite the challenges they face.

The key features of a high-reliability organization based on Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHQR) criteria include the following<sup>46</sup> :

- n  Acknowledgment of the high- risk nature of an organization’s activities and the determination to achieve consistently safe operations

- n  A blame- free environment where individuals are able to report errors or near misses without fear of reprimand or punishment

- n  Encouragement of collaboration across the ranks and disciplines to seek solutions to patient safety problems

- n  Organizational commitment of resources to address safety concerns

In addition to these key organizing principles, HROs are also characterized by four key structural pillars: (1) creation and sustainment of a culture of safety; (2) global and profound organizational learning, including incident reporting systems (IRS); (3) continuous training of individuals, teams, and work units including the regular use of simulation; and (4) continuous optimization of safetyrelevant structures and processes. The four pillars are intended to provide anchor points for systematic organizational improvement.

Successful HROs in any industry, including health care, pursue two important, but competing approaches to optimize their performance: prevention and resilience.<sup>42</sup>

### PREVENTION

Prevention (anticipation) requires that an organization anticipate events that should not occur, define possible causes, and create policies and procedures for avoiding them.<sup>47–49</sup> To be most effective, the organization should implement highly standardized procedures to define how to identify and minimize potential or actual errors or unintended consequences.<sup>50</sup> In health care organizations the implementation of clinical protocols, evidence- based clinical practice guidelines, and checklists represent good examples of how HROs anticipate and prevent adverse events from occurring.<sup>51</sup> In addition, the formal review of adverse events through both quality improvement programs within departments and service lines as well as root cause analyses are essential components of the commitment to a culture of safety, the learning health system, and the goal to prevent future similar outcomes.<sup>52</sup>

While this process is effective at reducing “never events,” it does not and cannot prevent all incidents from occurring. First, an organization cannot prevent events that it is not able to anticipate. Second, if policies and procedures become too complex, the likelihood that everyone in the organization will comply is low. In addition, blind adherence to strict and complex policies prevents capable providers from reacting appropriately to events that do not “fit the mold” or reacting to rapid changes in the clinical situation that do not match the definitions in the policy. To build and maintain a high-reliability organization requires not only prevention, but also resilience.

### RESILIENCE

HROs are unique in their abilities both to prevent and to manage mishaps before they can spread throughout the system, thus causing widespread damage or failure. At the same time, the hallmark of an HRO is not that it is errorfree, but that errors do not disable it. Resilience is therefore an essential characteristic of an HRO. It requires that an organization continuously assesses variations in care, has systems in place to monitor untoward events as they occur and responds to inevitable complications rapidly and consistently. Resilience involves three abilities: (1) the ability to absorb strain and preserve functioning in spite of the presence of adversity (e.g., rapid change, ineffective leadership, performance and production pressures, increasing demands from stakeholders); (2) an ability to recover or bounce back from untoward events as the team, unit, or system becomes better able to absorb a surprise and stretch rather than collapse; and (3) an ability to learn and grow from previous episodes of resilient action.<sup>53</sup>

HROs have a number of other critically important and unique attributes, including openness to dialog and respectful interaction among all providers, environment of trust, and a set of organizing principles, the most important of which is a preoccupation with failure as described in Table 6.2.

### EDUCATION THEORY AND ITS RELEVANCE TO SIMULATION

Although health care educators have not always embraced, or even been aware of the value of education theory,<sup>58,59</sup> this is rapidly changing. Education theory encompasses a range of theories that are useful for explaining the application, interpretation, and purpose of learning and education. Education theory can help the instructor to better understand the learning process and select appropriate techniques, assessments, and curricular plans.<sup>62</sup> There are many theoretical constructs that can inform simulation education practice, but no one theory covers all aspects of simulation education. Nevertheless, there are several key education theories that are essential for developing and delivering simulation- based education programs.

One of the most famous behaviorists was Pavlov, the scientist who found that dogs would begin to salivate spontaneously when they heard a bell after they had become conditioned to associate their food with the sound of a bell. Behaviorism in simulation is most closely tied to automaticity as individuals repeatedly practice skills until they can “automatically” perform a series of maneuvers without

<!-- PDF page 11 -->

having to think about every step. This is particularly applicable to skills training where learners can perform a task on models until they have “mastered” the basic steps and psychomotor maneuvers required. It can also be applied to other consistent behavior such as closed- loop communication, routine use of a time- out, or checking an identification before initiating a discussion or procedure.

Learning psychomotor skills for procedures may also be understood through the theory of embodied learning or embodied cognition.<sup>63</sup> Briefly, this is the concept acknowledging that humans think and make meaning through all of the senses, not just through concepts, reasoning, and judgment in the mind. Embodied cognition theory includes the role that the body plays in shaping cognition and understanding though interactions between the sensory input to the brain from the actions of the body.<sup>64</sup> Cognition is not simply the result of thinking but is the result of how brain interacts within the context of the body and its surroundings and experiences. The concept of embodied learning also has implications across other theoretical models.

### Constructivism

This learning theory acknowledges that people actively “construct” or make their own knowledge and reality is determined by the experience and perception of the individual. New knowledge is built on a foundation of previous knowledge, so each student’s particular experience influences their interpretation of new events and how new knowledge is incorporated into their existing framework. Since learners are constructing or building their own understanding, they are actively involved in their own learning, so discussion and experience are key elements for learning. This requires connection with other people and is therefore a social activity and requires the learner to make connections with prior knowledge and beliefs. Knowledge is personal and contextual, so while hands- on activities are important, they are inadequate to build new connections. The learner must be motivated and actively engage their mind to learn.

### Experiential Learning Cycle

David Kolb’s theory of experiential learning has been highly influential for simulation educators and is frequently combined with Jack Mezirow’s transformation learning theory. In health care education, the integration of simulation and clinical experience allows for a safe and effective way to develop understanding, belief, and behaviors consistent with professional competence. Two key approaches are repeated simulated events interspersed with facilitated reflection- on- action [Schon] and simulated events interspersed and actual clinical experienced with reflection and study (facilitated or not)<sup>65</sup> (Figs. 6.4 and 6.5).

### USES FOR SIMULATORS AND SIMULATIONS

A key turning point in the use of simulation was apparent in the early 2000s when patient safety became recognized as a key component of good health care and simulation was specifically named as a method to reduce patient harm.<sup>68</sup> Health care simulation went from being somewhat a novelty, with limited utilization in a few specialties, to an “ethical imperative.”<sup>69</sup> Currently, various types of simulators are found in practically every health care setting and used to train the diverse set of health care providers.<sup>70</sup> Anesthesiology, as a leader in the patient safety movement, has successfully integrated simulation education, assessment, evaluation, and research into every level of training and practice.

### PATIENT SIMULATION FOR EDUCATION AND TRAINING

Nearly every anesthesia program in the United States offers simulation training experiences, although the scope, frequency, and target content vary. Although there is some variation from one program to the next, it is common for programs to provide simulation- based skills practice for initial training of medical students and nurse anesthesia students, residents and fellows, certified nurse anesthetists, and attending anesthesiologists.

Some examples of technical skills included in simulation exercises include practice on training manikins and other devices that provide a mechanism for learners to go through the motions of learning the steps required to safely perform procedures in an environment that does not put the patient at risk but ensures that the trainee achieves the skills necessary to transfer them to the clinical setting (Video 6.1). Skills can be practiced until a baseline level of skill is achieved and errors in performance can be corrected ahead of patient encounters. The “simulators” used for this practice range from low- tech, home- made items that provide a way to mimic the feel of a procedure to more advanced devices for procedure- specific haptic, augmented, or virtual reality specialty machines that provide feedback about performance as a procedure is learned. More sophisticated simulators include a computer- controlled curriculum that progressively builds from simple to challenging cases with feedback and a scoring rubric. Gamification is often used to motivate the learner. These devices allow learners to proceed at their own pace until competence is achieved without the need for a live coach to be continually present.

Simulation- based education may also be used to provide a more realistic demonstration of a clinical situation or to develop familiarity with health care processes or settings, especially for novices. ### PATIENT SIMULATION FOR ASSESSMENT

In addition to providing an opportunity to train providers in skills and interactions critical to the clinical environment while minimizing risk to patients, simulation is frequently employed to provide a consistent, safe method of evaluating and assuring that a person can actually perform a task or figure out a realistically presented clinical problem. Often referred to as “competency assessment,” many fields use simple simulations or simulators to provide assurance that health care providers have the basic skills needed to perform a task such as measuring blood pressure, placing an IV, or performing a lumbar puncture. The simulation model selected must be appropriate for the level and type of task or skill being assessed. For example, an assessment of a medical student performing a cardiac exam could utilize a comprehensive cardiac simulator such as Harvey or a real human model; an assessment of laparoscopic surgery might employ a basic task trainer model, a haptic simulator, or a laboratory animal.

Simulators have also become valuable tools in assessing other skills unrelated to specific procedures or technical capabilities. They can be successfully employed to assess communication skills and professionalism, often with the assistance of standardized patients (SPs), usually actors who are specifically trained to portray a particular state of health or disease. This model can be used to evaluate history and physical examination skills, communication between patients and providers as well as other traits that determine the level of professionalism in the patient encounter. In these scenarios, the SP is often trained to evaluate the student’s performance on a number of key measures. To do so effectively, SPs must be trained to provide a consistent narrative.<sup>73</sup>

### Simulation and OSCE

The OSCE (Objective Structured Clinical Examination) is a simulation- based assessment tool that has become integrated into training and certification processes. It was originally developed in the 1970s to “sample” the learner’s performance across a range of skills. In the 1990s this format was adapted for surgical or technical skills as

the OSATS (Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills) by Reznick _et al._<sup>74</sup>

Administering an OSCE is resource intensive and requires a high level of skill and expertise, not only of what to test, but also how to test and measure what is deemed important. Scoring can be challenging, particularly to ensure that all learners are assessed based on the same criteria<sup>75</sup> and in many cases there is a tension between measuring authentic skills and assuring a fair and accurate test. One must be aware that key skills are not really measured by the OSCE, even a well- designed one. Factors that are inherently human, such as persistence, humor, flexibility, and honesty, may not be measured at all or may actually reduce a student’s score. Additionally, some things are more easily tested with available “simulators” but are less important to include and _vice versa_ . A robust, reliable, and meaningful OSCE is labor- and time- intensive, making it a challenge to deliver.<sup>76</sup> Nevertheless, the OSCE remains a commonly used and potentially meaningful assessment of actual clinical performance.

### Debriefing and Feedback

The use of high- fidelity simulation continues to expand as a method to both train and assess clinical competence, decision- making, and team- based care. The debriefing is an integral and critical component of the simulation experience. Not only does it ensure an appropriate level of oversight and supervision, but also it provides the opportunity to review the experience and each of the components of a clinical scenario with the learners. For most of these simulation experiences, the simulation is not only directly observed by the supervisors, often viewed from a location remote to the actual simulation, but also video recorded for follow- up review. Both the direct observation during the simulation and review of the recorded session allows the supervisor/ teacher to monitor what each trainee is doing and for interdisciplinary models how each participant interacts with their colleagues and the simulated patient, depending on the model.<sup>77,78</sup>

After completion of these simulated experiences, the participants are given the opportunity to observe their performance and provide discussion of the strengths, weaknesses, and opportunities for improvement. The debriefing allows for discussion about clinical skills, decision- making process, and other aspects of the simulation to receive feedback and foster self- reflection for the learner in a way that fosters professional growth and development in what is hopefully a non- judgmental, but constructive manner. A variety of debriefing models have been described to make the experience most beneficial to the learners. The most important aspect of the debriefing session and feedback is that it provides the learner with insights based on the review of the performance that are insightful and demonstrate areas in which there is opportunity for improvement.<sup>78–80</sup>

### Simulation for Continuing Medical Education and Remediation

Many simulation centers offer continuing medical education (CME) and training for experienced practitioners. Many of the simulation models used to train and evaluate students and residents have been expanded to assess skills, knowledge, and decision- making for practicing providers. Several studies have demonstrated

<!-- PDF page 14 -->

that experienced anesthesiologists have deficiencies in the management of critical patient situations and make severe errors comparable to those of anesthesia residents. A variety of simulation models have been modified to address specific areas of concern and provide both education and assessment to ensure ongoing competence. In addition, simulation can be useful in assessing the provider who has not been practicing for some period of time for a variety of reasons (leaves of absence, illness, etc.). Additionally, since many clinical events are rare, particularly crisis situations, simulation can be an effective way of reinforcing skills and management issues unique to some of these situations.

### PATIENT SIMULATION FOR SYSTEM EVALUATION AND PROCESS TESTING

Simulated cases are an excellent way to find out how a system or planned process works (and much better than finding practice gaps during real patient encounters). The most common approach is to initiate a “call for help” in an actual patient area (also known as _in situ_ ), and then watch what happens. Planning is essential, both for the roll- out of an improvement program, and for the activation of each event, as there may be a high degree of psychological risk to participants. To mitigate this risk, potential participants should know ahead of the evaluation that an unannounced emergency may occur as part of the assessment. The purpose and goals should be clear to everyone, including administration; the goals would not include targeting individuals involved in the mock response. Since responders typically have other demands, it is imperative that events of this type be kept short and focused. Finally, in a clinical setting, the mock event cannot interfere with actual patient care or other emergencies.

The information gleaned from these “mock” events is focused on how the whole system functions. If participants do not have the knowledge or skills to perform the necessary care, the educational program may be questioned; if needed equipment is not available or functional, one might look at how it is stocked or if equipment was changed or on shortage; if the team is slow to arrive, the delay might be accounted for by work overload, slow elevators, faulty alarm system, or other system failures. Since the goal is to assess system- related issues, the focus of these assessments must not focus on any one individual’s performance.

Process or protocol testing can be done in the simulation center or _in situ_ . In this case, the goal is to determine how a policy or process is actually implemented. Many plans that look great on paper are found to have important gaps or outright failures when put into action. Using simulation to perform a “final check” on the feasibility and workability of a protocol can allow for adjustments and clarification before it is released.

One popular use of both system assessment and process review can be seen in simulated mass casualty or disaster events.<sup>81</sup> These are often held on a large scale in military settings, but there is an increasing reliance on these largescale, multisystem, multidepartment, and multilocation simulations in health care settings. Health care is relatively inexperienced in how to best leverage these events for highest value,<sup>82</sup> but given the growing frequency of actual

disaster events and the growing acceptance of simulation to assist with planning, disaster simulation is increasingly needed.<sup>83</sup>

Finally, simulation has been used to investigate poor outcomes and their causes. One application recreates a case or situation to help identify the actual root causes after a concerning clinical event. The case is run multiple times with a different team each time. If the different teams end up with the same problem, it must be due to a system fault rather that the individuals.<sup>84</sup> Simulation has also been used extensively as a method for teaching how to understand the concept of root cause analysis,<sup>85</sup> and how to apply this approach to actual case reviews.<sup>86–88</sup> Finally, simulation has been used to re- create a case to educate a jury for a malpractice case.<sup>89</sup> The details of the simulation were taken directly from the records, and the recording of the re- enactment was able to better convey the timing and complexity of the situation much better than verbal description.

### PATIENT SIMULATION FOR PRODUCT EVALUATION

Health care equipment and technology options have grown at a rapid pace with devices that were only the stuff of science fiction a short time ago. The interface between this technology and the humans who use it can contribute to a variety of safety concerns and actual mishaps. Unfortunately, this is often identified after new equipment is put into use for patient care, possibly because engineers and health care experts have different mental models and expectations. Human factors engineering is a discipline that studies how tools are designed to interface with humans, and it plays an important role in improving patient safety. Companies have a strong incentive to identify product flaws that will lead to poor acceptance before marketing, and have adopted methods to anticipate and address potential problems as early as possible such as Prospective Health Technology Assessment.<sup>90</sup> Despite this important work, it behooves purchasers to trial new equipment, especially for large purchases or emergency equipment because the final product is not always as user- friendly as hoped. This can usually be readily accomplished with actual clinical staff using simulation. One example was a simple comparison of three automatic external defibrillators (AEDs). The study asked lay responders to use each device and then measured how quickly the device could be deployed. Subjects were also asked which device they found easier to use.<sup>91</sup> Usability data is an essential factor for determining which AED to purchase; once that is determined, other factors can then be considered, but an emergency device that cannot be easily deployed is of no use.

In many cases, equipment is assumed to be usable merely because it is available, however, the user interface may be quite challenging or non- intuitive for people with a health care mindset rather than an engineer’s perspective. Trialing monitors, pumps, hospital beds, and other large purchases is fairly simple. Another area where simulation is highly effective is in the design and construction of clinical space where multimodal simulation can be employed throughout the design, planning, and construction phases to enhance usability and mitigate threats and deficiencies.<sup>92</sup>

## Crisis Resource Management

Crisis resource management (CRM) in health care is based on the concepts developed for the airline industry related to crew resource management (cockpit resource management). The term arose as a result of the realization that planes crashed in spite of good planes and good pilots in situations that should have been manageable. Former pilot and author, David Beaty, wrote a book in 1969 describing the principles related to a hierarchal culture, poor communication strategies, and particular cognitive errors. These principles largely apply today for the airline industry, with marked similarities for health care and anesthesia.<sup>93</sup>

Crisis resource management refers to a set of principles dealing with cognitive and interpersonal behaviors that contribute to optimal team performance. Investigations of airline accidents identified that the majority of errors were related to teamwork failures rather than deficiencies in either knowledge or technical skills. In response, the industry developed simulation- based “crew resource management” training programs that focused on core teamwork behaviors such as leadership, communication, situational awareness, and resource utilization. These same concepts were adopted by anesthesiology as the first specialty to incorporate CRM principles into the training of its clinicians. In the late 1980s, David Gaba and his colleagues recognized the many similarities between the experience in the airline industry and anesthesiology with respect to errors and unintended consequences of care despite the extensive knowledge and skills of anesthesiologists. Anesthesia training had traditionally emphasized the technical skills necessary to manage the anesthetized patient but did not address some of the behavioral aspects of either the individual provider or the team performance. In response, an Anesthesia Crisis Resource Management (ACRM) simulation- based course was implemented to address the importance of teamwork and collaborative decision- making, interpersonal communication, and leadership. ACRM has been wellreceived and widely adopted by anesthesiologists around the world.<sup>94</sup>

Based on this background, simulation programs have been developed, often utilizing sophisticated models to advance training in crisis management that emphasized collaborative, team- based approaches to address many of the complex situations that have potential risk and to incorporate both the technical and behavioral considerations into the training.
