# Neuromuscular Disorders and Other Genetic Disorders

<!-- PDF page 1 -->


JIE ZHOU, JING ZHAO, XIAOBING YU, ALA NOZARI, ISAAC N. PESSAH, and PAUL D. ALLEN

### KEY POINTS

- Malignant hyperthermia (MH) is a pharmacogenetic disorder inherited primarily in an autosomal dominant pattern.

- MH susceptibility (MHS) is linked to 230 mutations in the skeletal muscle ryanodine receptor (RyR1) and four mutations in the calcium voltage- gated channel subunit alpha1 S (CACNA1S) genes that encode two Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels necessary for skeletal muscle excitation–contraction coupling.

- Physical interactions between L- type Ca<sup>2+</sup> channel (Cav1.1) and RyR1 tightly regulate initiation and termination of skeletal muscle excitation–contraction coupling.

- Skeletal muscle accounts for approximately 40% of body weight and inherent changes in its metabolism have profound impacts on whole- body metabolism and physiology.

- Carriers of MH mutations can exhibit mild to moderate muscle impairments in the absence of triggering agents but are rarely diagnosed.

- Carriers of MH mutations are susceptible to anesthetic- triggered runaway skeletal muscle metabolism, which if not promptly treated is lethal.

- Signs of MH, including increased end- tidal CO2, increased core temperature, muscle rigidity, tachycardia, and more _,_ are consequences of the fulminant hypermetabolic crisis.

- Exposure to triggering agents or heat stress leads to acute loss of RyR1/CaV1.1 channel regulation, rapid accumulation of Ca<sup>2+</sup> within the sarcoplasm, and a hypermetabolic crisis that stimulates adenosine triphosphate (ATP) utilization by pumps attempting to restore resting Ca<sup>2+</sup> balance among sarcoplasmic reticulum, mitochondrial, and extracellular compartments.

- Dantrolene markedly attenuates myoplasmic calcium (Ca<sup>2+</sup> ) concentrations and thereby restores resting Ca<sup>2+</sup> balance and metabolism, with reversal of clinical signs.

- Evaluation of persons susceptible to MH includes an _in vitro_ contracture test (IVCT) and caffeine/halothane contracture test (CHCT), and evaluation of DNA to identify mutations.

- Currently DNA testing alone can be used to evaluate 42 human mutations and all swine, equine, and canine MH.

- Future MH goals include advancement of genetic evaluations in North American and European medical programs and stronger finances to support genetic studies, the identification of the mode of action of dantrolene, a determination of the immediate cause of triggering MH, and the development of effective, noninvasive tests for MH susceptibility.

- The absence of mutations in dystrophin, along with dystrophin- associated glycoproteins, is involved in sarcolemmal stability. Its defects are responsible for Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD).

- Whereas the risk for an MH mutation in DMD and BMD patients is like that in the general population, the incidence of MH- like anesthetic events has been reported to be 0.002 with DMD and 0.00036 with BMD.

- Succinylcholine is contraindicated in DMD and BMD patients because of the risk of rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalemia due to their unstable sarcolemmal membrane.

- Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis patients have exaggerated respiratory depression and sensitivity to sedatives and hypnotics and have higher risk for aspiration and pulmonary complications.

- For patients with fibrodysplasia ossificans progressive, awake nasal fiberoptic intubation is the first choice for airway management under non- emergent conditions, and intramuscular injection and regional anesthesia should be avoided as heterotopic ossification may exacerbate after muscle injury.

- Patients with limb- girdle muscular dystrophy are generally sensitive to opioids and muscle relaxants; short- acting anesthetic agents are preferred.

- Patients with glycogen storage disease type I should be monitored frequently to avoid hypoglycemia when they are fasted, and lactate- containing solutions should be avoided in these patients.

- Cardiomyopathy and cardiac failure are present in many patients with glycogen storage disease type II, and deterioration of diaphragmatic function can lead to postoperative acute respiratory failure in these patients.

- Anesthesia has a significant impact on mitochondrial function. For patients with mitochondrial diseases and mitochondrial myopathies, the implications of the patients’ comorbidities and metabolic status are more important than the specific choice of anesthetic agents.

- Guillain–Barré syndrome is an immune- mediated acute polyradiculopathy. Autonomic dysfunction may lead to hemodynamic instability with an exaggerated response to anesthesia induction agents or stimulating interventions such as laryngoscopy.

- Prolonged immobility in critically ill patients is associated with a relative increase in immature AChRs that can lead to an insensitivity to nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent (NMBA), while the sensitivity to depolarizing neuromuscular blockers is increased with risk for life- threatening succinylcholine- induced hyperkalemia. Succinylcholine is therefore contraindicated in this condition.

- Myasthenia gravis is a disorder of neuromuscular transmission caused by autoantibodies against the muscle-type AChRs. Because of the decreased number of functional AChRs, these patients are very sensitive to nondepolarizing NMBAs but resistant to succinylcholine.

- For myasthenia gravis patients, a preoperative vital capacity &lt;2 L is thought to be associated with a higher risk of intensive care unit admission. Other risk factors include pyridostigmine dose >750 mg, preoperative bulbar symptoms, high anti- AChR antibody titers, prior history of myasthenic crisis, and an expected blood loss >1 L.

- Reversal of neuromuscular blockade with sugammadex is a practical alternative to the management of many of these disorders, if rocuronium or vecuronium is used. The combination of rocuronium and sugammadex has improved the anesthetic management for some of these challenging disorders.

## Malignant Hyperthermia

Malignant hyperthermia (MH) has the potential to be one of the most devastating anesthesia- related complications. The fulminant MH syndrome is elicited by the administration of triggering anesthetic agents, such as volatile halogenated anesthetics or depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs). MH has been and continues to be a lifethreatening complication of anesthesia if the diagnosis is not made promptly and treatment is not begun in a timely fashion. Unlike other disorders described in this chapter, MH has virtually no characteristic phenotype before exposure to the triggering agent and is truly an example of the interaction of genes and the environment. Also covered in this chapter are some of the neuromuscular disorders. These disorders challenge both perioperative management by affecting the function of central and/or peripheral nerves, the neuromuscular junction, and/or muscles. Although such diseases are thought to be rare, the number of patients that a clinician may encounter is increasing because of better medical care, increasing longevity of patients with these diseases, and other possible unidentified factors.

MH is a pharmacogenetic clinical syndrome that, in its classic form, occurs during anesthesia with inhaled anesthetics such as halothane, isoflurane, sevoflurane, desflurane, and/or administration of the depolarizing muscle relaxant succinylcholine. The fulminant MH episode observed clinically involves muscle hypermetabolism as demonstrated by tachycardia and in increasing arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2). Late signs include rapidly increasing body temperature, by as much as 1°C in 5 minutes, and extreme acidosis. The molecular basis for these signs and symptoms is an acute loss of control

of intracellular ionized calcium (Ca<sup>2+</sup> ). It is the sustained high level of myoplasmic Ca<sup>2+</sup> that rapidly drives skeletal muscle into a hypermetabolic state and subclinical or clinically observed muscle contractures that may proceed to severe rhabdomyolysis. Although MH was initially associated with a mortality rate of 60%, earlier diagnosis and the use of dantrolene have reduced the mortality to less than 1.4%.<sup>1</sup> Estimates of the incidence of fulminant MH vary widely from one case per 10,000 to 1:250,000 anesthetics administered.<sup>2,3</sup> However, the prevalence of MH mutations in the general population may be as high as 1:2000.<sup>4</sup> Males appear to be more susceptible to developing a clinical MH episode than females.<sup>5,6</sup> A sex difference in MHS has also been demonstrated in knock- in mice expressing human MH mutation _RyR1_ - T4825I.<sup>7</sup> The pediatric population accounts for 52.1% of all MH reactions.<sup>8,9</sup>

Between 50% and 80% of genotyped patients who have had a clinical MH syndrome and a positive muscle biopsy have had their disease linked to one of more than 230 mutations in the type 1 ryanodine receptor ( _RyR1_ ; sarcoplasmic reticulum [SR] Ca<sup>2+</sup> release channel) gene and 3 mutations in _CACNA1S_ which encode the pore containing subunit of the sarcolemmal slow voltage gated L- type Ca<sup>2+</sup> channel (CaV1.1, also referred to as the dihydropyridine receptor [DHPR]).<sup>10</sup> The genetics of MHS and the related abnormal function of _RyR1_ , the DHPR, and associated proteins are being investigated at the molecular level, with a porcine model and several new mouse models which have provided intricate details about the etiology of the disorder. Parallel studies in humans are limited by scarce material for scientific study and are complicated by the fact that phenotypes within a single genotype vary because of sex, age, genetic, epigenetic, and environmental modifiers.

### HISTORY

Between 1915 and 1925, one family experienced three anesthetic- induced MH deaths with rigidity and hyperthermia and was puzzled for decades regarding the cause of these deaths.<sup>11</sup> Susceptibility to MH was eventually confirmed in three descendants by _in vitro_ muscle biopsy tests.<sup>11</sup> In 1929, Ombrédanne described anesthesia- induced postoperative hyperthermia and pallor in children accompanied by significant mortality but did not detect any familial relationships. Critical worldwide attention to MH began in 1960 when Denborough and associates reported a 21- year- old Australian with an open leg fracture who was more anxious about anesthesia than about surgery because 10 of his relatives died during or after anesthesia.<sup>12</sup> Denborough and colleagues initially anesthetized him with the then- new agent halothane, halted it when signs of MH appeared, successfully treated the symptoms, aborted the syndrome, and subsequently used spinal anesthesia. Further evaluations by George Locher in Wausau, Wisconsin, and Beverly Britt in Toronto, Canada, led to the discovery that MH risk was indeed familial.<sup>13</sup> It was also found that the cause of the syndrome was the result of skeletal muscle involvement rather than central loss of temperature control by the recognition of increased muscle metabolism or muscle rigidity early in the syndrome, low- threshold contracture responses, and elevated creatine kinase (CK) values.<sup>14</sup>

Interestingly, a similar syndrome was discovered in swine derived from breeding patterns designed to produce a rapid growth rate, lean bacon, and superior ham muscle development: e.g., Landrace, Piétrain, Duroc, and Poland China. In 1966, Hall and co- workers reported that a syndrome that appeared to be identical to MH could be induced in stress- susceptible swine by the administration of halothane and succinylcholine.<sup>15</sup> The problem with these swine that had been noticed earlier was that they were susceptible to the porcine stress syndrome,<sup>16</sup> which is associated with increased metabolism, acidosis, rigidity, fever, and death from rapid deterioration of muscle that results in pale, soft, exudative unsaleable pork.<sup>17</sup> This syndrome can be triggered by any stress, such as separation, shipping conditions, weaning, fighting, coitus, or preparation for slaughter, and had become a significant problem for meat production using these breeds. The cause of this syndrome

in pigs was discovered to be a single missense mutation in _RYR1_ , and all susceptible swine have the same Arg615Cys mutation in the SR calcium release channel RyR1.<sup>18</sup>

In 1975, Harrison described the efficacy of dantrolene in preventing and treating porcine MH,<sup>19</sup> which was rapidly confirmed in humans by a multi- hospital evaluation of dantrolene used to treat anesthetic- induced MH episodes.<sup>20</sup> Today, dantrolene still remains the primary pharmacologic approach for successful MH therapy.

### PHYSIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

MH is a syndrome caused by dysregulation of excitation– contraction coupling (ECC) in skeletal muscle. Normal muscle contraction is initiated by nerve impulses arriving at the neuromuscular junction (i.e. _,_ the motor end plate) that trigger the release of acetylcholine (ACh) from the nerve terminal. ACh activates nicotinic cholinergic receptors (nAChR), the nonselective cation channels located at the postsynaptic neuromuscular junction, and initiates action potentials. The action potentials propagate rapidly along the sarcolemma (muscle cell membrane) and travel down the transverse tubules (invaginations of the sarcolemma, T tubules). A T tubule is flanked on both sides by a terminal cisternae element from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), forming triad junctions. The depolarization of T- tubular membrane is sensed by CaV1.1, the pore subunit of the voltage- gated calcium channel residing within the T tubules. Freeze fracture of the surface membrane has shown that CaV1.1 aligns in tetrads opposite to a single RyR1 embedded in the SR at the triad junctions. Conformational changes of CaV1.1 mechanically communicate with RyR1, causing it to open and release large amounts of calcium from the SR into the sarcoplasm (Fig. 31.1). The released Ca<sup>2+</sup> binds to accessory protein troponin C which is on the actin thin filament. It then causes a conformational change in tropomyosin, exposing actin’s myosin binding sites, which allows shortening and/or force development by the muscle fibers (i.e. _,_ a muscle contraction).

The contraction ends when the intracellular Ca<sup>2+</sup> pumps, SR Ca<sup>2+</sup> - adenosine triphosphatases [ATPases] (SERCAs), rapidly sequester Ca<sup>2+</sup> back into the SR lumen. Muscle relaxation begins when the Ca<sup>2+</sup> concentration falls below 10<sup>−6</sup> M and ends when the resting sarcoplasmic Ca<sup>2+</sup> concentration is restored to 10<sup>−7</sup> M.

Because both contraction and relaxation are energyrelated processes that consume adenosine triphosphate (ATP), knowing the molecular events contributing to ECC and the subsequent relaxation phase is essential to understanding the cause of MH (Fig. 31.2). Clinical and laboratory data from humans, swine, and mice with knock- in mutations indicate that the fulminant MH syndrome is associated with a persistent increase in the concentration of sarcoplasmic Ca<sup>2+</sup> .<sup>21–24</sup> The increased activity of pumps and exchangers trying to correct the increase in sarcoplasmic Ca<sup>2+</sup> associated with triggered MH increases the need for ATP, which in turn produces heat. Thus, the common etiologic feature of the disorder is hyperthermia. The rigidity that is frequently seen during a fulminant MH episode is the result of the inability of the Ca<sup>2+</sup> pumps and transporters to reduce the unbound sarcoplasmic Ca<sup>2+</sup> below the contractile threshold (10<sup>−6</sup> M). ### Ryanodine Receptors

Ryanodine receptors (RyRs) within the muscle are synonymous with the junctional foot protein/SR calcium release

channel, and are so named because they specifically bind the toxic plant alkaloid ryanodine, which can activate or inhibit the channel depending on its concentration.<sup>33,34</sup> Each functional RyR is a homotetramer consisting of four identical subunits (∼5000 amino acids each).<sup>35</sup> The total mass of the tetramer exceeds 2 megadaltons, making it the largest known ion channel in mammals. ### Voltage- Gated Calcium Channels: Role of CaV1.1

The voltage- gated calcium channel DHPR embeds in the sarcolemma, detects the action potential in the T tubule, and communicates the signal to the RyR1. Skeletal DHPR consists of four principal subunits: the pore- forming α1s subunit, the auxiliary extracellular α2δ subunit, the cytoplasmic β1a subunit, and the transmembrane γ subunit. The α1s subunit of DHPR, also referred to CaV1.1, is the major subunit and contains the voltage sensor. Though CaV1.1 functions as a Ca<sup>2+</sup> channel, it is not the Ca<sup>2+</sup> current but the conformational change of the protein upon depolarization that is responsible for the signal transmission to RyR1.<sup>39</sup>

About 1% of MH cases are caused by mutations in the _CACNA1S_ gene, which encodes CaV1.1.<sup>40</sup> Although normally the DHPR acts as a RyR1 stabilizer, preventing spontaneous opening, the mutations located in different domains of CaV1.1 appear to sensitize the calcium release to activation by the triggering agents like caffeine and volatile anesthetics.<sup>41–44</sup> The mechanism of how this happens remains to be explored.

Other cellular processes can affect MH episodes. Firstly, functional neuromuscular junctions and/or depolarization of the sarcolemma may be the precondition for MH, supported by the observation that concurrent administration of nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocking drugs at the same time as triggering agents can delay or prevent the onset of clinical MH syndrome.<sup>45</sup> Secondly, excitationcoupled Ca<sup>2+</sup> entry (ECCE), which is a pathway of extracellular Ca<sup>2+</sup> entering into skeletal muscle cells, is sensitive to the conformation of the RyR1 and is enhanced by several mutations in _RYR1_ , including MH mutations.<sup>26,46,47</sup> In addition, classic store- operated capacitive Ca<sup>2+</sup> entry pathways similar to the store- operated Ca<sup>2+</sup> entry seen in non- excitable cells have been shown to be present in skeletal muscle and appear to be more active in MHS muscles both at rest as a response to chronic store depletion and

during an MH crisis.<sup>48–51</sup> This enhanced calcium entry, when combined with decreased sensitivity of RyRs to Ca<sup>2+</sup> and Mg<sup>2+</sup> inhibition, could provide cellular conditions that heighten sensitivity to triggering agents and perpetuate the fulminant clinical MH syndrome.

### GENETICS

Mutations in _RYR1_ are the most common defects present in patients who developed MH. _RYR1_ mutations have been found in 50% to 80% of patients and relatives who are labeled MHS by positive contracture tests and in almost all families with central core disease (CCD) and King–Denborough syndrome (KDS) (see MH- Associated Syndromes/ Pathological Conditions, below). More than two hundred missense mutations and 8 deletions associated with MH have thus far been detected.<sup>10</sup> It was thought that most _RYR1_ mutations were clustered in three “hot spots”: between amino acid residues 35 and 614 (MH/CCD region 1, C- terminal), between amino acid residues 2129 and 2458 (MH/CCD region 2, Central) in the sarcoplasmic foot region of the protein, and between amino acids 3916 and 4942 in the carboxyl- terminal transmembrane loop or pore region (MHS/CCD region 3, N- terminal).<sup>10</sup>

Another gene that has been shown to be associated with MH is _CACNA1S_ ,<sup>52</sup> which codes for CaV1.1 (the α1S- subunit of DHPR). Two causative mutations in this gene are linked to less than 1% of MHS families worldwide. Recently, a homozygous mutation of the _STAC3_ gene, which encodes the protein STAC3 that directly participates in the interaction between CaV1.1 and RyR1,<sup>25</sup> has been linked to Native American myopathy associated with MHS in one Native American family.<sup>53</sup> However, until now there are only 48 mutations of the _RYR1_ gene, and 2 mutations of the _CACNA1S_ have been described as diagnostic MH mutations by EMHG.<sup>54</sup> Hence for practical reasons, the _RYR1_ gene remains the primary target for current clinical genetic analysis.

The true ethnic distribution of MH is difficult to ascertain. Genetic analysis of MH patients has been predominantly reported in North America and Europe, and several mutations of _RYR1_ and _CACNA1S_ have been detected in more than one family there.<sup>55–61</sup> The mutation common to North America and Europe is G2434R, which occurs in 5.5% of North American and 4% to 7% of European families.<sup>62</sup> Single- family mutations are the most common mutations reported in Japanese, Chinese, and Australians.<sup>63–65</sup> The unique family mutations in Asia and Australasia may reflect the small number of cases that have been investigated.

Inheritance of human MH is predominantly in an autosomal dominant fashion with variable penetrance. More than one MH- linked mutation has been identified in some probands and families. Six non- consanguineous families harbor at least two _RYR1_ mutations that have both been linked to MHS, and in two of those families, one is an _RYR1_ mutation and the second is a CaV1.1 mutation.<sup>4</sup> Allelespecific differences in _RYR1_ mRNA expression and monoallelic silencing has been observed,<sup>66,67</sup> which may help explain the variable penetrance of MH mutations. ### In Vitro Contracture Test (IVCT) and Caffeine Halothane Contracture Test (CHCT)

Prior to having easy access to gene sequencing, the gold standard for diagnosis of MH is the halothane and caffeine muscle contracture test, also known as the IVCT or CHCT, developed by the European Malignant Hyperthermia Group (EMHG) and the North American Malignant Hyperthermia Group (NAMHG), respectively.<sup>69,70</sup> The two protocols are similar, but not identical. For differentiation, we designate IVCT to the EMHG protocol and CHCT to the NAMHG protocol.

For IVCT, the muscle biopsy will be performed on the quadriceps (either vastus medialis or vastus lateralis)<sup>71,72</sup> and consists of three parts: a static caffeine test, a static halothane test, and a dynamic halothane test.<sup>72</sup> For the static caffeine test, a stepwise increased concentration (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, and 32 mmol/L) is applied. The lowest concentration of caffeine which produces a sustained increase of at least 0.2 g in baseline tension is reported as the caffeine threshold. Then, the halothane threshold is obtained using the same method by exposing the muscle to halothane concentrations of 0.5%, 1%, 2%, and 3% v/v. The dynamic halothane test is performed with the muscle stretched at a constant rate of 4 mm/min to achieve a force of approximately 3 g and held at the new length for 1 minute for a 3- minute exposure to halothane. For each cycle, halothane concentration will be increased from 0.5%, 1%, 2%, to 3% v/v: volume (solute) per volume (solvent). The concentration of halothane which produces a sustained increase of at least 0.2 g in the muscle tension compared to the pre- halothane control is defined as the dynamic halothane threshold.<sup>72</sup> The IVCT protocol formerly classified the patients into three groups: MH Susceptible (MHSHC) group with a caffeine threshold at the caffeine concentration of 2 mmol or less and a halothane threshold of 2% v/v halothane or less; MH Normal (MHN) group with a caffeine threshold at the caffeine concentration of 3 mmol or more without a halothane response at 2% v/v; MHSH and MHSC groups, which describe individuals that used to be classified as MHE (equivocal) who are only responsive to either halothane or caffeine alone.<sup>70</sup> The MHE descriptor was dropped because use of the “equivocal” label outside of its laboratory context has the potential to confuse patients and clinicians unfamiliar with its derivation and these patients are now considered for clinical purposes to be MHS.<sup>70</sup>

For CHCT, a muscle biopsy can be taken from the following sites in the order of preference: (1) the vastus group, (2) the rectus abdominis, and (3) other muscle groups under special circumstances.<sup>73</sup> Required tests include exposure of muscle to 3% v/v halothane alone and to incremental caffeine concentrations (0.5, 1, 2, 4 mmol, and 8.0 mmol if the response at 4 mmol is &lt;1 g, and 32 mmol) alone. Optional tests include exposure of muscle to a combination of both 1% halothane and incremental caffeine concentrations, and to 2% v/v halothane alone.<sup>73</sup> According to CHCT protocol, an individual is MHS when either of the halothane or the caffeine test is positive, and MHN when both tests are negative.<sup>73</sup>

The sensitivity of IVCT was reported to be 99.0% (95% confidence interval [CI] 94.8%–100%) if the MHE group is considered susceptible and the specificity of IVCT was 93.6% (95% CI 89.2%–96.5%),<sup>72</sup> while the sensitivity and specificity of CHCT was 97% (95% CI 84%–100%) and 78% (95% CI 69%–85%), respectively.<sup>74</sup> Recently, fluoroquinolones and statins, 3- hydroxy- 3- methylglutaryl- coenzyme A (HMG- CoA) reductase inhibitors, were found to induce significant contractures in MHS muscle bundles, but not in MHN.<sup>75,76</sup> Ondansetron and 3,4- methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA) may also dose- dependently induce contracture or increase the sensitivity of the contractile apparatus to calcium in both MHS and MHN fibers.<sup>77,78</sup> Modifications to the IVCT protocol by adding ryanodine or 4- chloro- _m_ - cresol,<sup>79,80</sup> a RyR- specific agonist, has been reported, but has not been included in the standard protocol. Furthermore, Metterlein and associates studied the possibility of replacing halothane with newer volatile agents in IVCT.<sup>81</sup> At increasing concentration, except for sevoflurane, all newer volatile agents, including enflurane, isoflurane, and desflurane, induced significantly greater contractures in MHS muscle compared to MHN bundles. However, within the MHS muscle bundles, halothane produced significantly higher contractures, and was considered the strongest discriminator for MH using the IVCT protocol.<sup>81</sup> A direct application of high sevoflurane concentration of 8%, instead of the stepwise application, has been shown to induce significantly stronger contractures in MHS subjects.<sup>82</sup> Nevertheless, from the retrospective analysis of the Japanese MH database, there was no evidence between the severity of MH triggered by sevoflurane and isoflurane, or other agents, suggesting sevoflurane is a weak or weaker MH triggering agent.<sup>83</sup>

### Discordance Between Genetic TEST and IVCT/ CHCT

Discordance has confounded linkage analysis worldwide. Examples include MHN patients carrying an _RYR1_ mutation associated with MH and MHS patients who do not carry the familial _RYR1_ mutation. Several explanations are possible, the most likely being that IVCT/CHCT is not clinically precise and that the thresholds for IVCT or CHCT are inexact. This would lead to errors in determining whether a patient was MHN or MHS. A second possibility is the variable penetrance of MH mutations (see Genetics, above). A third reason is that individuals with discordance have mutations in other unknown genes or modifier genes that affect the function of RyR1 and its phenotypic penetrance. There remains significant fraction of approximately 15%–30% patients studied after what was considered a clear MH episode in whom a causative mutation in _RYR1_ or _CACNA1S_ has not been discovered.<sup>40</sup> New pathological mutations which may influence susceptibility to MH could be the reasons for the segregation to loci on chromosomes 1, 5 and 7.<sup>59</sup>

### Guidelines for Genetic Screening

In 2000, the EMHG formulated guidelines for _RYR1_ mutation screening with linkage data to other loci for some MH families, but all MH investigators emphasized the vital role of IVCT in the diagnosis of MH.<sup>84</sup> These guidelines for screening have reduced the number of relatives requiring

<!-- PDF page 7 -->

contracture testing without increasing the risk of misdiagnosis.<sup>85,86</sup> In 2015, the EMHG published a revision of the guidelines for the investigation of MHS. This updated guideline provided a detailed patient referral criteria and clinical interpretation of IVCT results.<sup>70</sup>

Only a small number of MHS families have been investigated extensively in North America by phenotyping, linkage analysis, and screening of specific genes. Over the past few years, genetic screening of _RYR1_ mutations in MHS patients had been carried out in the USA,<sup>61</sup> UK,<sup>56</sup> Japan,<sup>87</sup> and Switzerland.<sup>55</sup> The penetrance of MH mutations is variable, while once MH occurs the morbidity is high. This underscores the complication of determining the prevalence of MHS in the absence of an inexpensive MH diagnostic test. Nevertheless, recommendation for genetic screening should be carefully considered based on solid clinical suspicion.

### ANESTHETIC TRIGGERING

Fulminant MH is rare. Acute episodes of MH depend on four variables: a genetic (perhaps rarely acquired) predisposition, the absence of inhibiting factors, the presence of an anesthetic or nonanesthetic trigger, and the presence of environmental factors that could potentiate the action of one or more of the other three variables.

Anesthetic drugs that trigger MH include ether, halothane, enflurane, isoflurane, desflurane, sevoflurane, and depolarizing muscle relaxants, the only currently used of which is succinylcholine. Desflurane and sevoflurane appear to be less potent triggers than halothane and produce a more gradual onset of MH.<sup>88,89</sup> The onset may be explosive if succinylcholine is used. MHS swine were traditionally screened by induction with a volatile anesthetic, which led to pronounced hind limb rigidity within 5 minutes, frequently sooner.<sup>90</sup> Prior exercise even an hour before induction of anesthesia increased the severity and hastened the onset of rigidity in swine.<sup>90</sup> Similarly, in the new knock- in mouse models, the onset of limb rigidity after commencing exposure to volatile anesthetics is very rapid. There are also several modifying factors that are more likely to be present in humans than in pigs or mice and can alter, or even prevent, the onset of clinical MH. Mild hypothermia and pre- administration of barbiturates, tranquilizers, propofol, or nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers delay or prevent the onset of MH in MHS humans, thus making them respond less predictably than swine or MH knock- in mice.<sup>23,45,90,91</sup> There have been many instances in which fulminant MH has been reported in patients who have previously tolerated potent triggers without difficulty.<sup>68,92</sup> The reason behind why this occurs is unknown, but it is likely to be related to prior or concurrent administration of drugs that prevent or delay onset of the syndrome, as described earlier, or unknown environmental influences that help provoke the positive incident. Thus, onset of the syndrome in humans is extremely variable both in initial symptoms and in the time of onset of the syndrome. Its onset is so variable that making the diagnosis in the setting of a clinical anesthetic can be quite difficult. Although not perfect, the clinical grading scale developed by Larach and colleagues is a useful way for clinicians to retrospectively determine whether a patient who responded abnormally to anesthesia is in any way likely to actually have had a clinical MH

episode.<sup>93</sup> However, MH is most easily diagnosed prospectively by vigilance, recognizing its signs and symptoms, and knowing how to treat the syndrome.

The two classic clinical manifestations of fulminant MH syndrome may start in one of the following two scenarios.

### Scenario 1

1.  Rigidity after induction with thiopental and succinylcholine, but successful intubation, followed rapidly by the symptoms listed after scenario 2.

2.  Normal response to induction of anesthesia and uneventful anesthetic course until onset of the following symptoms:

   - Unexplained sinus tachycardia or ventricular arrhythmias, or both

   - Tachypnea if spontaneous ventilation is present

   - Unexplained decrease in O2 saturation (because of a decrease in venous O2 saturation)

   - Increased end- tidal partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2) with adequate ventilation (and in most cases unchanged ventilation)

   - Unexpected metabolic and respiratory acidosis

   - Central venous desaturation

   - Increase in body temperature above 38.8°C with no obvious cause

### Scenario 2

The usually muted onset of MH is in most cases detected quickly by the development of tachycardia, increased levels of expired CO2, and muscle rigidity. It can be delayed for several reasons and may not be overt until the patient is in the recovery room. Once initiated, the course of MH can be rapid. When clinical signs such as increased expired CO2, muscle rigidity, tachycardia, and fever suggest MH, more than one abnormal sign must be observed before making the diagnosis because according to a meta- analysis of many reported cases, a single adverse sign does not usually indicate MH.<sup>93</sup> The mechanism by which anesthetics and depolarizing muscle relaxants trigger MH is unsolved, but it cannot be ignored that they are etiologic agents and that early diagnosis is critical for successful treatment.

### NONANESTHETIC MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA

MH can be triggered by stress such as exercise and overheating, known as “awake” MH. Numerous anecdotal reports of MH- like episodes in humans after stressful situations were reported.<sup>94–99</sup> Measurement of plasma catecholamine levels during exercise showed no differences between MHS and normal individuals.<sup>100,101</sup> Therefore, it is unlikely that these responses were provoked by sympathetic overdrive or catecholamine surge.<sup>102</sup>

In susceptible swine, environmental stress, such as exercise, heat, anoxia, apprehension, and excitement, triggers fulminant MH (see History, above).<sup>9,90</sup> These responses are related to muscle movement or to increased temperature. Increased ambient temperature triggers fulminant MH in four strains of heterozygous MH mice and in two homozygous strains.<sup>103,104</sup> Epidemiologic studies have shown that exercise- induced symptoms, including rhabdomyolysis, may occur more frequently in MHS patients,<sup>99,105</sup> and mutations in _RYR1_ and _CACNA1S_ were present in a

<!-- PDF page 8 -->

considerable number of patients with exercise- induced rhabdomyolysis.<sup>94,98,99,106</sup> Other reports are largely anecdotal and relate heat stroke, sudden and unexpected death, unusual stress and fatigue, or myalgia to possible “awake” MH episodes. Stresses associated with these episodes include exercise and environmental exposure to volatile nonanesthetic vapors.<sup>107–109</sup> In the United States, MHAUS provided recommendations of adverse effects of heat and exercise in relation to MHS.<sup>110</sup>

### DIAGNOSIS IN THE OPERATING ROOM AND POSTANESTHESIA CARE UNIT

As stated earlier, fulminant MH is rare, and early signs of clinical MH may be subtle (Box 31.1). These signs must be distinguished from other disorders with similar signs (Box 31.2).

When the diagnosis is obvious (i.e. _,_ fulminant MH or succinylcholine- induced rigidity with rapid metabolic changes), marked hypermetabolism and heat production occur, and there may be little time left for specific therapy to prevent death or irreversible morbidity. If the syndrome begins with slowly increasing end- tidal CO2 (defined earlier), specific therapy can await a complete clinical workup before treatment. In general, MH is not expected to occur when no triggers are administered (see Anesthetic Management of MHS Patients, below). However, several confirmed fulminant nonanesthetic cases of MH that resulted in death have been reported (see Nonanesthetic Malignant Hyperthermia, above).<sup>105</sup>

When volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine are used, MH should be suspected whenever there is an unexpected increase in end- tidal CO2 (ETCO2), undue tachycardia, tachypnea, arrhythmias, mottling of the skin, cyanosis, muscle rigidity, sweating, increased body temperature, or unstable blood pressure. If any of these occur, signs of increased metabolism, acidosis, or hyperkalemia must be sought. The most common cause for sudden ETCO2 during general anesthesia and sedation is hypoventilation. Increased minute ventilation should be able to correct such a problem.

The diagnosis of MH can be supported by the analysis of arterial or venous blood gases, which demonstrates a mixed respiratory and metabolic acidosis<sup>111</sup> ; however, the respiratory component of acidosis may be predominate in the very early stage of the onset of fulminant MH. O2 and CO2 change more markedly in the central venous compartment than in arterial blood; therefore end- expired or venous CO2 levels more accurately reflect whole- body stores. Venous CO2, unless the blood drains an area of increased metabolic activity, should have PCO2 levels of only about 5 mm Hg greater than that of expected or measured PaCO2. In small children, particularly those without oral food or fluid for a prolonged period, the base deficit may be 5 mEq/L because of their smaller energy stores.

### TREATMENT

Dantrolene is the only drug which has been shown to be effective in both preventing and reversing fulminant MH.<sup>103,112</sup> Dantrolene sodium is a hydantoin derivative (1- [[[5- (4- nitro phenyl)- 2- furanyl]methylene]imino]- 2,4- imidazolidinedione)

that does not block neuromuscular transmission, but causes muscle weakness by direct muscular action. The properties of dantrolene have been closely correlated with its ability to reduce efflux of Ca<sup>2+</sup> from the SR.<sup>113–115</sup> The idea that

<!-- PDF page 9 -->

dantrolene suppresses SR Ca<sup>2+</sup> release as a result of direct interactions with RyR1 is somewhat controversial.<sup>116</sup> Dantrolene also attenuates depolarized–triggered Ca<sup>2+</sup> entry mediated by CaV1.1, which is exacerbated in MHS muscle cells and MH normal muscle cells exposed to ryanodine.<sup>117</sup> Thus whether dantrolene directly inhibits RyR1 or requires additional intermediates within the triad junctions remains to be clarified. The 2017 discovery that inhibition of SR Ca<sup>2+</sup> release by dantrolene requires Mg<sup>2+</sup> may help resolve the controversy of the conflicting observations on dantrolene inhibition of RyR1 channel activity in Mg<sup>2+</sup> - free bilayer experiments.<sup>118</sup>

Acute management for MH can be summarized as follows:

1.  Discontinue all triggering anesthetics, maintain intravenous agents, such as sedatives, opioids, and nondepolarizing muscular blockers as needed, and hyperventilate with 100% oxygen with a fresh flow to at least 10 L/min. With increased aerobic metabolism, normal ventilation must be increased.<sup>3</sup> However, CO2 production is also increased because of neutralization of fixed acid by bicarbonate; hyperventilation removes this additional CO2.

2.  Administer dantrolene rapidly with the initial dose of 2.5 mg/kg intravenously to a maximum of 10 mg/kg until the initial symptoms subside.

3.  Administer bicarbonate (1–4 mEq/kg intravenously) to correct the metabolic acidosis with frequent monitoring of blood gases and pH.

4.  Control fever by cooling interventions. Circulating water mattresses or ice packs should be considered first. If external cooling is insufficient, infuse 20 mL/kg of refrigerated intravenous (IV) crystalloid. Peritoneal lavage is probably the safest and most effective of the invasive approaches if the peritoneum is already open. Cooling should be halted at 38°C to prevent inadvertent hypothermia.<sup>119</sup>

5.  Monitor and treat arrhythmia. Advanced cardiac life support protocol may be applied.

6.  Monitor and maintain urinary output to greater than 1 to 2 mL/kg/h and establish diuresis if urine output is inadequate. Administer bicarbonate to alkalinize urine to protect the kidney from myoglobinuria- induced renal failure.

7.  Further therapy is guided by blood gases, electrolytes, CK, temperature, muscle tone, and urinary output. Hyperkalemia should be treated with bicarbonate, glucose, and insulin, typically 10 units of regular insulin and 50 mL of 50% dextrose for adult patients. The most effective way to lower serum potassium is reversal of MH by effective doses of dantrolene. In severe cases, calcium chloride or calcium gluconate may be used.

8.  Recent data demonstrated that magnesium level could be a prerequisite for dantrolene efficacy in managing MH crisis.

9.  Analyze coagulation studies (e.g. _,_ international normalized ratio [INR], platelet count, prothrombin time, fibrinogen, fibrin split, or degradation products).

10.  Once the initial reaction is controlled, continued monitoring in the intensive care unit for 24 to 48 hours is usually recommended.

Adequate personnel support is critical to the successful management of such a crisis. Discontinuation of the trigger may be adequate therapy for acute MH if the onset is slow or if exposure was brief. Changing the breathing circuit and CO2 absorbent can be time- consuming. However, application of activated charcoal filters may rapidly reduce the volatile anesthetic concentration to an acceptable level in less than 2 minutes, if they are readily available.<sup>120</sup>

The dantrolene from two suppliers is packaged in 20- mg bottles with sodium hydroxide for a pH of 9.5 (otherwise it will not dissolve) and with 3 g of mannitol (converts the hypotonic solution to isotonic). The initial dose should be 2 to 3 mg/kg dantrolene reconstituted in sterile water and administered intravenously. Dantrolene in this presentation must be reconstituted in sterile water rather than salt solutions or it will precipitate. It has been shown that prewarming of sterile water may expedite the solubilization of dantrolene compared to water in ambient temperature.<sup>121</sup> In 2009, a newer, rapid lyophilized powder form of dantrolene became available for intravenous use. It reconstitutes as a suspension that makes it safe to administer a full dose much faster than the older versions.<sup>122</sup> The higher dose per vial, 250 mg compared to 20 mg, of the newer version of dantrolene also reduces the storage space but makes it more difficult to give less than a 2.5 mg/kg dose without waste. It has a similar recommended shelf life as the older versions.

In awake healthy volunteers, the maximum twitch depression occurs at a dantrolene dose of 2.4 mg/kg.<sup>123</sup> Therefore it is not surprising that at therapeutic concentrations, dantrolene may prolong the need for intubation and assisted ventilation. Brandom and associates reviewed the complications associated with the administration of dantrolene from 1987 to 2006 using the dataset in the NAMHR via the AMRA reports and found that the most frequent complications of dantrolene were muscle weakness (21.7%), phlebitis (9%), gastrointestinal upset (4.1%), respiratory failure (3.8%), hyperkalemia (3.3%), and excessive secretions (8.2%).<sup>124</sup> Given its high pH, it is advisable to administer dantrolene through a large-bore IV line. It has been demonstrated that dantrolene interferes with EC coupling of murine intestinal smooth muscle cells, rat gastric fundus, and colon, which in part explains its gastrointestinal side effect.<sup>125,126</sup> Caution should be used when ondansetron is to be used in this setting. As a serotonin antagonist, ondansetron may increase serotonin at the 5- HT2A receptor in the presynaptic space. It has been suggested that in MHS individuals, agonism of 5- HT2A receptor may produce a deranged response, precipitating MH.<sup>127</sup>

Recrudescence of MH can approach 50%, usually within 6.5 hours, but also can occur in up to 16 hours after the initial reaction is controlled.<sup>128,129</sup> Therefore, after initial bolus dosing to treat the acute MH crisis, maintenance dantrolene should be continued at a 1- mg/kg dose every 4–6 hours for 24 hours while monitoring the patient for signs of recrudescence.<sup>119</sup> The total dose of dantrolene that can be used is up to 30 mg/kg in some cases. When recrudescence occurs, calcium and cardiac glycosides may be used safely, and CPR may be required. They can be lifesaving during persistent hyperkalemia. Slow voltage- gated calcium channel blockers do not increase porcine survival.<sup>130,131</sup> Instead, a study by Migita demonstrated that calcium channel

<!-- PDF page 10 -->

blockers, including dihydropyridine (i.e., nifedipine), phenylalkylamine (i.e., verapamil), and benzothiazepine (i.e., diltiazem), led to increased Ca<sup>2+</sup> in human skeletal muscle cells.<sup>132</sup> Interestingly, the potency of such calcium release is correlated with the number of binding sites on DHPR (i.e., nifedipine > verapamil > diltiazem).<sup>132</sup> Clinical doses of dantrolene were only able to attenuate 20% of the nifedipineinduced Ca<sup>2+</sup> surge.<sup>132</sup> Current recommendations of MHAUS discourage the use of calcium channel blockers in the presence of dantrolene because they can worsen the hyperkalemia resulting in cardiac arrest. Although administration of magnesium sulfate could not prevent the development of MH and did not influence the clinical course in succinylcholine- induced MH,<sup>133</sup> recent data suggested that dantrolene might require magnesium to arrest the course of MH triggered by halothane.<sup>118</sup> Permanent neurologic sequelae, such as coma or paralysis, may occur in advanced cases, probably because of inadequate cerebral oxygenation and perfusion for the increased metabolism and because of the fever, acidosis, hypo- osmolality with fluid shifts, and potassium release.

For MH cases diagnosed in the ambulatory surgical centers, guidelines have been recently proposed for the transferring of care to receiving hospital facilities.<sup>134</sup> Although immediate treatment and stabilization of the patient must be achieved onsite, several factors need to be considered before implementation of a transfer plan, which include capabilities of the available professionals at the initial treatment and receiving facilities, clinical best interests of patients, and capabilities of the transfer team. The validity of stocking dantrolene in ambulatory surgery centers was confirmed with a cost- effectiveness analysis.<sup>135</sup>

### MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA SUSCEPTIBILITY

MHS is suspected in individuals with a history of a clinical event indicative of an acute MH crisis, or with a family history of susceptibility, or who tested positive by IVCT or CHCT, or were found to have a pathogenic mutation in _RYR1_ , _CACNA1S_ , or _STAC3_ gene by genetic screening. The prevalence of MHS varies from 1/900 to 1/2000 in different studies,<sup>4,87,136,137</sup> even though clinical incidence of MH is much lower.<sup>2,3</sup> The mainstay of prevention is the identification of these genetically susceptible individuals before anesthesia.

Evaluation of susceptibility includes a history and physical examination to detect any subclinical abnormality. A genealogy with specific information about anesthetic exposure and agents can estimate the likelihood of exposure to triggering agents. Blood CK values, when determined in a resting, fasting state without recent trauma, reflect muscle membrane stability. When the CK level is elevated in a close relative of a person with known MHS, the cautious approach would be to consider them to be MHS without genetic screening or contracture testing confirmation. If the CK level is normal on several occasions, there is no predictive value, and if possible, genetic screening and/or contracture studies are necessary. The patient must travel to the test center for a surgical biopsy to ensure viability and accurate results. Muscle biopsy contracture studies are performed at about 30 centers around the world (only 4 centers in North America<sup>138</sup> ; 15 accredited and 18

non- accredited units by the EMHG<sup>139</sup> ). It is also important to note that contracture responses are sometimes positive in patients with myopathies that bear no direct relationship to MH and therefore may not indicate MHS. Dantrolene must be avoided before biopsy because it masks the response to contracture- producing drugs. The order of what should be done first, muscle biopsy or genetic screening, is controversial. If a mutation is detected, the proband and other relatives with that mutation should be considered MHS without the need for an invasive contracture test, and they need not travel to a testing center. If that is not the case, then it is important to have the proband tested with a muscle biopsy if possible.

MHS patients and all patients who are not biopsy tested, but who present with a clinical picture that suggests a high probability for MHS, should be given advice. Precautions are necessary regarding general anesthesia, and triggers include all potent volatile agents and succinylcholine. The true predictive value (i.e. _,_ percentage of positive results that are true positives) or efficiency (i.e. _,_ percentage of all results that are true, whether positive or negative) of contracture testing in determining susceptibility in the general population cannot be estimated because of the selection process that has been used to date for testing (i.e. _,_ limited to those with anesthesia reactions who do not have any other muscle disease pathology). False- positive results from cautious interpretation or decreased specificity are masked because if the patient has an MHS diagnosis they will never be exposed to triggering agents. In an attempt to decrease the invasiveness of a muscle biopsy, a promising innovative technique of microdialysis infusion of caffeine or halothane into muscle appears to be able to differentiate MHS, based on exaggerated localized changes in acid–base balance.<sup>140–144</sup> Some MH teams have studied B- lymphocytes, which express RyR1, to provide a substrate for a less invasive analysis for susceptibility. Unfortunately this test has the limitation that not all MHrelated proteins, such as DHPR or STAC3, are expressed in the white blood cells.<sup>145–149</sup> Nuclear magnetic resonance to detect a presumed resting metabolic difference between MHS and MHN has promise,<sup>150,151</sup> but to date it has been difficult to standardize a stress, such as forearm ischemia, that can differentiate MHS from MHN individuals.

For anesthesia assessment of the non- MHS pregnant patient carrying a potential MHS fetus, the parturient should be treated as MHS until the fetus is delivered.<sup>152–154</sup> For emergencies in such patients, the use of succinylcholine, although little of the drug crosses the placenta, is controversial.<sup>155</sup>

### CENTRAL CORE DISEASE

Central core disease (CCD) is a rare hereditary disease. A recent population study in northern England revealed a prevalence of 1:250,000.<sup>163</sup> As mentioned, almost all CCD cases are due to dominant missense mutations in the _RYR1_ gene. Clinically, CCD patients present with muscle weakness of variable degree and histologically with central cores in the type I skeletal muscle fibers.<sup>164</sup> Serum CK levels in CCD patients are often normal but may be elevated up to 6 to even 14 times normal in rare cases. Muscle ultrasound often demonstrates increased echogenicity in the quadriceps muscle with relative sparing of the rectus muscle. This characteristic pattern of selective involvement can also be seen on the muscle magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and has been reported in the patients with typical CCD,<sup>165</sup> which seems to be distinctive to conditions linked to _RYR1_ locus. The relationship between CCD and MHS is complex. A positive IVCT test has been confirmed in many patients with CCD, whereas MHS has been excluded in some. In consideration of the strong link and potential risk, it is advisable to consider all patients with CCD at risk for MH unless the patient has a negative IVCT.

### MULTIMINICORE DISEASE

### HEAT- OR EXERCISE- RELATED RHABDOMYOLYSIS

The symptoms of rhabdomyolysis include hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, rhabdomyolysis, or even life- threatening hyperkalemia, which resemble the signs of MH. There are many cases of patients with a history of heat- or exerciseinduced rhabdomyolysis who either subsequently developed anesthesia- induced MH, tested positive by muscle biopsy contracture test, or were found to possess a pathogenic mutation in the _RYR1_ or _CACNA1S_ gene.<sup>94,96,98,9</sup> 9,105,106,159,160 There are also patients known to be MHS that subsequently had a serious MH- like syndrome during exposure to heat and/or intense exercise.<sup>95,97,109,161,162</sup> However, the relationship between MH susceptibility and heat- /exercise- related rhabdomyolysis remains unclear. Given the possible same etiology of heat- /exercise- induced rhabdomyolysis and MH, patients with a history of unexpected rhabdomyolysis triggered by heat or exercise should be treated as MHS.

Multiminicore disease (MMD) is also known as minicore myopathy and multicore myopathy, as characteristic short core lesions called minicores were found in the muscle fibers. MMD is considered a recessively inherited myopathy with severe axial weakness, while respirator, bulbar, and extraocular muscles are commonly affected.<sup>164</sup> MMD has a heterogeneous genetic association with a recessive mutation in the _SEPN1_ gene on chromosome 1p36 and in the _RYR1_ gene.<sup>164</sup> Although MHS has not been reported in _SEPN1_ - related myopathies, it is prudent to apply a non- triggering approach to MMD patients, given the potential risk in _RYR1_ - related MMD. In a large family of MH, MMD was found in 16 of the 17 MHS patients (confirmed by IVCT test).<sup>166</sup> Clinical MH reactions have also been reported in MMD patients.<sup>167,168</sup>

### KING–DENBOROUGH SYNDROME (KDS)

To address the KDS, we first introduce Noonan syndrome, an autosomal dominant condition involving the face, cardiovascular, hematologic, and skeletal systems. Named after Dr. Jacqueline Anne Noonan, a pediatric cardiologist, typical Noonan syndrome features include delayed puberty, down- slanting or wide- set eyes, hearing loss, lowset or abnormal shaped ears, mild mental retardation (in about 25% of the cases), ptosis, short statue, small penis and undescended testicles in males, pectus excavatum, and a webbed and short neck. The incidence is 1:1000–1:2500 live births.<sup>170</sup> Fifty percent of the patients have proteintyrosine phosphatase, nonreceptor- type II ( _PTPN2_ ) mutations.<sup>171</sup> Other genes involved are _SOS1, KRAS, RAF1, BRAF, MEK1,_ and _NRAS_ , and they encode proteins that are part of the Ras (a GTPase)–mitogen activated protein kinase (RAS–MAPK) signaling pathway.<sup>172</sup> Noonan syndrome was recently defined as part of the neuro- cardiofacial- cutaneous syndrome family.<sup>173</sup> An earlier study with a series of 27 patients demonstrated one case of mildly elevated CK despite multiple uneventful cases of general anesthesia with halothane and succinylcholine.<sup>174</sup> Although there is weak evidence for MHS for patients with Noonan syndrome, its resemblances to KDS should raise the concern for the confirmation of the diagnosis. Prevalence of bleeding disorders in Noonan syndrome was reported to be from 20% to 89%,<sup>172</sup> ranging from thrombocytopenia to platelet dysfunction to von Willebrand disease to factor deficiencies. Routine screening including, but not limited to, bleeding history, platelet count, coagulation panel, and factor XI level, was recommended.<sup>173,175</sup> Hematologic consultation becomes appropriate if any of these tests are abnormal. The high palatal arch, dental malocclusion, and the webbed neck of Noonan syndrome make tracheal intubation potentially risky,<sup>176</sup> and considering that the associated odontoid hypoplasia and atlanto- axial instability may result in cervical cord compression, preoperative cervical spine evaluation is advisable.<sup>177</sup> Right ventricular function needs to be monitored closely because 30% to 50% of the patients with Noonan syndrome have pulmonary stenosis.<sup>178</sup> Regional anesthesia in Noonan patients may be technically challenging due to the prevalence of scoliosis. There are anecdotal reports that some human patients have experienced a hypermetabolic state despite these precautions, but they have always responded favorably to the administration of intravenous dantrolene. ## Other Neuromuscular Disorders

As for malignant hyperthermia, many neuromuscular disorders are associated with serious perioperative challenges and complications requiring careful perioperative considerations. Neuromuscular disorders are traditionally categorized as central and peripheral neuropathy, neuromuscular junction disease, and myopathy. The disorders can also be classified into hereditary/mutational diseases and acquired (e.g. _,_ stress, infection, druginduced) diseases (Box 31.3).

### CENTRAL NEUROPATHY: MOTOR NEURON DISORDERS

Motor neuron disorder (MND) is a progressive and fatal disorder from progressive loss of motor neurons of the spinal cord, brainstem, and motor cortex.<sup>203</sup> MND involves either the upper or the lower motor neurons of the cerebral cortex, brainstem, and spinal cord. Some forms are mixed, whereas others have predominately upper or lower motor neuron involvement. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS, also known as Lou Gehrig’s disease) is the most common disease within this group and involves both upper and lower motor

neurons. Other examples of motor neuron disease are Kennedy disease (spinobulbar muscular atrophy), Friedreich ataxia (mixed upper and lower motor neurons), and spinal muscular atrophy (lower motor neurons). ALS is characterized by degeneration of the anterior horn α- motoneurons in the spinal cord and brainstem motor nuclei, as well as the primary descending upper motor neurons of the corticospinal tract. Degenerative loss of these neurons leads to progressive muscle weakness, muscle atrophy, and loss of neuronal mass in these locations. Patients present with gradually spreading focal weakness and muscle atrophy (typically of the hands), spasticity, and hyperreflexia of lower extremities. Dysarthria and dysphagia, tongue atrophy, and fasciculations may also occur. Progressive weakness can lead to respiratory failure and death. Frontotemporal dementia is also a common representation of MND. Sensory functions, including intellectual capacity and cognition, as well as bowel and bladder function, are not usually affected in ALS.

Most MND cases are sporadic, but rare familial forms (autosomal dominant and recessive forms) do exist. MND can be affected by environmental factors. ALS has an incidence of about 2 in 100,000, and onset of the disease usually

### Anesthetic Considerations

Bulbar involvement in combination with respiratory muscle weakness leads to a risk for aspiration and pulmonary complications. Notably, these patients may have increased sensitivity to the respiratory depressant effects of sedatives and hypnotics. There are reports of sympathetic hyperreactivity and autonomic failure. Sympathetic hyperreactivity and autonomic dysfunction, often manifested as orthostatic hypotension and resting tachycardia but also significant hypotension or even pulseless electrical activity upon anesthesia induction, have been reported,<sup>208</sup> and should be considered during the perioperative management of these patients. Succinylcholine is usually avoided because of the risk for hyperkalemia due to denervation and immobilization. Nondepolarizing NMBA may cause prolonged and pronounced neuromuscular blockade; however, successful reversal with sugammadex has been reported.<sup>209</sup> Caution is needed for specific disease progress of the patients with regards to the reliability of neuromuscular monitoring based on ulnar nerve stimulation and subsequent measurement of train- of- four ratio if it were to be used to guide muscular relaxation reversal dosage. Both general and epidural anesthesia have been successfully administered to these patients (Box 31.4).

Major manifestations of CMT include symmetric, slowly progressive distal motor neuropathy of the arms and legs resulting in weakness and atrophy of the muscles in the feet and/or hands.<sup>210</sup> Damage to sensory axons may also lead to sensory loss leading to frequent tripping and falls. Neuropathic pain may develop in some patients. Typical symptoms are distal muscle weakness and atrophy, weak ankle dorsiflexion, depressed tendon reflexes, and _pes cavus_ (high- arched) foot deformity. Clinical evaluation and nerve conduction studies can help affirm the diagnosis, supported by a family history.<sup>210</sup> Diagnostic findings include nerve conduction velocity, typically significantly reduced to less than 10 ms.<sup>212</sup> Slow conduction with both distal motor latencies and F- wave latencies are prolonged.<sup>210</sup> For treatment, exercise is generally encouraged for HMSN, which could slow the rate of progression. Recent disease- modifying therapy targeting intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate- signal molecule (cAMP) has been found to lower toxic _PMP22_ gene expression.<sup>210</sup>

### MYOPATHIES: MYODYSTROPHIES, MYOTONIC MYOPATHIES, AND METABOLIC MYOPATHIES

Myopathies mainly involve disorders of skeletal muscles. Skeletal muscle consists of multinucleated cells called muscle fibers. The cell membrane of muscle fibers is also called sarcolemma, which forms transverse tubes (T tubes) across the myofibrils. Sarcoplasmic organelles in muscle fibers include bundles of myofibrils, longitudinal tubes, and mitochondria. Myofibrils are surrounded by sarcoplasmic endoplasmic reticulum (SR). The part of SR that locates between the junction of the T tube and the longitudinal tube is called terminal cistern. Terminal cisterns store Ca<sup>2+</sup> while muscles are at resting phase. In the gap between T tube and SR, a protein attached to SR (i.e., the RyR receptor) acts to activate calcium ion release through electrical contraction coupling. The myofibrils are composed of contraction proteins (myosin and actin) and regulatory proteins (tropomyosin, troponin, and nebulin). Calcium ions promote contractile proteins of myofibrils to interact to achieve muscle contraction, whose energy comes from ATP produced by oxidative metabolism of mitochondria. Myopathies may result in varying degrees of muscle dysfunction, atrophy, degeneration, or regeneration of skeletal muscles throughout the body. Myocardium could be affected as well. Myodystrophy and myositis could present with myofibrillary lesions. Abnormal membrane potential may occur. Metabolic myopathies may present electrical contraction coupling abnormalities. Myopathies are characterized by muscle strength, muscle tension, and muscle metabolite abnormality. Electromyography (EMG) and muscle biopsy may show myogenic myopathy lesions.

### Central Core Disease

Central core disease (CCD) is one of the earliest known hereditary myopathies, also known as the allelic disease

of MH. Almost all CCD cases are due to dominant missense mutations in the _RYR1_ gene (see MH- Associated Syndromes/Pathological Conditions, above).

### FIBRODYSPLASIA OSSIFICANS PROGRESSIVA

Characterized by pathological extraskeletal bone formation within muscle and connective tissues, heterotopic ossification (HO) is commonly acquired following orthopedic surgery, severe burns, blast or combat wounds, and traumatic injury of the central nervous system.<sup>220,221</sup> Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP) is a rare autosomal- dominant musculoskeletal disorder characterized by progressive HO of muscle, fascia, ligaments, and tendons,<sup>222</sup> which results in progressive disability and significant comorbidities.<sup>223–226</sup> Approximately 97% of patients with FOP carry an arginine to histidine gain- offunction point mutation in the intracellular domain of the bone morphogenetic protein (BNP) type I receptor ACVR1 (ACVR1<sup>R206H</sup> ),<sup>222</sup> which constitutively activates intracellular signaling through phosphorylated SMAD1/5/8<sup>227</sup> and also misinterprets Activin A as a BMP ligand, resulting in abnormal BMP- like signaling.<sup>228</sup>

Malformed great toes at birth and migratory soft tissue swellings are early hallmarks of FOP.<sup>229</sup> Unfortunately, up to 90% of FOP patients can be misdiagnosed initially: the soft tissue swellings are often mistaken for lymphedema, soft tissue sarcoma, or aggressive juvenile fibromatosis.<sup>230</sup> In a 2005 report, more than half of patients underwent unnecessary invasive procedures such as biopsies that could trigger robust HO and result in permanent harm.<sup>222,230</sup> Other characteristic skeletal malformations include clinodactyly, vertebral abnormalities, shortened and broadened femoral necks, and tibial osteochondroma.<sup>231–239</sup> HO typically impacts the upper back and neck first, before progressing to the trunk and extremities as the patient ages.<sup>224</sup> Moreover, progressively ossified muscles of mastication and ankylosis of the temporomandibular joints (TMJ) limit mouth opening and lead to feeding difficulty and poor oral hygiene, which eventually requires dental procedures.<sup>240</sup>

In addition to the remarkable skeletal abnormalities, various non- musculoskeletal phenotypes have been observed.<sup>241</sup> The most common causes of death in patients with FOP are cardiopulmonary complications from thoracic insufficiency syndrome or pneumonia.<sup>242</sup> The development of thoracic insufficiency syndrome in FOP can result from costovertebral chest wall deformities, leading to restrictive lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, and right congestive heart failure.<sup>242</sup> Structural cardiac defects have been observed but with unknown clinical significance.<sup>243,244</sup> Notably, chronic pain affects 86% of patients in one cohort<sup>245</sup> and 23%–31% of patients reported hypersensitivity to touch or abnormal temperature sensation at baseline.<sup>246</sup> Mechanical and heat pain hypersensitivity in adult patients with FOP have been documented by quantitative sensory testing (QST),<sup>247</sup> the clinical “gold standard” for assessing pain thresholds and clinical function of small- diameter sensory neurons. Of particular interest is that ACVR1<sup>R206H</sup> is both necessary and sufficient for the hyperexcitability of sensory neurons differentiated from the patient- derived pluripotent stem cells (iPSC), hallmark of

<!-- PDF page 16 -->

neuropathic pain.<sup>247</sup> Together, FOP should be considered and managed as a multisystem disease.

### Anesthetic Considerations

Cervical spine fusion, ankylosis of the TMJ, and severe restrictive pulmonary disease complicate airway management and pose significant challenges to the anesthesia providers. To secure the airway in FOP patients, general anesthesia is commonly recommended.<sup>240</sup> For all elective procedures, preoperative anesthesia evaluation is required with a multidisciplinary perioperative care plan.

As stretching the TMJ during direct laryngoscopy may exacerbate HO and result in permanent ankylosis, an awake nasal fiberoptic intubation is the first choice for airway management under non- emergent conditions. An otolaryngologist should be immediately available for a tracheostomy to manage a difficult airway. In addition, high- flow nasal cannula (HFNC) has been suggested as an alternative airway management in patients with FOP undergoing surgical procedures.<sup>248</sup>

During the surgery, all pressure points must be padded meticulously to minimize soft tissue injury. As ossification at the injection site after routine immunizations in children has been reported,<sup>249</sup> intramuscular injection should be avoided. In contrast, it is safe to place an intravenous or arterial catheter, although some technical difficulty may be experienced due to marked restriction of limb movement.

Given the extensive ossification at the thoracolumbar region of many patients with FOP, spinal or epidural anesthesia is technically challenging. Furthermore, the efforts may lead to catastrophic flare- ups. Although an ultrasoundguided ankle block successfully provided uneventful analgesia for a patient with FOP who underwent a foot surgery in a case report,<sup>250</sup> regional anesthesia remains to be relatively contraindicated if the block needle has to penetrate through the fascia and muscle layers. Notably, HO complicated by permanent ankylosis of the jaw was triggered by mandibular block during a dental procedure.<sup>251</sup>

To prevent and mitigate HO flare- ups incited by surgery or intubation, prophylactic perioperative corticosteroids are standardly administered. The detailed treatment guidelines can be found on the website of the International FOP Association (www.ifopa.com) (Box 31.5).

### X- LINKED MUSCULAR DYSTROPHY

X- linked muscular dystrophy includes Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD).<sup>252</sup> Both DMD and BMD are characterized by progressive weakness and wasting of predominantly the proximal musculature. Pseudohypertrophy of the calves and other muscle groups is common. As the more severe of the two diseases, DMD tends to be symptomatic early in life: 74% of children with DMD were found to manifest the disease by 4 years of age.<sup>252</sup> Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) may present with much wider range of severity.

DMD has an incidence of 1 per 3500 live male births.<sup>252</sup> BMD is relatively rare and has an incidence of about 1.53 per 100,000 males.<sup>253</sup> Both DMD and BMD are X- linked recessive diseases. The defect is located on the short arm of the X chromosome at the Xp21 region, which contains the gene for the large protein Dp427, also known as dystrophin.

Loss of muscle units accounts for the weakness and contracture.<sup>254</sup>

### Anesthetic Considerations

Patients with DMD and BMD may require anesthesia for muscle biopsy, correction of scoliosis, release of contractures, and exploratory laparotomy for ileus,<sup>217</sup> as well as for dental<sup>267</sup> and obstetric<sup>268</sup> procedures. As the natural course of the disease progresses, the risk of surgery increases concomitant with the increased comorbid conditions associated with the later phase of the disease. However, perioperative complications are not proportional to the severity

<!-- PDF page 19 -->

of the disease. They occur even in mildly affected patients. Consequently, patients should undergo careful preoperative consultation and evaluation.

Preoperative cardiology assessment is essential: 50%– 70% of patients demonstrate some cardiac abnormality, although only 10% clinically significant.<sup>217</sup> Unexplained cardiac arrest<sup>269,270</sup> and acute heart failure<sup>271</sup> have been reported in DMD/BMD patients. Continuous cardiac Holter monitoring is necessary if suspicious of arrhythmias. An echocardiography will demonstrate mitral valve prolapse in 10% to 25% of patients; it may also show posterobasilar hypokinesis in a thin- walled ventricle and a slow relaxation phase with normal contraction characterizing the cardiomyopathy seen in DMD.<sup>217</sup> However, echocardiography may not always reflect the ability of the diseased myocardium to respond to perioperative stress. A stress echocardiography using angiotensin to detect latent heart failure and identify inducible contraction abnormalities has been advocated.<sup>272</sup> Acute and progressive left ventricular dysfunction might result in markedly elevated cTnI level.<sup>265</sup>

An estimated 30% of deaths in individuals with DMD are due to respiratory causes.<sup>273</sup> Therefore careful preoperative pulmonary assessment is important. Webster demonstrated that the manual muscle strength test has a strong statistical correlation with all the timed functional tests. Peak expiratory flow was not only easy to perform, but also correlated statistically with all timed functional tests.<sup>274</sup> However, the correlation was not significant for VC or forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1).

In terms of airway management, patients with DMD and BMD may have decreased laryngeal reflexes and prolonged gastric emptying time, which increases the risk for aspiration.<sup>275</sup> Decreased ability to cough up the accumulation of oral secretions predisposes muscular dystrophy patients to postoperative respiratory tract infections.<sup>217</sup> Masseter spasm is also a possible complication during induction of anesthesia in these patients.<sup>269</sup> Preparedness for a difficult airway is necessary, especially in patients with potential airway problems. Postoperatively, DMD patients are at an increased risk for respiratory compromise. Retrospective reviews have indicated that the incidence of prolonged postoperative ventilation (>36 hours) was greatest in DMD patients who had a preoperative forced vital capacity (FVC) of less than 40% of the predicted value.<sup>276,277</sup> Preoperative pulmonary function studies are valuable in determining the postoperative course. Patients with a VC of greater than 30% of the predicted value can usually be extubated immediately after surgery.<sup>217</sup> Sleep apnea may also compound the condition and contribute to the development of pulmonary hypertension. Continuous positive airway pressure and bilevel positive airway pressure have been demonstrated to be effective in the management of postoperative respiratory depression. Delayed pulmonary insufficiency may occur up to 36 hours postoperatively despite the apparent recovery of skeletal muscle strength.<sup>278</sup>

Reports have suggested a relationship between DMD/ BMD and MH, but this association is not based on good rational grounds.<sup>279</sup> Whereas the risk for an MH mutation in DMD/BMD patients is similar to that of the general population, the incidence of MH- like anesthetic events has been reported to be 0.002 with DMD and 0.00036

with BMD. Succinylcholine is contraindicated in these patients because of the potential for rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalemia because of their unstable sarcolemmal membranes. Succinylcholine- induced hyperkalemia during acute rhabdomyolysis is more likely to result in cardiac arrest and unsuccessful resuscitation.<sup>278</sup> Although the use of nondepolarizing muscle relaxants is usually accompanied by an increase in both depth of neuromuscular block and duration of action,<sup>280</sup> proper and rapid reversal with sugammadex will assure return to patient baseline neuromuscular function. Current offlabel experience in infants and children has been favorable. Narcotics can be used, but small incremental dosing and short- acting medications are recommended given the respiratory depression associated with these medications, as well as the reports of inadvertent reactions to volatile anesthetics.<sup>217</sup>

Inhaled anesthetics may cause life- threatening rhabdomyolysis in these patients, therefore total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) has been recommended.<sup>281,282</sup> Regional anesthesia may be a good alternative to general anesthesia because it avoids the risk of triggering agents and respiratory depression and enables the use of local anesthetics for postoperative analgesia. It may also facilitate chest physiotherapy.<sup>283</sup>

### LIMB- GIRDLE MUSCULAR DYSTROPHY

Limb- girdle muscular dystrophy (LGMD) is a group of disorders with heterogeneous causes. The characteristic feature of this group of diseases is a genetically inherited, progressive proximal muscle (shoulder or pelvic) girdle weakness caused by a loss of muscle fibers, “which could result in loss of ambulation within 20 years after disease onset in most patients.”<sup>284</sup>

The estimated prevalence of LGMD is 1 to 6.5 in 100,000.<sup>285</sup> LGMD can be categorized into autosomal dominant (LGMD type 1) and autosomal recessive (LGMD type 2) with 8 subtypes and 26 subtypes, respectively. Sporadic cases of LGMD have been reported in the anesthesia literature.<sup>286–288</sup> LGMD2 is about 10 times more common than LGMD1. At least 18 causative genes of LGMD have been identified as being responsible for this disease.<sup>289</sup> Mutations within the same gene may result in different phenotypes. The most common gene mutations associated with LGMD include Fukutin- related protein ( _FKRP_ ) (for LGMD2) and calpain 3 ( _CAPN3_ ) (for LGMD1. In addition, a number of other disorders may have LGMD- like phenotypes.<sup>289</sup> Mutation of causative genes leading to abnormal protein synthesis localized to various parts of the muscle fiber is thought to be the pathogenesis of LGMD. The classic pathological change of LGMD involves degeneration and necrosis of muscle fibers, which are replaced by connective tissue and fat.<sup>285,290</sup>

The European NeuroMuscular Center (ENMC) in 2018 refined the definition of LGMD to include: elevated serum creatine kinase activity, degenerative changes on muscle imaging over the course of the disease, and dystrophic changes on muscle histology. The disease was previously classified by clinical manifestations, but now it is necessary to confirm the diagnosis by genotyping.<sup>290</sup> For treatment, oral glucocorticoid steroid may be beneficial for LGMD

General approaches to these patients are the same as those for DMD/BMD. LGMD may be complicated with cardiac or pulmonary involvements. Cardiac impairment should be noticed as well. Increased risk of rhabdomyolysis could lead to life- threatening complications for LGMD patients.<sup>285</sup> Since these patients are generally sensitive to opioids and muscle relaxants, short- acting anesthetic agents are preferred. Sugammadex would be a good candidate for reversal. When using regional anesthesia for patients with LGMD, the myotoxic effects of local anesthetic should be within the considerations.<sup>285</sup>

### MYOTONIC DYSTROPHY

Myotonic dystrophy ( _dystrophia myotonica_ , DM) is an inherited muscular disorder characterized by myotonia, progressive muscle weakness, and wasting. It is a multisystem disease with cardiac, digestive, ocular, and endocrine abnormalities with two subgroups: DM1 and DM2. DM1 refers to _dystrophia myotonica_ (Steinert disease), while DM2, formerly named proximal myotonic myopathy (PROMM), has a later onset.<sup>292</sup>

The prevalence of DM is 1 in 8000. DM1 is by far the most common of the two types and accounts for about 98% of all cases. DM1 is caused by a mutation in the _dystrophia myotonica_ protein kinase ( _DMPK_ ) gene, located on chromosome 19q13.3.<sup>293</sup> The mutation in the disease- causing gene is the expansion of a CTG trinucleotide repeat in the _DMPK_ gene resulting in abnormal expression of embryonic isoforms in the adult. DM is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner.<sup>293</sup> DMPK encodes a protein kinase. The CTG repeat’s size can increase through the years, which explains that DM1 becomes more severe in subsequent generations.<sup>292</sup> DM2 is a less severe form.<sup>294</sup> DM2 is caused by mutation in CysCysHisCys (CCHC)- type zinc finger, nucleic acid binding protein ( _CNBP_ ) gene, located on chromosome 3q21<sup>293</sup> ; intron 1 of the _CNBP_ gene contains a complex repeat motif, (TG)n(TCTG)n(CCTG)n, and expansion of the CCTG repeat was determined as the cause of DM2. Either chloride voltage- gated channel 1 (CLCN1) or sodium voltage- gated channel alpha subunit 4 (SCN4A) mutations may contribute to exaggeration of the myotonia in DM2. In both DM1 and DM2, mis- splicing of insulin receptor gene ( _INSR_ ) was responsible for its insulin resistance.<sup>294</sup>

Typical signs and symptoms of DM1 are usually mild and congenital, and include muscle weakness and wasting (most prominent in the cranial and distal limb musculature), periodic myotonia, progressive myopathy, insulin resistance, defects in cardiac conduction, neuropsychiatric impairment, cataracts, testicular atrophy, and frontal balding in males. The typical cranial muscle weakness and wasting are manifested not only in the facial, temporalis, masseter, and sternocleidomastoid muscles but also in the vocal cord apparatus. Mitral valve prolapse is found in 20% of patients.<sup>278</sup> First- degree atrioventricular

General considerations for DM are similar to those for other muscular dystrophies. Careful cardiac evaluation is necessary to identify patients at risk for potential cardiac major arrhythmia. Regular ECG and echocardiography screening is appropriate in these patients.<sup>294</sup> Close cardiac monitoring is required for DM patients, even in the postoperative period. Pacing equipment should be readily available because atrioventricular blocks may not respond to atropine.<sup>217</sup> Preoperative evaluation should also include blood glucose monitoring, thyroid function test, and serum calcium level.

The majority of complications were found to be pulmonary related and significantly more frequent in patients undergoing upper abdominal operations and those with severe disability, as assessed by the presence of proximal limb weakness.<sup>295</sup> The pulmonary complications of DM are the result of hypotonia, chronic aspiration, and central and peripheral hypoventilation.<sup>217</sup> Smooth muscle atrophy, which leads to poor gastric motility, when coupled with a diminished cough reflex, promotes aspiration.

Triggering factors, such as hypothermia, shivering, and mechanical or electrical stimulation, may also cause a myotonic reaction.<sup>296</sup> DM symptoms exaggerate during pregnancy, and some patients had their first myotonic symptoms during their pregnancy.<sup>294</sup> Perioperative temperature management is important as coldness could trigger the symptoms.

DM patients can be very sensitive to anesthetic agents, with hypersomnolence and CO2 retention sometimes being observed. Careful titration with relatively short- acting anesthetic agents may be beneficial. There is no case report in the literature linking DM to MH.<sup>297</sup> However, note that succinylcholine will produce contractions lasting for several minutes, thus making intubation and ventilation a challenge or even impossible. These contractions are not antagonized by nondepolarizing muscle relaxants. Other agents, including methohexital, etomidate, propofol, and even neostigmine, may also induce myotonic reactions. Case reports have demonstrated normal responses to sugammadex when rocuronium<sup>298</sup> was used as the neuromuscular blockade.<sup>298–300</sup>

### MYOTONIA CONGENITA

Myotonia congenita (MC) is a congenital form of muscular dystrophy characterized by uncontrolled temporary skeleton muscle excitability caused by mutations in the muscle chloride channel gene ( _CLCN1_ ). There are two forms of MC, one with autosomal dominant inheritance, known as Thomsen disease, and the other with recessive inheritance, known as Becker myotonia. Thomsen disease starts during infancy and myotonia is mainly localized in the facial and upper limb muscles. Becker myotonia usually starts in the second decade of life.<sup>302</sup> The myotonia in MC patients is usually initiated by a forceful muscle contraction, particularly after being at rest for at least 10 minutes. The myotonic muscle stiffness then becomes increasingly obvious after a second and third short, but forceful, contraction. Further contractions usually dampen the myotonia (the “warm- up” effect).<sup>302</sup>

It is estimated the prevalence is 1:23,000 for the Thomsen type and 1:50,000 for the Becker type. MC has a male sex dominance. Becker myotonia is 10 times more common than Thomsen myotonia.<sup>303</sup> The sarcolemmal chloride conductance is reduced due to the defect in the _CLCN1_ gene encoding the ClC- 1 chloride channel, resulting in both clinically and also electrically delayed relaxation of the hyperpolarized muscle.<sup>303</sup>

Thomsen disease was the first myotonic disease to be described. Patients may have a hypertrophic and athletic appearance. The sign of percussion myotonia is described as an indenting- appearing myotonia triggered by tapping the muscle. Lid lag is common and muscle stretch reflexes are normal.<sup>304</sup> Myotonia symptoms in Becker myotonia usually start at 10 to 14 years of age or even later and are more severe than those of Thomsen disease. Becker myotonia may be associated with severe generalized stiffness resulting in falling. It can frequently be misdiagnosed as epilepsy. EMG test displays myotonic discharges<sup>303</sup> ; and electroencephalogram (EEG) test could be applied for differential diagnosis.

The diagnosis of MC is based on typical clinical manifestation of myotonia, possible family history, EMG, and identification of _CLCN1_ variant via molecular genetic testing. Mexiletine improves stiffness and quality of life in these patients,<sup>305,306</sup> and it is considered the first- line drug for the treatment of non- dystrophic myotonic disorders.<sup>307,308</sup> Other sodium channel blockers such as lamotrigine or ranolazine can also be used.<sup>308</sup> Other medications such

as acetazolamide and botulinum toxin A have also been reported.<sup>308</sup>

### Anesthetic Considerations

MC patients are at increased risk for adverse anesthesiarelated events. Molecular genetic testing of at- risk family members is appropriate. Myotonia caused by disease, or drugs, anesthesia, and the operation environment are major concerns for anesthesiologists. Because shivering in the operating room can trigger the myotonic reaction, MC patients should be kept normothermic during surgery.<sup>304,309–311</sup>

There have been reports that MC patients are predisposed to MH, but without definite support.<sup>309–311</sup> However, depolarizing muscle relaxants can lead to severe masseter spasms in MC patients and should be avoided. Betaadrenergic agonists in high doses may aggravate myotonia. Propranolol has likewise been reported to worsen myotonia. Accordingly, β- agonists and β- antagonists should be used with caution.<sup>312</sup>

Generalized spasms involving the respiratory and skeletal muscles have been reported, which lead to difficult ventilation.<sup>309</sup> The findings resemble those of MH, so dantrolene is sometimes administered.<sup>313</sup> Because dantrolene is an inhibitor of calcium release from the SR, it can usually abolish the myotonia effectively.<sup>309,310</sup>

### MYOTUBULAR MYOPATHY

Myotubular myopathy (MTM) is pathologically defined by the presence of centrally placed nuclei in the majority of extrafusal muscle fibers, an appearance resembling fetal myotubes during normal muscle development. Consequently, MTM is also called centronuclear myopathy (CNM).<sup>314</sup> However, MTM now mostly refers to the X- linked form of the disease (XL- CNM), whereas CNM is used for the autosomal form.<sup>314</sup>

MTM and CNM are rare. The estimated incidence is 1 in 50,000 newborn males,<sup>314</sup> and MTM is the most common form. MTM has been linked to the myotubularin ( _MTM1_ ) gene on Xq28. Both autosomal recessive and autosomal dominant forms have been observed in CNM patients. It has been implicated that CNM is linked to _MTM1_ , myotubularinrelated protein ( _MTMR2_ ), and myotubularin- related phosphatase ( _MTMR3_ ) genes mutations.<sup>314</sup> Other mutations in _DNM2_ , _RYR1_ , _BIN_ , and _TTN_ have also been found in CNM cases.<sup>315</sup> MTM1, BIN1, and DNM2 proteins are involved in membrane remodeling (including T- tubule remodeling) and trafficking.<sup>316</sup> ECC relies on the tight control of SR Ca<sup>2+</sup> release via RyR1, which is directly activated by DHPR upon T- tubule depolarization. Defects in ECC and calcium homeostasis are the common cause for the observed muscle weakness in these patients.<sup>316</sup>

MTM is the most severe form of the disease.<sup>315</sup> Affected males typically have severe floppiness and weakness and respiratory distress at birth. The patient usually has a normal response to pain, but tendon reflexes are absent. Pregnancy is often complicated by polyhydramnios and reduced fetal movements. The long- term prognosis for MTM is extremely poor.<sup>304</sup> In patients who survive the first year of life, most are either completely or partially ventilator dependent.<sup>317</sup> These patients often have abnormal liver

<!-- PDF page 22 -->

function.<sup>317</sup> The cause of death is usually related to respiratory failure. Cardiac muscles are not generally involved. CNM is a relatively mild condition.<sup>318</sup> Its clinical features include respiratory distress, hypotonia, bulbar weakness, ophthalmoplegia, ptosis, and facial diplegia.<sup>315</sup>

Pathologically, MTM and CNM share a similar, characteristic histologic feature: predominantly type 1 fiber with centrally placed nuclei seen on hematoxylin–eosin staining of formalin- fixed, paraffin- embedded tissue.<sup>310</sup> Diagnosis could be confirmed through clinical findings, muscle biopsy, and identification of pathogenic gene mutation. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (i.e., edrophonium and pyridostigmine) have been proved by FDA to alleviate fatigability in these patients.<sup>316</sup> Clinical trials on gene therapies such as DNM2 reduction by antisense oligonucleotides and the repurposing use of tamoxifen are underway.<sup>316</sup>

### Anesthetic Considerations

Reports of anesthesia for patients with MTM are sparse.<sup>318–323</sup> Hepatobiliary disease and cholestasis are common comorbidities in MTM patients.<sup>316</sup> Patients could also receive tracheostomy or gastrostomy tube feeding during the course, as well as orthopedic procedures related to scoliosis, hip dislocations, club foot, or temporomandibular joint disease. Chronic respiratory failure often necessitates postoperative mechanical ventilation. Increases in ventilator requirements and surgical pressure may further decrease preload and increase afterload of the right ventricle, worsening right- sided cardiac output.<sup>323</sup>

Non- triggering general anesthesia has been used because of the unfounded concern of possible susceptibility to MH. Agents such as propofol, fentanyl, remifentanil, and nitrous oxide have been used successfully without adverse effects.<sup>318–324</sup> The possibility of a prolonged effect of nondepolarizing muscle relaxants has been suggested with mechanomyography.<sup>318–322,324</sup> However, in reality, intubation of such patients may not require any muscle relaxant (provided adequate hypnotic dosing) because of their hypotonic state.

## Endocrine and Metabolic Myopathies

### GLYCOGEN STORAGE DISEASE TYPE I

Glycogen storage disease type I (GSD I) is caused by deficiency of glucose 6- phosphatase enzyme activity or transport proteins of glucose 6- phosphate (G6P). Impaired glycogenolysis results in accumulation of glycogen and G6P in the liver, kidney, intestine, skeletal muscle, and heart and is manifested as hepatomegaly, renomegaly, proximal tubular dysfunction, and diarrhea.<sup>325</sup> Muscle GSDs present in one of two different ways: exercise intolerance and rhabdomyolysis or fixed muscle weakness without rhabdomyolysis.<sup>326</sup> GSD Ia (von Gierke disease) is due to a deficiency in G6P hydrolase (catalytic subunit) activity and accounts for more than 80% of cases. GSD Ib (G6P transporter deficiency), Ic, and Id represent allelic defects in the translocase associated with G6P.

GSD I is the second most common type of GSD.<sup>327,328</sup> The incidence of GSD I is approximately 1 in 100,000 live

newborns.<sup>328</sup> The incidence in non- Ashkenazi Jews from North Africa may be as high as 1 in 5420 people.<sup>328</sup> GSD I inheritance is autosomal recessive. The G6P gene ( _G6PC_ ) encoding the hydrolase resides at 17q21, with the gene encoding G6P translocase ( _G6PT_ ) located at 11q23. Mutations responsible for GSD I have been described in both type Ia and Ib patients.<sup>328</sup>

Fasting hypoglycemia is the initial manifestation of the disease. As a result, upregulation of the synthesis and transport of counter- regulatory hormones, such as glucagon, cortisol, catecholamines, and growth hormone, becomes obvious and leads to the release of pyruvate, lactate, and free fatty acid. Other common and characteristic endocrine symptoms of GSD I include lactic acidosis, hyperuricemia, and hypertriglyceridemia.<sup>329</sup> Lipid deposition in lean tissues such as the liver, skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, and pancreas results in lipotoxicity and organ failure, including pulmonary hypertension, steatohepatitis, end- stage renal disease, insulin resistance, cardiac contractile dysfunction, and pancreatic β- cell failure.<sup>328</sup> For type Ib disease, specific problems such as neutropenia and neutrophil dysfunction are prominent. Patients may have recurrent infections and inflammatory bowel disease.<sup>330</sup>

GSD I children may present with full cheeks, relatively thin extremities, short stature, and a protuberant abdomen. Symptoms of abnormal metabolism with blood glucose and lipids would be noticed prior to diagnosis. Diagnosis is made through identification of pathogenic variants in either _G6PC1_ (GSD Ia) or _SLC37A4_ (GSD Ib). High carbohydrates can help maintain normal blood glucose levels and prevent hypoglycemia. Lipid- lowering medication, angiotensinconverting enzyme inhibitors, allopurinol, and citrate supplementation may be needed.<sup>329</sup>

### Anesthetic Considerations

GSD I patients may receive liver tumor radiofrequency ablation or liver transplantation, or surgeries dealing with urolithiasis, or renal transplantation. Other complications such as seizures, hypertension, hypothyroidism, pancreatitis, enterocolitis, bowel ulcers, impaired platelet function, and neutropenia should be evaluated before surgery.<sup>329</sup>

Anesthesia case reports for GSD I patients are rare.<sup>331,332</sup> Patients with GSD I may have a high risk of hemorrhage due to platelet dysfunction. Patients also should be given intravenous glucose- containing fluid preoperatively when they have fasted. Lactate- containing solutions should be avoided because these patients are not able to convert the lactic acid to glycogen.<sup>333</sup> Patients need to have their blood glucose levels monitored frequently throughout the perioperative period to avoid hypoglycemia.

### GLYCOGEN STORAGE DISEASE TYPE II (ACID MALTASE DEFICIENCY)

GSD II is also known as acid maltase deficiency (AMD). Deficiency results from impaired lysosomal acid α- glucosidase (GAA) function and accumulation of lysosomal glycogen in skeletal, respiratory, and cardiac muscle.<sup>326</sup> AMD can present anytime from early infancy into adulthood. Infantile AMD, also known as Pompe disease, is the most severe condition, being the classic infantile- onset form. It is usually manifested within the first few months of life as rapidly

<!-- PDF page 23 -->

progressive weakness and hypotonia and enlargement of the tongue, heart, and liver. Proximal myopathy with diaphragmatic weakness is seen in late- onset disease.<sup>326</sup>

The incidence of AMD is estimated to be 1 in every 14,000 to 40,000 births. Its inheritance is autosomal recessive with a few exceptions.<sup>334,335</sup> Mutations of the acid maltase gene on chromosome 17q25 cause deficiency of lysosomal acid maltase (acid α- 1,4- glucosidase).<sup>335</sup> Cases of AMD have been arbitrarily classified into three groups – infantile, childhood, and adult – according to the age at onset or death, rate of progression, and tissue–organ involvement.<sup>335</sup> Acid maltase is a lysosomal enzyme that catalyzes the one- way hydrogenation of glycogen to G6P and is found in all tissues, including skeletal and cardiac muscle.<sup>336</sup> Consequently, glycogen accumulates within the muscle tissues of maltase- deficient patients.

Untreated AMD patients are characterized by severe hypotonia, macroglossia, muscle and diaphragmatic weakness, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with cardiomegaly with/ without Wolff–Parkinson–White arrhythmias, and hepatomegaly with fatality from cardiac failure within the first year of life.<sup>335–338</sup> Massive amounts of glycogen (8%–15% of the wet weight of the tissue) are seen accumulating in the heart, liver, and skeletal muscle, with relatively smaller deposits in smooth muscle, eyes, kidneys, endothelial cells, lymphocytes, brain, and spinal cord. Echocardiography demonstrates marked thickening of the interventricular septum and posterior left ventricular wall, left ventricular outflow obstruction, and trabecular hypertrophy.<sup>334</sup> Ventricular wall thickness may be increased to up to 25 mm.<sup>337</sup> Death usually results from cardiorespiratory decompensation within several years of disease progression.<sup>335,339</sup> Childhood AMD occurs in infancy to early childhood and is manifested by clinical signs of myopathy. Respiratory muscles tend to be selectively affected. Calf enlargement can also occur. The disease progresses relatively slowly in this form, with a few patients surviving beyond the second decade of life.<sup>335</sup> However, involvement of vascular smooth muscle is more severe than in the infantile form. There has been a report of extensive glycogen deposition in the arterial wall causing basilar aneurysms.<sup>340</sup> On the other hand, adult AMD usually occurs after age 20 and is characterized by slow progressive myopathy or symptoms of respiratory failure.<sup>335</sup> The weakness in proximal muscles is more prominent than the weakness in distal muscles. A third of adult AMD patients suffer from respiratory failure with a restrictive pattern. Weakness in the diaphragm causes extensive atelectasis. VC may be significantly reduced.<sup>335</sup>

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and shortened PR interval and high QRS complexes in ECG, and elevated CK and blood aminotransferases are characteristic of the disease. GAA enzyme activity is usually absent (in infantile–onset disease) or decreased (in late- onset disease). Diagnostic confirmation is made by analysis of biallelic _GAA_ gene.<sup>326</sup> Enzyme replacement therapy using human recombinant GAA is approved in the US and Europe.

### Anesthetic Considerations

Cardiomyopathy and cardiac failure are present in many patients. Glycogen accumulation can also occur in the cardiac conduction system, leading to ventricular and supraventricular tachycardia.<sup>341</sup> Subendocardial ischemia may

occur in patients with a thickened ventricular wall and results in higher left ventricular end- diastolic pressure at lower ventricular volume.<sup>342,343</sup> Close cardiac monitoring is therefore necessary. A central venous or pulmonary artery catheter is not essential in patients who are normovolemic without preexisting heart failure.<sup>342</sup> Adequate filling pressure and normal to high systemic vascular resistance (SVR) are required to ensure effective coronary perfusion.<sup>342</sup> Isolated intraoperative cardiac arrest during halothane anesthesia in infantile AMD has been documented.<sup>344</sup> Enflurane<sup>345</sup> and sevoflurane<sup>341,342</sup> have been used without complications. Theoretically, total intravenous general anesthesia with propofol may cause a reduction in afterload leading to an increased risk for myocardial ischemia. This may become significant when the patient is tachycardic.<sup>342,346</sup> Ketamine and etomidate are advised as they better support coronary perfusion pressure.<sup>342</sup> The use of local anesthetic technique should also be considered in children with severe cardiomyopathy.<sup>347,348</sup>

Respiratory failure and muscle weakness are other concerns for anesthesiologists. Deterioration of diaphragmatic function leads to postoperative acute respiratory failure.<sup>349</sup> Chest imaging, restrictive pattern in pulmonary function test, and diaphragm ultrasound are used to evaluate these patients.<sup>349</sup> A spectrum of uses of muscle relaxants from none<sup>345</sup> to atracurium<sup>350</sup> to rocuronium<sup>342</sup> have been attempted. Low- dose rocuronium, 0.5 mg/kg, with close monitoring of neuromuscular function and adequate use of reversal agents (e.g. sugammadex), has been sufficient to prevent prolonged postoperative weakness.<sup>342</sup> Depolarizing agents should be avoided because of the potential risk of hyperkalemia and rhabdomyolysis.<sup>342,344</sup>

## Mitochondrial Diseases and Mitochondrial Myopathies

_Mitochondrial diseases_ refer to defects in all of the five main steps of mitochondrial metabolism: substrate transport, substrate utilization, the Krebs cycle, the electron transport chain, and oxidation–phosphorylation coupling.<sup>351</sup> The respiratory chain is composed of five multimeric complexes (I–V) embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, plus two small mobile electron carriers, coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) and cytochrome _c_ , for a total of more than 80 proteins,<sup>351</sup> among which 13 are encoded by mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and all others by nuclear DNA (nDNA).

mtDNA is different from nDNA in several aspects: (1) mtDNA is circular and contains no intron, (2) it has larger numbers of copies than nDNA does and a much higher spontaneous mutation rate, and (3) its inheritance is maternal. Primary mtDNA mutations may include point mutations in polypeptide, tRNA, or rRNA encoding regions and largescale rearrangements, duplications, or deletions.<sup>352</sup> nDNA mutations can cause deficiencies in complexes I to IV and CoQ10 of the electron transport chain.<sup>351</sup> Since mitochondria are ubiquitous, every tissue in the body can be affected by mtDNA mutations.

Among all the mitochondrial functions, abnormalities in electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation are the most common causes of mitochondrial myopathies and mitochondrial encephalomyopathies.<sup>353</sup> Some

<!-- PDF page 24 -->

of the common conditions caused by mtDNA point mutations include myoclonic epilepsy with ragged red fibers (MERRF); mitochondrial encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke- like episodes (MELAS); neuropathy, ataxia, and retinitis pigmentosa (NARP); maternally inherited Leigh syndrome; and Leber hereditary optic neuropathy.<sup>351</sup> Sporadic large- scale mutations may lead to Kearns–Sayre syndrome, progressive external ophthalmoplegia, and Pearson syndrome.<sup>351</sup>

Mitochondrial myopathies are characterized by proximal muscle weakness. Fatigue and poor stamina are prominent clinical features. Movement disorders such as ataxia, dystonia, myoclonus, chorea, athetosis, and tremors have also been described.<sup>354</sup> Common laboratory findings include a very high lactate to pyruvate ratio (50–250:1 instead of a normal ratio of &lt;25:1), increased blood levels of free carnitine, and occasionally low levels of folate (for instance in Kearns–Sayre syndrome). The hallmark of mitochondrial myopathies is the “ragged red fiber” when muscle biopsy specimens are stained with modified Gomori trichrome stain.<sup>355</sup> As for mitochondrial encephalomyopathies, patients with MELAS demonstrate basal ganglia calcifications and stroke- like patterns not confined to vascular territories.<sup>355</sup> Clinical features of two relatively common encephalopathies, MELAS and MERRF, are briefly discussed below.

### MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY, ENCEPHALOPATHY, LACTIC ACIDOSIS, AND STROKE- LIKE EPISODES (MELAS)

MELAS is the most common mitochondrial encephalomyopathy. Patients usually have migraine- like symptoms with hyperlactic acidosis, epilepsy, and stroke- like episodes.<sup>356,357</sup> The most typical feature of MELAS are spontaneously reversible “stroke- like” neurologic symptoms; however, the pattern of the lesions is inconsistent with vascular distribution.<sup>356,357</sup> Seizures are common and strokelike episodes may produce hemiparesis, hemianopia, and cortical blindness.

The prevalence of MELAS is 3.5 in 100,000.<sup>356,357</sup> In MELAS, the activity of complex I nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide- coenzyme Q reductase and complex IV cytochrome c oxidase are decreased. These impairments will reduce the production of ATP. Organs with high dependency on ATP produced by mitochondria, such as the skeleton muscle and central nervous system, are mostly affected. Mutation in the _MTTL1_ gene encoding the mitochondrial tRNA- Leu is most commonly associated with MELAS, which affects the taurine modification of tRNA and subsequently impairs protein synthesis.<sup>357</sup>

Onset of MELAS is most commonly before age 20. Associated findings include diabetes mellitus, hearing loss, pituitary and thyroid hypofunction, and lack of secondary sexual characteristics. In its full expression, MELAS leads to dementia, a bedridden state, and death.

Any patient with a stroke below the age of 40 years should be worked up for MELAS. Patients may present with repeated spontaneous suspected acute ischemic stroke and seizures. Brain MRI of patients with MELAS typically demonstrates multifocal, symmetric, infarct- like lesions.<sup>356</sup> Elevated levels of pyruvic and lactic acids could be detected.

A genetic test confirming mutation in the mtDNA leads to a definitive diagnosis.<sup>357</sup> Supplementation with L- arginine or taurine has been proposed as preventive treatments for stroke- like episodes.

### MYOCLONIC EPILEPSY WITH RAGGED RED FIBERS (MERRF)

The prevalence of MERRF is approximately 1:100,000. It is a multisystem disorder characterized by predominant progressive myoclonus, generalized epilepsy, ataxia, and ragged red fibers on muscle biopsy. Clinical presentation of MERRF is variable and proportional to normal and abnormal mtDNA in each tissue.<sup>358</sup> An A- to- G mutation at nucleotide 8344 of the mtDNA lysine tRNA gene ( _MT- TK_ ) is presented in about 80% of MERRF patients.<sup>358</sup> Other clinical features may include hearing loss, peripheral neuropathy, optic atrophy, dementia, short stature, and exercise intolerance.<sup>351</sup>

Onset of MERRF is usually in childhood. Photosensitive myoclonus may be intermittent or continuous, which could be the manifestation of seizure activity and the characteristic feature of MERRF. Cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias, such as Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome, and ventricular dysfunction could lead to cardiac failure and sudden death.

The lactic acid levels are typically increased in both blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with MERRF patients. A raised creatine kinase (CK) and EMG should demonstrate myopathy. EEG shows generalized spike and wave discharges with background slowing. The typical red ragged fibers on muscle biopsy are present in more than 90% of patients with MERRF.<sup>124</sup> Genetic testing could confirm the diagnosis. Supportive therapy with coenzyme Q10, vitamin B complex supplementation, and L- carnitine are often used.

### Anesthetic Considerations

The anesthesiologist may be involved in the care of patients with mitochondrial disease in multiple situations – often in the setting of obtaining a muscle biopsy in a child with an undiagnosed myopathy.<sup>359</sup> These patients may also present for surgery for procedures related to the disease, such as implantation of a permanent pacemaker in a patient with Kearns–Sayre syndrome,<sup>360</sup> as well as in the labor and delivery suite.<sup>361</sup>

Patients need a comprehensive preoperative evaluation with a particular focus on neurologic, cardiac, respiratory, musculoskeletal, endocrinopathic, and metabolic compromise. Patients should be evaluated for signs and symptoms of cardiomyopathy or conduction defects. Laboratory studies consisting of glucose, electrolytes with anion gap, complete blood count, blood urea nitrogen, lactate, pyruvate, ammonia, CK, biotinidase, acyl carnitines, and blood and urine amino and organic acids could be used as initial investigation for suspected mitochondrial disorders. Further workup should include an erythrocyte sedimentation rate, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), liver and renal profiles, thyroid function tests, arterial blood gas, and urinalysis.<sup>354,362</sup> Multidisciplinary consultation with neurologic, ophthalmologic, cardiologic, and endocrinologic specialists may be required.<sup>354</sup>

## Periodic Paralyses

The periodic paralyses are a group of disorders that are characterized by alterations of function in the voltage- gated ion channels.<sup>394</sup> Symptoms of the particular channelopathy depend on the specific ion channel involved and can be divided into three broad categories: (1) chloride channelopathies (myotonia without paralysis e.g., MC, see earlier); (2) sodium channelopathies (myotonia with paralysis, such as hyperkalemic periodic paralysis [HyperPP]); and (3) other cation channelopathies (paralysis without myotonia, such as hypokalemic periodic paralysis [HypoPP]).<sup>395</sup>

### HYPERKALEMIC PERIODIC PARALYSIS

HyperPP is characterized by attacks of flaccid weakness associated with increased serum potassium.<sup>396</sup> It is a disorder with a prevalence of 0.17 per 100,000<sup>397</sup> and is caused

<!-- PDF page 26 -->

by abnormal voltage- gated sodium channel in mature muscle fibers due to _SCN4A_ gene mutation. It is an autosomal dominant disorder first described by Tyler and associates in 1951.<sup>398</sup> The pathogenesis of HyperPP involves mutations in _SCN4A_ , encoding the voltage- gated sodium channel NaV1.4; and such mutations lead to pathologically increased sodium current and an increased tendency of the muscle fiber to become depolarized.<sup>395,398</sup> Influx of sodium into the muscle is accompanied by simultaneous efflux of potassium and hyperkalemia. Sustained sodium currents lead to prolonged membrane depolarization, which will then cause myotonia followed by membrane desensitization and will eventually result in paralysis. The typical clinical manifestation of HyperPP is paralysis that lasts for 15 minutes to an hour, with decreased tendon reflexes. In the interictal state, HyperPP is usually associated with mild myotonia that does not impede voluntary movements.<sup>398</sup> Precipitating factors include potassium-rich foods, and rest after exercise. In addition, a cold environment, emotional stress, fasting, glucocorticoids, and pregnancy can provoke or worsen the attacks.

In HyperPP patients, serum CK can be elevated, sometimes 5 to 10 times above the normal limit, whereas serum sodium and potassium levels are normal in the interictal state.<sup>398</sup> EMG recordings will often show myotonic discharges during and between attacks. Muscle biopsies may show small peripheral vacuoles in the sarcoplasm. Family history can also help with diagnosis and further screening of the disease. Genetic testing with the SCN4 ionic channel gene mutation is gold standard for the diagnosis of HyperPP, but up to 20% of the cases would be negative for the gene mutation.<sup>397</sup> There is only symptomatic treatment for HyperPP. Treatment includes medication with acetazolamide and mexiletine. Behavioral modification, such as avoiding potassium- containing foods, strenuous exercise, fasting, and exposure to cold, are also important.<sup>398</sup>

### Anesthetic Considerations

Preparation for elective surgery is crucial. Neurologic investigation could rule out myotonia. As for muscle symptoms, potassium, cholinesterase inhibitors, and depolarizing muscle relaxants will aggravate the myotonia in HyperPP patients.<sup>398</sup> Prolonged muscle weakness has been reported when succinylcholine is used.<sup>399</sup> Although one- third of patients had no signs of myotonia,<sup>400</sup> masseter spasm and respiratory and skeletal muscles stiffness could still occur during intubation, ventilation, and postoperatively.<sup>398</sup> Therefore succinylcholine and anticholinesterase such as neostigmine should be contraindicated in HyperPP patients. Postoperatively, HyperPP patients may remain paralyzed for up to several hours. Ideally, all patients with HyperPP need to be admitted preoperatively so that proper preoperative fasting can be accompanied by the administration of dextrose- containing potassium- free maintenance fluid.<sup>401</sup> Preventive measures such as maintaining normal body temperature and low serum potassium levels and avoiding hypoglycemia are helpful.<sup>402</sup> Early recognition of hyperkalemia on ECG could be helpful. Close monitoring of the patient’s acid–base status and serum potassium levels is also recommended.<sup>403</sup> Although patients with sodium channel pathology have often been considered to be susceptible to MH, there

is no increased risk for MH in HyperPP patients.<sup>404</sup> General anesthesia has been shown to have satisfactory outcomes.<sup>399,401,402,405,406</sup> The safe use of both volatile agents and propofol has been documented.<sup>401,402,407</sup> Nondepolarizing muscle relaxants may be safely used.<sup>401,402</sup> Regional techniques may also be appropriate.<sup>400,405,406</sup>

### NORMOKALEMIC PERIODIC PARALYSIS

Recent studies supported that normokalemic periodic paralysis (NormoKPP) is a variant of the HyperPP, not a distinct disease, because all the subjects exhibited clinical and laboratory features of the HyperPP.<sup>408–410</sup> It features paroxysmal muscle weakness without changes in serum potassium level.<sup>411</sup> NormoKPP is caused by mutations of the same gene, _SCN4A_ , which is why it is suspected that it is a subtype of HyperPP.

### HYPOKALEMIC PERIODIC PARALYSIS

HypoPP is characterized by a decrease in potassium levels in blood. It is important to remain aware of the differences in the clinical features between HyperPP and HypoPP. HypoPP is not associated with myotonia, a spontaneous attack is associated with hypokalemia (a diagnostic criterion), potassium is a remedy, and glucose triggers an attack.<sup>398</sup> Mutations in the skeletal muscle voltage- gated calcium channel encoded by _CACNA1S_ (HypoPP type 1) and _SCN4A_ (HypoPP type 2) have been identified.<sup>297</sup>

HypoPP is an autosomal dominant disease with higher penetration in males. The disease is due to loss of function of one of the two different ion channel types: CaV1.1 and NaV1.4.<sup>396</sup> Genetic abnormality in L- type calcium channel (CaV1.1) makes muscle sarcolemmas inexcitable, leading to flaccid paralysis.<sup>412</sup> Since many cases of HypoPP are genetically undetermined, it is still unclear the exact mechanisms that lead to hypokalemic paralysis.

The severity of attacks in HypoPP patients is usually greater than those occurring in patients with HyperPP. The most common muscle groups affected are those in the arms and legs; however, the disorder can also affect swallowing and respiratory muscles, which can be fatal in severe cases. Attacks of HypoPP can be triggered by rigorous exercise, stress, high- carbohydrate or high- salt meals, pregnancy, menstruation, hypothermia, or drugs such as insulin.<sup>413,414</sup>

The diagnosis of HypoPP is made by laboratory tests demonstrating hypokalemia during attacks and normokalemia between attacks. EMG does not usually show myotonia.<sup>396</sup> Treatment is centered on the identification and avoidance of triggers. Potassium administration can be useful in the treatment of an acute attack. The prophylactic medication of choice in HypoPP type 1 is acetazolamide<sup>415</sup> ; however, acetazolamide can worsen symptoms in type 2 HypoPP.<sup>416</sup> Such patients may respond to potassium- sparing diuretics such as spironolactone.<sup>416,417</sup> Dichlorphenamide is the only FDA- approved treatment, which is a much more potent carbonic anhydrase inhibitor than acetazolamide.

Clinically resembling hereditary or familial HypoPP, thyrotoxic periodic paralysis (TPP) occurs later in life than HypoPP. Presentation of TPP is characterized by recurrent episodes of weakness with profound hypokalemia.<sup>418</sup> It has a strong male predominance, and is much more common

<!-- PDF page 27 -->

in patients of oriental descent. Loss-of-function mutations in the inward rectifying potassium channel (Kir2.6) may be involved in some cases of TPP.<sup>419</sup> Overactivity of the sodium–potassium ATPase (Na/K pump) with potassium influx and repolarization of muscle cells is considered as the pathophysiology of the disease. The primary activators of the Na/K pump include β- adrenergic agonists (primarily epinephrine) and insulin. Hyperstimulation of the pump can be caused by elevated thyroid hormones.<sup>418</sup> Severity is directly related to the underlying hypokalemia, but not to the underlying hyperthyroidism.<sup>418</sup> However, the condition responds to antithyroid medications such as methimazole.<sup>398</sup> Nonselective beta blockers, such as propranolol, are considered first- line treatment. Note that administration of large amounts of exogenous potassium places patients at risk of dysrhythmias.<sup>418</sup>

### Anesthetic Considerations

Management of HypoPP patients should focus on avoiding triggers and medications causing a shift of potassium. Triggers, such as the cold environment, use of saline and dextrose during surgery, and stress may cause muscle weakness. Potassium monitoring is crucial for these patients perioperatively.

Patients with HypoPP undergoing general anesthesia have been reported to exhibit weakness and respiratory distress in the postoperative period.<sup>420,421</sup> General anesthesia, postoperative stress, glucose- containing intravenous solutions, and long- acting neuromuscular blockers are associated with postoperative paralytic events.<sup>413</sup> While safe use of intermediate- and medium- acting nondepolarizing muscle relaxants, such as atracurium and mivacurium, have been documented,<sup>422–424</sup> long- acting muscle relaxants should not be used.<sup>413</sup>

Epidural analgesia has been shown to reduce both pain- related hyperventilation and serum catecholamines, thereby minimizing changes in serum potassium levels.<sup>413</sup> The sympathomimetic effect of epinephrine- containing local anesthetics may also precipitate hypokalemia.<sup>413</sup>

Unlike HyperPP, an association between HypoPP and MH cannot be categorically ruled out,<sup>297</sup> although the use of isoflurane in HypoPP has been reported.<sup>424</sup> Theoretical association of HypoPP to MH was made because a few MH patients were known to have mutations in _CACNA1S_ .<sup>297</sup> However, it is generally accepted that the risk of susceptibility of HypoPP patients to MH is similar to that of the general population. There are a number of reports of MH- like metabolic crises in patients with HypoPP.<sup>425–427</sup> Contracture- like responses to succinylcholine have also been described.<sup>428</sup> In one of the above descriptions, it seemed likely that there were two unrelated mutations conferring both HypoPP and sensitivity to MH in the same patient.<sup>427</sup> Therefore while the probability of MH in a given patient with HypoPP is likely to be remote, it cannot be ruled out, and the safest course might be the use of a non- triggering anesthetic. If volatile agents are used, they should be used with extra vigilance.<sup>297</sup>

## Acquired Neuromuscular Diseases

Acquired neuromuscular diseases can be triggered by infections, drugs, tumors, autoimmune diseases, malnutrition,

etc. Neurons, myelin sheath, axons, nerve terminals, neuromuscular joints, and muscles can be attacked by endotoxin or neurotoxin, or autoantibodies and inflammatory factors. When perioperative patients have suspicious symptoms, it is necessary to be alert to neuromuscular abnormalities, which can be diagnosed according to the etiology and EMG manifestations. Anesthesiologists should pay attention to two points: (1) to find out the cause of acquired neuromuscular disease and consider the systemic effects of the disease; (2) to clarify the anesthesia precautions for neuromuscular disease. Invasive manipulation near the nerve, surgical stress as well as muscle relaxants, may aggravate the condition. Anesthesia should be performed weighing the risks and benefits of surgery and anesthesia.

### CENTRAL NEUROPATHIES

### Multiple Sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic neuroinflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS) that is characterized by pathological demyelination and neurodegeneration with axonal and neuronal loss.<sup>429,430</sup> MS typically affects adults between 20 and 30 years of age, with a female- tomale ratio of 3:1. Major genetic and environmental risk factors include human leukocyte antigen- DRB1 ( _HLA- DRB1_ ) gene in the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) locus, vitamin D deficiency, ambient UV radiation, Epstein–Barr virus infection, and smoking.<sup>429,431</sup>

The common symptoms of MS include monocular optic neuritis; partial myelitis with limb weakness, and ataxia; sensory disturbances with paresthesia, hypesthesia, or pain; and brainstem syndromes. Notably, MS is also associated with impaired autonomic function.<sup>432</sup> In addition to clinical findings, MRI is essential for diagnosis with demonstration of dissemination of MS disease characteristics in space and time. Abnormal evoked potentials to assess function in afferent or efferent CNS pathways, and specific oligoclonal bands in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), are commonly detected in patients with MS.<sup>433</sup>

Three main clinical types of MS have been defined based on the clinical presentation. The classic relapsing–remitting form of MS (RMS) is characterized by the onset of recurring clinical symptoms followed by partial or complete recovery. Secondary progressive multiple sclerosis (SPMS) always starts with RMS. Its disease course is progressive deterioration over 10–15 years in up to 50% of untreated patients. By contrast, primary progressive MS (PPMS), which accounts for about 10%–15% of patients with MS, manifests as persistent disease progression from its onset.<sup>434</sup>

The initial pathological finding of MS is the focal white matter demyelination, which is characterized by the perivascular infiltration of immune cells.<sup>435,436</sup> As disease progresses, chronic mixed active–inactive lesions become more dominant.<sup>435</sup> Neurodegeneration with cumulative axonal and neuronal loss leads to irreversible neurologic deficits. The contribution of aberrant immune cell interactions to relapsing MS is supported by the success of immune therapies to target activated peripheral T-cells, myeloid cells, and B-cells that infiltrate into the CNS through the leaked blood–brain barrier (BBB).<sup>437</sup> In contrast, non- relapsing PMS involves degenerative mechanisms and inflammatory

<!-- PDF page 28 -->

responses of both infiltrated immune cells and resident CNS glial cells.<sup>438,439</sup>

A multidisciplinary approach is the mainstay of current treatment for MS, including disease- modifying therapies (DMTs) to target peripheral immune cells, symptomatic treatment, psychological support, and rehabilitation _._ Systemic steroids are commonly used to manage either first attacks or acute exacerbations. Initiating DMTs in the early disease course has been shown to reduce long- term neurologic disability in retrospective observational studies.<sup>440</sup> Since anti- inflammatory IFN- β was approved in 1993, there has been considerable progress in the development of DMTs for RMS.<sup>437</sup> Oral fingolimod is an analogue of sphingosine 1- phosphate (S1P) that prevents T- cell migration. Teriflunomide, an active metabolite of leflunomide, inhibits the proliferation of B- and T- cells. Cladribine induces apoptosis in actively proliferating and resting lymphocyte populations. Natalizumab is the first monoclonal antibody approved for treating MS. It binds to α4 integrin of lymphocytes to prevent lymphocytes from crossing the BBB. Alemtuzumab targets against CD52, a receptor on lymphocytes and monocytic cells. CD20- binding antibodies such as rituximab and ocrelizumab deplete mature B- cells, which can activate proinflammatory T- cells. Recently, ocrelizumab has become the first drug licensed for treating primary PMS. However, the therapeutic options for PMS remain limited. Notably, therapies aimed at targeting progressive injury and CNS inflammation within the CNS are emerging.<sup>441</sup> In addition, patients with MS often receive symptomatic treatments for spasticity, tremor, and mood disorders.

**Anesthetic Considerations.** Although patients with MS are sensitive to physical and emotional stress, a recent retrospective study concluded that postoperative relapse risk in adult patients with MS is not increased after surgery and anesthesia exposure.<sup>442</sup> However, patients with MS should be informed of a potential risk for relapse during the postoperative period. Preoperative evaluation is important to document existing neural deficits and assess respiratory dysfunction. In general, MS medications should be continued during the perioperative period.

As heat may trigger MS relapse by blocking conduction in demyelinated axons, body temperature should be closely monitored during the perioperative period. Intravenous induction agents and volatile anesthetics have been used safely. Given that MS can lead to autonomic dysfunction,<sup>432</sup> great care must be exercised to maintain fluid homeostasis and minimize exaggerated hemodynamic response to anesthetics, vasodilators, and sympathomimetic drugs.

Upregulation of nAChRs by MS- induced denervation or disuse muscle atrophy may lead to succinylcholine- induced hyperkalemia<sup>443</sup> and fatal cardiac arrest.<sup>444</sup> Therefore, it is wise to avoid depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs). In contrast, nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers are safe to use but should be dosed cautiously as both prolonged responses (increased sensitivity in patients with preexisting muscle weakness) and resistance to these NMBAs have been reported. The use of rocuronium with sugammadex has been suggested as a safe alternative to ensure a rapid and full reversal.<sup>445,446</sup> Administration of any neuromuscular blocking agent should be guided by objective neuromuscular monitoring.

It is speculated that the demyelination associated with MS renders the spinal cord susceptible to the neurotoxic effects of the local anesthetics. The safety of epidural anesthesia and analgesia in MS patients has been studied in the obstetric population. Epidural application of low concentrations of local anesthetics is safe in MS patients.<sup>447</sup> A subsequent large prospective study of the natural history of MS in pregnant women supported that epidural analgesia had no impact on relapse during the first three months postpartum in women with MS.<sup>448</sup> Spinal anesthesia, on the other hand, has been implicated in postoperative exacerbations of MS.<sup>449</sup> As the BBB may also be compromised in MS, spinal anesthesia is usually not recommended. However, a recent retrospective cohort study found that the postpartum relapse rate did not differ between epidural analgesia/anesthesia, spinal anesthesia, and no neuraxial analgesia/anesthesia.<sup>450</sup> Moreover, no exacerbation or deterioration of symptoms in patients with MS after spinal anesthesia was noted in another retrospective study.<sup>451</sup> In contrast, peripheral nerve block has been generally considered safe. However, reducing the local anesthetic concentration is certainly justifiable for both peripheral and neuraxial block.

The need for postoperative care is dependent on the preoperative symptoms, type of surgery, and status of the patient at the end of the surgical procedure. In this context, MS patients with severe weakness and respiratory distress, including pharyngeal dysfunction, may need extended postoperative care, such as noninvasive respiratory support and intense physiotherapy, to avoid further impairment of their pulmonary function (Box 31.7).

As the most common cause of acute flaccid paralysis worldwide,<sup>452</sup> GBS typically presents as an ascending paralysis characterized by symmetric weakness that can vary from mild difficulty with walking to nearly complete paralysis of all extremities, facial, respiratory, and bulbar muscles. Mild variants can present with ataxia, ophthalmoplegia, or hyporeflexia without significant appendicular weakness. Fulminant cases can present with severe ascending weakness leading to complete tetraplegia, and paralysis of cranial nerves and phrenic and intercostal nerves with facial and respiratory muscle weakness necessitating tracheostomy and ventilatory support. Importantly, patients may also have autonomic dysfunction that could lead to hemodynamic instability and cardiac arrhythmias with an increased risk for sudden circulatory collapse and fatal cardiac arrest. Notably, up to two- thirds of the deaths during the recovery are caused by cardiovascular and respiratory dysfunction.<sup>458</sup> Pain is another common feature of GBS and at least one- third of patients have chronic pain one year after disease onset.<sup>458,459</sup> Acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculopathy (AIDP) is the most common subtype of GBS.

Diagnosis of GBS is based on clinical features, supported by neurologic examination, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, and electrophysiologic studies.<sup>458</sup> CSF analysis may show a typical increase in CSF protein in combination with a normal cell count, which is a classic sign of the disease. Management requires a multidisciplinary approach including immunotherapy, nutritional, and respiratory support.<sup>458</sup> Considerable evidence supports that intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and plasma exchange are equally effective for all subtypes of GBS to accelerate recovery and improve outcome. However, neither disease progression nor the extent of nerve damage is altered.<sup>452</sup> Importantly, IVIG and plasma exchange are not contraindicated during pregnancy.

### Critical Illness Polyneuropathy and Critical Illness Myopathy

Critical illness polyneuropathy (CIP) and critical illness myopathy (CIM) are the most common neuromuscular impairment in critically ill patients.<sup>464</sup> Despite earlier reports of a rapid development of weakness, muscle atrophy, and polyneuropathy in critically ill patients, it was not until the 1987 report by Bolton and associates that the characteristic widespread axonal degeneration of motor and sensory fibers and the extensive denervation atrophy of limb and respiratory muscle associated with this polyneuropathy were clearly identified.<sup>465</sup>

Although the true incidence is difficult to determine, CIP and CIM are believed to affect up to 50% of all patients remaining in the intensive care unit for more than 2 weeks.<sup>466</sup> CIP is a sensory–motor axonal polyneuropathy, primarily involving distal axonal degeneration of motor and sensory fibers. CIM is an acute primary myopathy with loss of thick myosin filaments, muscle fiber atrophy and necrosis, and sodium channel dysfunction.<sup>464</sup>

### NEUROMUSCULAR JUNCTION DISEASES

### Myasthenia Gravis

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is the most common disorder of neuromuscular transmission, with an annual incidence of 7 to 23 new cases per million.<sup>473,474</sup> It is an autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction characterized by fluctuating weakness and fatigability of the ocular, bulbar, limb, and respiratory muscles. Autoantibodies against the muscle- type nAChRs block ACh binding and cross- link the receptors, resulting in increased internalization and degradation. Bound antibodies can also activate

complement- mediated lysis of the AChR- containing membranes. Autoantibodies are present in 80%–90% of patients but may vary in their light- chain or subclass type. Antibodies directed against muscle- specific receptor tyrosine kinase (MuSK) have also been identified in some antiAChR seronegative MG patients. Although MG can occur at any age, it is more frequently seen in young women (in the second or third decade) and older men (in the sixth to eighth decade). Many reports suggest an association with other autoimmune disorders such as autoimmune thyroid disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, and rheumatoid arthritis. Moreover, there is an association between MG and thymic abnormalities, with more than 70% of MG patients having thymus hyperplasia and 10% having thymomas.<sup>475</sup> MG may also be seen as a part of a paraneoplastic syndrome.

Typically, patients first report bulbar symptoms consisting of diplopia and ptosis. Oropharyngeal muscle weakness produces dysarthria and dysphagia. Loss of pharyngeal coordination of swallowing and palatal weakness can lead to nasal regurgitation and an increased frequency of aspiration of oral or gastric content. The bulbar symptoms are often followed by unevenly distributed muscle weakness and fatigue of the extremities and face. The muscle weakness is often exacerbated during exercise and throughout the course of the day. Apart from the patchy distribution of muscle weakness, symptoms may also vary from day to day, and there may be periods of remission of varying durations. Involvement of the respiratory muscles is of particular importance as it can lead to respiratory insufficiency or failure, requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation. Although noninvasive ventilation may be effective in the treatment of respiratory failure, the threshold for elective intubation is generally low in patients with MG, particularly if serial measurements demonstrate vital capacity &lt;15–20 mL/kg or maximal inspiration pressure (MIP) less than −25 to −30 cmH2O.<sup>476,477</sup>

The diagnosis of MG is focused on confirming the history and neurologic examination findings. Testing of the tendency to fatigue and exhibiting increased weakness during exercise or repeated contractions is important. For most patients, the diagnosis may be confirmed by the presence of autoantibodies against AChR or against other muscle receptor- associated proteins. Electrodiagnostic testing that shows evidence of impaired signal transmission at the neuromuscular junction is used for seronegative patients. A classic decrement in the compound muscle action potential is present after repetitive nerve stimulation. The diagnosis can also be confirmed through the administration of pharmacologic agents to potentiate signal transmission of Ach. The Edrophonium or Tensilon test has, however, fallen out of use due to suboptimal sensitivity and specificity and the associated risks for adverse reactions.

**Anesthetic Considerations.** Neurologic consultation should be considered in all patients with MG, in addition to preoperative evaluation of the patient’s respiratory, bulbar, and neurologic function to optimize drug therapy and prepare for postoperative care. Generally, patients should keep taking their anticholinesterase medication and be informed about the possibility of postoperative ventilator support.

Immunomodulating therapies are unlikely to cause clinically relevant interaction with neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) but can be held on the morning of surgery, given their long duration of effect. Pulmonary function testing should be considered to better assess preoperative optimization potentials and plan for the level of postoperative care. Although literature has failed to establish clear predictors of postoperative myasthenic crisis and ventilation needs, a preoperative vital capacity &lt;2 L is thought to be associated with a higher risk of intensive care unit admission. Other risk factors include pyridostigmine dose >750 mg, preoperative bulbar symptoms, high anti- AChR antibody titers, prior history of myasthenic crisis, and an expected blood loss >1 L.<sup>478</sup>

In patients with MG, NMBAs can be avoided for most procedures. Because of the decreased number of functional AChRs, these patients are very sensitive to nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocking agents but resistant to succinylcholine.<sup>479</sup> Hence, if succinylcholine is to be administered for rapid sequence induction, a larger than normal dose (1.5 to 2.0 mg/kg body weight) may be required. On the other hand, due to the decrease in cholinesterase activity achieved by anticholinesterase treatment, neuromuscular blocks with succinylcholine or mivacurium are frequently prolonged.<sup>480,481</sup>

Nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers should be used with caution because their effects are highly unpredictable, and the distribution of muscle weakness is often uneven. Rocuronium and vecuronium are preferred if sugammadex is available for reversal of the block. They should be administered in incremental doses of 0.1–0.2 times their ED50 and their effects on the neuromuscular transmission measured by an objective neuromuscular monitor until the desired neuromuscular blocking effect is achieved. Perioperative anticholinesterase treatment will modify the response to reversal agents because of the preexisting acetylcholinesterase inhibition, and in some cases, recovery of neuromuscular function after the administration of an anticholinesterase agent has been reported to be prolonged. When rocuronium or vecuronium is used, sugammadex is therefore by many the preferred reversal agent in MG patients.

Potent volatile anesthetics have been used successfully in MG patients. Because of the impaired neuromuscular transmission, volatile anesthetics can provide adequate paralysis to allow most surgical procedures to be performed without the need for a NMBA. Epidural and spinal anesthesia can also be used in MG patients, provided that neuromuscular function and ventilation are carefully monitored perioperatively. However, high levels of neuraxial blockade affecting the accessory muscles of breathing may not be well tolerated, as are approaches to brachial plexus block that may affect the phrenic nerve function (supraclavicular or interscalene blocks). For a more detailed review, see Baraka<sup>480</sup> and Abel and Eisenkraft.<sup>482</sup>

Postoperative monitoring of MG patients should be individualized based on the severity and clinical features of the disease, complexity and duration of the procedure, and expected complications and analgesic needs. Signs of bulbar and respiratory weakness should be carefully assessed. Neurophysiologic studies may be considered to distinguish a myasthenic crisis from cholinergic crisis, which can

present as paradoxical weakness in patients who receive anticholinesterase for reversal of neuromuscular blockade. Cholinergic crisis is also suspected when “SLUDGE” symptoms are present (salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, gastrointestinal distress, and emesis), and should be treated with atropine and glycopyrrolate to counteract muscarinic effects.

### Lambert–Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome

Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) is an immune- mediated channelopathy caused by decreased release of ACh as a result of autoantibodies against presynaptic voltage- gated calcium channels (VGCC) and other presynaptic elements.<sup>483</sup> Patients with LEMS have muscle weakness and fatigability primarily of the proximal limb muscles, particularly in the lower extremities. Ocular symptoms and bulbar muscle weakness may occur but are rarely the dominant features of LEMS. The syndrome is frequently part of a paraneoplastic phenomenon, usually combined with small cell lung carcinoma. Expression of functional VGCC in the surface membrane of cancer cells is likely responsible for most paraneoplastic LEMS. The specific trigger for anti- VGCC antibody development in non- paraneoplastic LEMS is not known, but it is likely immune- mediated and typically occurs in patients with other autoimmune disorders.

Unlike MG, patients with LEMS are usually worse in the morning with gradual improvement throughout the day. Improvement of muscle function with exercise is due to the accumulation of presynaptic calcium and subsequent improved release of ACh.<sup>484</sup> A minority of patients exhibit autonomic dysfunction, but it is usually not of the significance to affect the perioperative care of the patient. The diagnosis of LEMS is made by careful physical examination combined with clinical electrophysiologic testing, which can show a typical facilitation of motor action potential with high- frequency nerve stimulation (30 to 50 Hz). A high antibody titer to the P/Q type VGCC is also suggestive of LEMS. Anticholinesterase treatment has little effect on patients with LEMS. Treatment of underlying malignancy is important in those with paraneoplastic LEMS. Patients with moderate to severe weakness may also be treated with 3,4- diaminopyridine (DAP). Plasmapheresis or IVIG may be considered in non- paraneoplastic LEMS or in those with persistent weakness after treatment of their malignancy. Steroids and immunosuppressive therapy may also be considered.

**Anesthetic Considerations.** As in patients with MG, those with LEMS should be carefully evaluated for the risk of postoperative respiratory failure and the need for prolonged respiratory monitoring in the postoperative period. Sensitivity to depolarizing and nondepolarizing NMBAs is usually increased, and these agents are best avoided when possible. In patients treated with DAP or an anticholinesterase agent, reversal of neuromuscular blockade with an anticholinesterase may be ineffective. Similar to MG, incremental doses of rocuronium with objective monitoring of the neuromuscular transmission and reversal with sugammadex can minimize the risk for residual blockade when an NMBA is required.
