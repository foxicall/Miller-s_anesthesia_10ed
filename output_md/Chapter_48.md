## What Is Palliative Medicine?

### DEFINITION

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines palliative care as “an approach that improves the quality of life of patients (adults and children) and their families facing the problems associated with life- threatening illness, through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial and spiritual.”<sup>1</sup> Importantly, palliative care does not necessarily need to be limited to the end of life. The Center to Advance Palliative Care states, “unlike hospice care, palliative care can be provided at the same time as curative treatments; it is appropriate at any age and at any stage of a serious illness.”<sup>1</sup> Illness has many facets; symptom management, family support, and aid in decision- making are common areas of focus for palliative care teams (Fig. 48.1).<sup>2</sup> Symptom management encompasses physical symptoms (e.g., pain, constipation, nausea and vomiting, and delirium) as well as emotional symptoms (e.g., depression, anxiety, and distress). Palliative _care_ refers to the work of an interprofessional team, whereas palliative _medicine_ refers to the medical subspecialty focused on providing symptom relief and decision- making support for patients with serious illnesses.

### PRIMARY VERSUS SPECIALIST PALLIATIVE MEDICINE

There is a distinction made among the levels of palliative care: primary versus specialist. This distinction facilitates

the differentiation among the skills expected from all physicians, including anesthesiologists, and from those more suitable to a specialist service. Examples of primary palliative medicine include basic pain and symptom management, as well as discussions about prognosis, goals of treatment, and resuscitation status.<sup>3</sup> Specialty palliative medicine includes the management of refractory or complex symptoms, as well as the facilitation of conflict resolution among families, staff members, and treatment teams related to the goals of care (Fig. 48.2).<sup>4</sup>

### HISTORY OF PALLIATIVE MEDICINE

The word _palliative_ comes from the Latin word _to clothe_ and refers to the “covering up” of symptoms such as pain. Modern palliative medicine grew out of the hospice movement started by Dr. Cicely Saunders in the late 1960s,<sup>5</sup> and introduced in the United States in the 1970s. Since that time, the field has broadened from a focus on patients at the end of life to include patients with serious illnesses. There is also growing recognition that many hospice principles, such as the relief from suffering, apply to all patients regardless of prognosis (Fig. 48.3).<sup>6</sup> To meet this growing need, several academic medical centers have organized inpatient palliative care teams over the past decades.<sup>7</sup>

Currently, 90% of hospitals with more than 300 beds and 67% of hospitals with 50 or more beds have palliative care teams.<sup>8</sup> Hospice and palliative medicine became a recognized subspecialty in 2006, with the first board examinations offered in 2008. Physicians from ten medical specialties, including anesthesiology, are eligible to complete a fellowship and take the board examination.<sup>9</sup> As of 2022, there are 6999 active board- certified hospice

### WHY IS PALLIATIVE MEDICINE NEEDED?

The combination of an aging population and medical advancement has contributed to an increase in the number of

patients with chronic illnesses. In the United States, Medicare expenditures currently exceed $689 billion, with between 13% and 25% of Medicare expenses going to patients near the end of life.<sup>12</sup> Many of these patients have multiple comorbidities, repeated or prolonged hospitalizations, or a life expectancy of less than 1 year, which make many of them appropriate for hospice or palliative care services.<sup>13</sup>

Patients with serious illnesses have a significant symptom burden, most often involving pain, dyspnea, anxiety, and depression, and their family members report similar concerns.<sup>14</sup> Pain control during a life- limiting illness is often a major concern for patients and their families, and unfortunately, several surveys have found that they are often dissatisfied with the quality of pain control.<sup>14,15</sup> Patients and families also describe poor communication with health professionals, particularly in the setting of conversations regarding prognosis.<sup>16</sup> Palliative care, with its emphasis on symptom management and goal setting, attempts to address these concerns.

Approximately one-half of the countries in the world have at least one hospice or palliative care service, although most exist in larger and more developed countries. Methods and locations of palliative care delivery are widely variable throughout the world, depending on the country’s infrastructure. The variation in availability is vast, from 1 physician for every 1000 inhabitants in the tiny country of Niue near New Zealand to 1 per 8.5 million inhabitants in China and 1 per 90 million in Pakistan.<sup>22</sup> The access to appropriate medications is often restricted and variable as well. An estimated 80% of people with pain worldwide are unable to access opioids, due to concerns about addiction or restrictive national opioid policies.<sup>23</sup> The WHO has implemented a “Public Health Strategy for Palliative Care” that incorporates aspects of policy, drug availability, and education to customize and integrate palliative care throughout the globe.<sup>24</sup>

### PALLIATIVE CARE TEAM

Guidelines suggest that professionals with skills to meet the patient’s and family’s physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs participate in the creation of a specialized care plan. Ideally, teams are interprofessional and include a nurse, physician, social worker, and chaplain, as well as other professionals, depending on the need.<sup>25</sup> Many professional disciplines have developed specialized certification or training in palliative care (Box 48.1).

### INPATIENT PALLIATIVE CARE TEAMS

### Outcomes With Palliative Care Teams

Inpatient palliative care teams reduce hospital costs and resource utilization. Palliative care decreases cost of care by $3237 per patient, and by $4251 for cancer patients.<sup>28</sup> A study of Medicaid beneficiaries showed hospital costs decreased by an average of $6900 during an admission once palliative care teams became involved with reduced intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay<sup>24</sup> and fewer ICU deaths.<sup>29</sup> A cohort study of over 41,000 inpatients who received an inpatient palliative care consultation for goals of care showed significantly reduced future acute care costs, health care utilization, and 30- day readmissions when compared with a propensity- matched cohort that did not receive a palliative care consultation.<sup>30</sup> Studies examining inpatients with advanced cancer have demonstrated greater cost savings when a palliative care consultation is requested earlier in the hospital stay,<sup>31</sup> and in patients with more comorbidities.<sup>31</sup> Earlier palliative care consultations also help to facilitate building consensus around goals of care and use of invasive means to support life.<sup>32</sup> Inpatient palliative care teams improve patientcentered outcomes including quality of life,<sup>33</sup> patient and caregiver satisfaction,<sup>33</sup> and, to a variable degree, symptoms.<sup>34,35</sup> Importantly, palliative care consultation does not increase in- hospital mortality,<sup>36,37</sup> and even has been shown to lead to fewer days in the hospital and fewer inpatient deaths.<sup>38</sup> This fact may be important for families and

patients who incorrectly view palliative care as hastening death.

### When to Consult Palliative Care Teams

Basic palliative care knowledge and skills are necessary for all clinicians; however, certain situations call for the expertise of a palliative care specialist. A variety of indicators have been developed to help physicians decide when a specialty palliative care assessment should be performed. Patients’ palliative care needs should be assessed first by the treating physician, then a consultation with a palliative medicine specialist should follow when warranted. The presence of a potentially life- limiting (e.g., multiorgan failure, major trauma, sepsis) or life- threatening condition (e.g., metastatic cancer, cirrhosis, chronic renal failure) serves as the foundation for specialty palliative care involvement.<sup>39</sup> The palliative care candidate should also fit at least one additional criterion, such as frequent hospital admissions for the same condition, functional decline, or an ICU stay of 7 days or longer. More specific consultation criteria should consider the local hospital system, resources, and patient population. Each hospital should develop a procedure for identifying patients who would benefit from palliative care consultation,<sup>39</sup> which often includes assistance with symptoms that are difficult to control, complex decision- making, and provider or family support.

### Palliative Care in the Surgical Intensive Care Unit

The three main models of palliative care integration in the ICU are the consultative model, the integrative model, or a hybrid model.<sup>40</sup> In the consultative model, a specialist palliative care team makes recommendations to the primary physician regarding the care of the patient. In the integrative model, the surgeon or intensivist identifies and addresses issues related to palliative care without specialist input. Hybrid models that combine the two approaches above also exist. ### Trigger Criteria in the Surgical Intensive Care Unit

Surgical patients who die in the ICU often follow one of two courses. The first often occurs in trauma patients or other more acutely ill surgical patients with a rapidly declining course, while the second is characterized by a prolonged hospital stay with multiple transitions between acute care and ICU settings and intervening periods of prognostic uncertainty.<sup>40</sup> One study of patients in a trauma ICU showed that early assessment of patient and family preferences and early interdisciplinary meetings led to unchanged mortality, do not resuscitate (DNR) orders, and withdrawal of life support; however, the DNR and withdrawal orders were completed earlier in the hospital stay, and the ICU length of stay decreased in patients who ultimately died.<sup>32</sup> Focus on identification of appropriate palliative care consultations early in the hospital course is increasingly important, particularly in the former group.

### Post–Intensive Care Syndrome (PICS)

Several landmark studies have demonstrated increased incidence of cognitive, psychiatric, and physical dysfunction following critical illness.<sup>75–79</sup> The development of new or worsening function in any of these domains is defined as PICS and has also included acute and chronic psychological morbidity in patients’ family members (defined as PICS- F).<sup>80</sup> Among survivors of critical illness, cognitive impairment occurs in 25%–78%<sup>81,82</sup> , depression in 36%– 40%<sup>83</sup> , anxiety in 39%, posttraumatic stress disorder in 10%–62%,<sup>76,84</sup> and new disability in approximately onethird.<sup>85</sup> Physical impairments even without disability are common<sup>79,86</sup> and ICU- acquired weakness can persist long term. PICS prevention and treatment are active areas of research and the development of post- ICU recovery clinics have grown, particularly with increased rates of patients receiving mechanical ventilation from COVID- 19.<sup>87,88</sup> The growing emphasis on function, recovery, survivorship, and meaningful outcomes following hospitalization<sup>89</sup> and critical illness mirrors many elements of palliative care and are intimately related.

### OUTPATIENT PALLIATIVE CARE

Outpatient palliative care clinics exist to follow patients after discharge or to provide outpatient symptom management and psychosocial support.<sup>90</sup> The providers, availability, and focus vary widely.<sup>90</sup> A landmark study by Temel in 2010 randomized 151 patients with metastatic nonsmall- cell lung cancer to early outpatient palliative care plus the standard oncologic care or standard oncologic care alone. Patients in the palliative care group had higher quality of life scores, as well as fewer symptoms of depression.<sup>91</sup> Perhaps most surprisingly to some, the patients in the palliative care group lived a median of 2.7 months longer than those in the standard care group, despite receiving less aggressive care.<sup>91</sup> These results and others have led to a recommendation by the American Society of Clinical Oncology that any patient with advanced cancer should receive palliative care alongside usual oncology care. Much of this care is provided in a clinic setting, whether an independent palliative care clinic or an embedded palliative care team within an oncology clinic. A systematic review of patients with a variety of terminal illnesses showed that outpatient palliative care improved depression and quality of life, reduced aggressive care at the end of life, increased advanced directives, reduced hospital length of stay and hospitalizations, and improved

## Differences Between Hospice Care and Palliative Care

In contrast to palliative care, the definition of hospice care as it applies to the type of patient, the involvement of the medical team, and the setting, varies between countries.<sup>94</sup> In some countries, the terms hospice care and palliative care are interchangeably used, but in the United States, hospice care refers to a benefit provided by the governmental health care system. Patients are eligible to enroll in hospice care when their life expectancy is less than 6 months, and typically after life- prolonging treatments, such as chemotherapy, are stopped. Despite the logistic differences, the philosophy of hospice care generally focuses on reducing suffering, enhancing the quality of life, and supporting the patient and family (Fig. 48.4).

### HOSPICE CARE IN THE UNITED STATES

In the United States, hospice is a _per diem_ capitated payment system for a defined set of services for patients with a life expectancy of 6 months or less, as agreed on by two physicians.<sup>95</sup> Medicare pays for approximately 80% of hospice care in the United States, and many private insurers have established similar guidelines. These services include visits to a patient’s residence by a nurse (the main provider of clinical care<sup>96</sup> ), aide, social worker, and chaplain.<sup>95</sup> Family members are also eligible to receive a year of bereavement counseling.<sup>95</sup> Continuous care and short- term hospital admission are available for patients with uncontrolled symptoms. A core set of requirements for the hospice benefit exists (Fig. 48.5). However, beyond these requirements, each hospice unit can decide which treatments to cover, and this coverage can vary widely. For example, some hospice organizations may cover palliative radiation treatments, whereas others may not. Patients who outlive their 6- month prognoses may have their benefits extended in 60- day increments after a face- to- face visit with a physician, recertifying that the patient continues to meet hospice criteria.

### Who Is Eligible for Hospice Care?

To qualify for hospice, an attending physician and a hospice medical director must certify that the patient’s prognosis is less than 6 months if the disease runs its normal course.<sup>97</sup> Medicare has created guidelines that describe eligibility criteria for hospice services, although the opportunity for interpretation does exist. For example, the criteria for a patient with chronic lung disease include disabling dyspnea at rest, poor response to bronchodilators, hypoxemia at rest, and repeated hospitalizations for emergency department (ED) visits.<sup>96</sup> As a result of the subjectivity of some of the guidelines, some hospices may accept patients that other hospices deem ineligible. Patients are not required to have a DNR order to be enrolled in hospice.

### Recipients of Hospice Care

Over half of Medicare decedents received hospice care, though the median length of stay was only 18 days.<sup>98</sup> The most common diagnosis continues to be cancer, with approximately 30% of hospice patients, followed by heart disease at 18%, dementia at 15%, and respiratory diseases like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) at 11%.<sup>98</sup> The lack of good prognostic criteria for a 6- month life expectancy in non- cancer disorders can complicate hospice referral timing.<sup>99</sup> The median length of stay on hospice for patients with neurologic disorders is significantly longer at 105 days vs. 18 days for cancer patients. Based on utilization review, 14.3% of Medicare patients with cancer did not enroll until the last 3 days of life.<sup>100</sup> This trend toward late admission suggests that many patients are referred to hospice well after they are eligible.

### Outcomes of Hospice Care

Hospice care decreases patient symptom burden<sup>101</sup> and increases caregiver satisfaction, with 98% of family

members recommending hospice care to others.<sup>96</sup> A survey of over 1500 bereaved family members showed that 70% of respondents whose family member received hospice services considered the care “excellent” with less than 50% of those with decedents receiving home health services considering the care “excellent.”<sup>102</sup> Studies related to cost-effectiveness vary in their conclusions. Yet, a few studies suggest that the length of hospice use is correlated with cost savings.<sup>101,103</sup> A 2007 study by Taylor suggested cost savings with a hospice stay of 53 to 107 days,<sup>103</sup> whereas a 2013 study by Kelley showed cost savings with shorter hospice stays as well.<sup>101</sup> In a study looking at projected savings for beneficiaries with poor- prognosis cancers, increasing hospice utilization from 60% to 80% and increasing duration from 2 to 6 weeks could save $1.79 billion annually.<sup>104</sup>

### PALLIATIVE CARE AND ANESTHESIOLOGISTS

Anesthesiologists, as well as subspecialists in pain management and critical care medicine, are likely to interact with patients who are receiving palliative care services. Awareness of the goals of care in the palliative and hospice setting and what services are provided will help the anesthesiologist develop an appropriate plan based on the individual patient’s goals. Critical care anesthesiologists are often called upon to aid in the determination of hospice eligibility and to help families discuss care plans. Pain medicine physicians may provide medication management and interventional procedures alongside palliative care physicians or as part of hospice benefit.

### Surgery in Seriously Ill Patients

Palliative surgery has been defined as “the deliberate use of a procedure in a patient with an incurable disease with the intention of relieving symptoms, minimizing patient distress, and improving the quality of life.”<sup>105</sup> A 2004 study of over 1000 patients with advanced cancer undergoing palliative surgery showed a 30- day morbidity of 29% and mortality of 11%, with 80% of patients experiencing improved symptoms at 30 days. A similar study by the same author in 2011 showed a significant improvement from their earlier study. Of 227 patients, there was a morbidity of 20% and mortality of 4%, with 90% experiencing symptom resolution.<sup>105,106</sup> The study authors attribute some of this change to better patient selection through shared decision- making with patients and families.<sup>105</sup> One national retrospective study demonstrated that perioperative specialty palliative care in older adult Veterans undergoing high- risk surgical operations improves end- of- life communication and family support in patients who died following surgery. However, few patients who died were exposed to specialty palliative care.<sup>107</sup> Whether primary or specialty palliative care is pursued, defining appropriate outcomes, goals, and motivations remain important at an individual patient and family level. However, tradeoffs between risks and benefits in palliative surgery or seriously ill patients undergoing high- risk surgery remain poorly studied in outcomes research.<sup>63</sup>

### Communicating Surgery Risk in Seriously Ill Patients

Decision- making concerning surgery near the end of life is complex; while some surgeries may increase quality of life or allow a patient to achieve a specific goal, others

may result in excessive pain, functional decline, prolonged ICU or hospital stay, or increased use of resources without a clear benefit. A need exists to improve the quality of preoperative communication, to support decision- making by surgeons and patients, and to prioritize advance care planning for patients undergoing high- risk surgery. Despite the recognized risk of performing surgery in high- risk patient populations, many patients do not have a complete understanding of the wide range of possible outcomes associated with surgery.<sup>108,109</sup>

The mental frameworks often employed by surgeons and their patients can contribute to both a lack of understanding of possible outcomes and the formation of a contractual obligation to engage in maximal life- sustaining measures after surgery.<sup>58,110,111</sup> Both of these issues may result in provision of care that is not well- aligned with patient goals and preferences.

Communication frameworks exist that can assist in conducting preoperative conversations with patients considering high- risk surgery.<sup>112,113</sup> The “Best Case/Worst Case” tool uses a shared decision- making model with a visual aid to explain the best case scenario, the worst case scenario, and the most likely outcomes related to both surgery and conservative management (Fig. 48.6).<sup>113</sup> This tool allows the surgeon to elicit treatment preferences that are informed by realistic outcomes. Optimal use of the Best Case/Worst Case tool takes patient- specific comorbidities and risk factors combined with surgery- specific risk into account when explaining possible outcomes to patients considering highrisk surgery. A qualitative analysis of the Best Case/Worst Case tool using focus groups of both surgeons and older patients who had been involved in making a medical decision for themselves or their family members within the last year was conducted.<sup>113</sup> From a patient perspective, the tool was seen as favorable overall in that it establishes a choice, allows visualization of distinct paths to help in decisionmaking, and encourages deliberation based on a variety of treatment preferences. Surgeons pointed out that use of this tool provides a useful structure to guide the conversation, allows discussion of patient preferences, and may legitimize non- operative treatment options as still providing care rather than doing nothing. Use of tools such as this one may improve a patient’s understanding of potential complications and consequences of choosing a specific therapy, which allows them to elect the treatment option that is best aligned with his or her goals near the end of life.

### INTRAOPERATIVE CONCERNS

Resuscitation status should be communicated to any perioperative providers caring for the patient. Consideration should be given to preoperative frailty and geriatric conditions, postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis, and

the care of fragile skin, muscle, and bone in this population. Patients may have coagulopathies, thrombocytopenias, or neutropenias that may preclude them from receiving regional or neuraxial anesthesia, thereby impacting their intraoperative pain management.

### ANESTHETIC CHOICE AND CANCER

Recent studies have attempted to clarify the role of anesthesia in cancer progression and recurrence. Studies of the effect of regional anesthesia on those with cancer are mixed but suggest a possible mortality benefit,<sup>116</sup> although meta- analyses have found neither a beneficial nor detrimental effect of regional versus general anesthesia regarding cancer recurrence.<sup>117</sup> Despite these, recent randomized controlled trials comparing anesthetic approaches and their effects on cancer progression and recurrence have largely been negative. In one large study in breast cancer, there was no difference in breast cancer recurrence (or other outcomes measuring analgesia) in patients treated with regional anesthesia–analgesia compared with volatile anesthesia and opioids.<sup>118</sup> Similarly, epidural anesthesia– analgesia for major lung cancer surgery did not improve cancer outcomes compared to general anesthesia<sup>119</sup> nor did combined epidural–general anesthesia improve cancerspecific mortality in patients undergoing abdominal or thoracic surgery.<sup>120</sup> Additional discussion is available in Chapter 41. Studies that examine the choice of specific systemic anesthetics on cancer are mostly _in vivo_ or _in vitro_ , but available data also suggest differences in responses to tumor cells among the different drugs (Table 48.1).<sup>121</sup>

### POSTOPERATIVE COURSE

Standard postoperative care is adequate for most patients in palliative care. Risks for postoperative pain, delirium, and nausea, and vomiting may be increased, depending on the individual patient. Providers should communicate any limitations in care and when limitations revert to their preoperative status.

## Communication

Communication about goals of care and treatment preferences influence many outcomes in patients with serious illness. Better communication has been associated with improvement in quality of life, decreased use of lifeprolonging therapies near the end of life, provision of care that is more consistent with patient preferences, and earlier hospice referrals.<sup>122,123</sup> The American College of Physicians describes communication with seriously ill patients as a “low- cost, high- value intervention.”<sup>122</sup> The American College of Physicians recommends that communication be initiated early in the course of life- threatening illness, and ideally by a physician with an established relationship with the patient.<sup>122</sup>

Physicians often rate their communication skills favorably, but surveys of other practitioners and of patients suggest that physicians may not be able to self- assess accurately. ### The Surgical “Contract” and Other Cultural Barriers to Palliative Care Use

When caring for surgical patients, it is important to understand the conceptual frameworks that frequently influence both providers and patients in their approach to care. First, many patients perceive that surgery will “fix” their acute abnormality and restore normalcy to their lives,<sup>113</sup> rather than potentially contribute to further functional or cognitive decline. The potential for the latter problems is infrequently discussed preoperatively. In addition, the relationships between surgeons and their patients are unique from other relationships in medicine, because many surgeons perceive an implicit “contractual” agreement to pursue maximal life- sustaining measures after surgery.<sup>58,111,147</sup> Surgeons are said to subscribe to a covenantal ethic, meaning that the surgeon takes on individual responsibility for the patient’s life once he or she performs surgery.<sup>147</sup> An ethnographic study examining differences in end- of- life management of surgical patients in three ICUs – one unit run by trauma surgeons, one unit co- managed by surgeons and intensivists, and one unit run by intensivists – identified

divergent priorities between surgeons and intensivists. While many intensivists considered use of scarce resources more globally in their decision- making, surgeons tended to focus on the individual patient only and delay withdrawal of life- sustaining treatment even when it was requested by family.<sup>147</sup> Surgeons and intensivists often also delivered differing messages regarding treatment and prognosis.<sup>147</sup> A study evaluating recorded preoperative conversations conducted by surgeons performing high- risk surgery found that surgeons often described high- risk surgery as “big surgery” and focused on the technical aspects of surgery during their preoperative conversations.<sup>110</sup> If they discussed need for life support after surgery, they focused on standard recovery rather than potential longer- term complications (e.g., prolonged mechanical ventilation or dialysis). Furthermore, while many surgeons perceived that a contractual agreement to pursue aggressive postoperative care had been made, only rarely did they make this contract explicit with their patients.<sup>110</sup> Other communication breakdowns may occur at the level of the patient (i.e., anxiety, fear, or inability to understand) or the medical system (i.e., time constraints, lack of advance care planning) and also contribute to decisions to proceed with nonbeneficial surgery.<sup>112</sup>

Other concepts may contribute to aggressive postoperative care including “clinical momentum” or the accrual of multiple interventions,<sup>148</sup> the “fix- it” model in which a medical diagnosis represents a deviation from normalcy and must be righted,<sup>149</sup> the notion of surgical and ICU “covenantal” care,<sup>60,147</sup> and the “rescue” culture in surgery and critical care.<sup>60</sup> Structural challenges may also dictate decisions on LSTs, including: ICU bed volumes,<sup>150</sup> bed occupancy,<sup>151</sup> ICU- to- hospital bed ratios,<sup>152</sup> nursing staffing,<sup>153</sup> and ICU strain.<sup>154</sup> This suggests a possible opportunity for specialized palliative care interventions preoperatively, especially for high- risk surgery, but evidence for this is sparse.<sup>155</sup> A number of ongoing trials in high- risk perioperative patients and critically ill patients are seeking to adopt a more proactive structured approach to palliative care utilization, potentially in response to some of these structural and cultural challenges.<sup>49,156–159</sup> Having a good understanding of the unique communication challenges in high- risk perioperative patients may help to facilitate communication with patients, families, and the care team.

### WHAT DO FAMILIES CONSIDER IMPORTANT IN END- OF- LIFE COMMUNICATION?

A majority of family members consider trust in the treating physician and truthful communication about the patient’s illness as two of the most important factors in receiving quality end- of- life care.<sup>160</sup> Families have greater satisfaction when a higher level of decision- making is shared.<sup>161</sup> In family meetings, family satisfaction increases with the number of empathetic statements made by a physician during a meeting.<sup>162</sup> A meta- synthesis of 16 qualitative studies examined factors that patients and families describe as important during inpatient end- of- life care.<sup>163</sup> Within the theme of effective communication and shared decisionmaking, family members identified honest and clear communication using simple, understandable language, provision of information necessary to help make decisions,

<!-- PDF page 12 -->

and engaging both the patient and family members in care planning as priorities.<sup>163</sup>

A variety of patient and family preferences influence the quantity of information desired and how to receive it. These preferences may be informed by a combination of personal, cultural, and family characteristics; however, physicians should not assume a preference based on a patient’s race or ethnicity.<sup>164</sup> Many practitioners use a question such as, “How much would you like to know?”,<sup>165</sup> although no studies of patient perceptions of that approach have been conducted. One study of patients with cancer indicated that some patients’ preferences for involvement in decisionmaking changed over the course of the illness.<sup>166</sup> A few studies have noted that caregivers wanted more information than the patients.<sup>167</sup> Some patients may indicate that they do not want to know about their condition and name a surrogate decision- maker instead.

### SPIRITUALITY AT THE END OF LIFE

Spirituality refers to the ways in which a person expresses meaning or purpose and affects an individual’s sense of connectedness to the world around them. Religion may be a component of spirituality for some people, but many other aspects of spirituality also exist. Swinton and colleagues conducted a qualitative study where patients, family members, and clinicians involved in the care of 70 patients dying in the ICU underwent semistructured interviews.<sup>168</sup> Respondents perceived dying as a spiritual process. They reflected that spirituality comprises an important part of a patient’s narrative before, during, and after death. Serious illness and the prospect of death can bring a number of spiritual issues to the forefront for patients and families, from questioning the meaning of one’s life to a religion’s teaching on specific medical interventions.<sup>169</sup> Patients typically want physicians to ask about their spiritual preferences<sup>170</sup> and describe their spiritual needs as frequently remaining unmet.<sup>171</sup> A simple screening question such as, “Is religion or spirituality important in your life?” has been proposed and may help identify patients in whom a spiritual need exists. While physicians may be concerned they are being intrusive when asking about spirituality, they can open the topic without imposing their own views.<sup>172</sup> Asking about religion and spirituality may provide a context for the health care team, since many patients and families make decisions based on these beliefs.<sup>173</sup> Physician understanding of the reasoning behind their patients’ decisions is likely to improve end- of- life care.

### COMMUNICATION FRAMEWORKS

The prior studies, which demonstrate frequent misunderstandings and poor communication, emphasize the need for recognizing the importance of effective communication among all participants.<sup>174</sup> A number of frameworks have been developed to aid physicians in discussions with patients and families.<sup>175–177</sup> No study has compared their relative effectiveness on patient or family outcomes, and very few assessed the effect of a specific communication framework on outcomes.<sup>165</sup> Commonalities in the approaches include active listening, validating patient concerns, and assessing understanding. A recommended

comprehensive conversation guide developed by synthesizing data collected in multiple qualitative studies includes the following elements: set up the conversation, assess understanding and preferences, share prognosis, explore key topics (i.e., goals, fears and worries, sources of strength, critical abilities, tradeoffs, and family), close the conversation, document your conversation, and communicate with key clinicians (Fig. 48.7).<sup>122</sup>

### Family Meetings

Family conferences in the ICU have increasingly been used to facilitate information sharing between families and providers and are recommended by various consensus groups.<sup>178–180</sup> In the ICU setting, meetings held within 72 hours of admission have shown decreased lengths of stay without increased mortality.<sup>181</sup> A growing body of literature is demonstrating the challenges and nuance of structured communication with patients and families in the ICU. One study evaluated a standardized communication approach of families of patients who were seriously ill and randomized to either a standardized communication template with a bereavement brochure or the standard care.<sup>176</sup> The communication intervention structured around the mnemonic of VALUE (value, acknowledge, listen, understand, elicit questions) emphasizes empathy, understanding of the patient as a person, and allowing family members to ask questions.<sup>176</sup> Family members in the study arm had decreased symptoms of psychological distress in a survey 3 months later (Box 48.2).<sup>176</sup> Other structured prompts like the Choosing Wisely 5 Questions out of Australia (“Do I really need this test or procedure?”; “What are the risks?”; “Are there simpler, safer options?”; “What happens if I don’t do anything?”; “What is the likely outcome?”) demonstrated increased inclusivity by families in ICU decisions.<sup>182</sup> Physician- driven, nurse- aided support strategies for dying patients may reduce prolonged grief symptoms in family members.<sup>183</sup> Other international studies using leaflets and brochures, education programs, use of a diary, changes in physical environment, open/flexible visiting hours, and support groups have shown variable improvements in outcomes, but more often than not, improve clinician–family interaction, family comprehension of the ongoing critical illness, and satisfaction.<sup>184</sup> However, many barriers to family meetings and communication within family meetings have been described, including time constraints, competing clinical responsibilities, the mindset that meetings should occur only when a clinical decision must be made,<sup>185</sup> and finally, more recently, physical, psychological, and structural limitations associated with the COVID- 19 pandemic, particularly limited visitation ability by families.<sup>186</sup>

These studies demonstrate the utility and benefit of family conferences either for newly critically ill patients, imminently dying patients, and chronically critically ill patients and must be weighed against other evidence suggesting possible harm. ### Breaking Bad News

The six- step SPIKES (setting, perception, invitation, knowledge, empathy, sequelae) protocol is a framework for breaking bad news. Several of the elements, such as finding a quiet meeting space, asking what the patient or family understands, and making a plan for the future, apply in many situations (Box 48.3).<sup>175</sup> A meta- synthesis of available qualitative data suggested that breaking bad news is a skill that requires the provider to reassess and adapt the conversation based on a number of factors including the provider–patient relationship, responses from the patient and family, the environment, and cultural or social factors.<sup>193</sup>

### Responding to Emotion

The NURSE (name, understand, respect, support, explore) protocol is a framework for responding to emotion, including anger. Patient and family anger directed toward health care providers is common in the setting of serious illness.<sup>194</sup> Crucial concepts in responding to anger include not becoming defensive or personalizing the anger, respectfully listening, and setting appropriate boundaries for safety.<sup>194</sup> Although difficult to accomplish in the moment, viewing anger as a coping mechanism in the grieving process is often helpful (Box 48.4).<sup>195</sup>

### Time- Limited Trials

In cases where the benefit of a clinical intervention is unclear for a specific patient, a time- limited trial, in which the physician and patient or family agree to reevaluate the benefit after a specified period, may be helpful.<sup>198</sup> Timelimited trials give families a sense of when the health care team expects to know whether an intervention is helping and creates the expectation that the issue will be readdressed. Time-limited trials are associated with decreased ICU length of stays, improved quality of family meetings, and reduced intensity and duration of ICU treatments (such as the use of mechanical ventilation).<sup>199</sup> In the face of prognostic uncertainty and/or unrealistically high expectations of survival by families/surrogates, these trials could serve as a bridge to further palliative and end- of- life care while respecting patient and family autonomy.<sup>200</sup>

### RESUSCITATION STATUS

### Outcomes of Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation

CPR was introduced around 1960, initially as a treatment for intraoperative events<sup>201</sup> and then expanded outside the surgical unit. Outcomes of CPR have improved, with over half the patients who suffer in- hospital cardiac arrest surviving the initial resuscitation and nearly one-quarter surviving to discharge.<sup>202,203</sup> Survival is better for patients with a shockable initial rhythm (37%) when compared with pulseless electrical activity (12%) or asystole (11%),<sup>204</sup> with time to defibrillation representing a critical factor in patient outcome.

Approximately 85% of cardiac arrests in surgical patients occur postoperatively, and this patient population may have a higher likelihood of survival than other groups of patients.<sup>205,206</sup> In one large study of older surgical patients who survived to discharge after in- hospital CPR, approximately one-half had moderate- to- severe neurologic defects<sup>207</sup> and 60% were alive 1 year later.<sup>207</sup> Cardiac arrest in the operating room or in the postanesthesia care unit is rare (5.6 per 10,000 cases) but mortality remains high at 58%<sup>208</sup> and 30- day mortality in general surgical patients who had a postoperative cardiac arrest was 71%.<sup>209</sup> Evidence for induced mild hypothermia is mixed but may improve neurologic outcome after cardiac arrest, with benefits better demonstrated in the out- of- hospital arrest population.<sup>210,211</sup> It is important to note that a recent randomized controlled trial demonstrated no difference in targeted temperature management to 33°C compared to normothermia when incorporated into a comprehensive care bundle.<sup>212</sup> While previous meta- analyses demonstrated benefit in certain patient populations,<sup>211</sup> newer updated meta- analyses demonstrated targeted temperature management was not associated with improved survival or neurologic outcomes compared to normothermia.<sup>213,214</sup> Data increasingly suggest that there are gender and racial differences between outcomes after cardiac arrest. A study comparing outcomes in men and women after cardiac arrest showed that women who survived to discharge after cardiac arrest had worse cognitive, functional, and psychiatric outcomes after multivariate adjustment for multiple confounders.<sup>215</sup> In out- of- hospital cardiac arrests, African Americans were more likely to have poor neurologic disposition compared to White patients.<sup>216</sup> Black patients compared to White patients also had higher mortality following in- hospital cardiac arrests.<sup>217,218</sup>

### Resuscitation Status Discussions

Discussion of resuscitation status can be challenging to some providers.<sup>136</sup> Anesthesiologists may be involved in conducting discussions related to resuscitation status with patients in the ICU or in the perioperative period. Ideally, the discussion about code status takes place in the context of a conversation about the patient’s overall health and goals. For example, one patient might decide on life prolongation as a goal, in which case a physician might recommend resuscitation attempts even if unlikely to succeed, whereas another patient with the same illness might prioritize physical independence as a goal, in which case the physician might recommend a DNR order to decrease the likelihood of prolonged ICU time or an unacceptable

physical status. The impact of this approach is unknown. A small simulated study that played a standardized video of a code status discussion to cancer patients showed no difference between asking the patient their desired resuscitation status or making a suggestion for DNR order.<sup>219</sup> The few studies that formally examine the best way to approach discussions regarding resuscitation do not clearly demonstrate a single best approach.<sup>201</sup> For example, one study examined using the terminology “allow natural death” instead of “do not resuscitate” with terminal cancer patients and did not show that either term was favored over the other.<sup>220</sup> VitalTalk<sup>221</sup> is an important resource for strategies for discussing goals of care. A stepwise approach to resuscitation status discussion (using the mnemonic REMAP)<sup>222</sup> could help providers with such discussions by following a similar strategy found in VitalTalk. This includes reframing the scenario, expectation of emotion, mapping out the future, aligning with patient/caregiver values, and planning treatments that match values.

### Perioperative Limitations on Treatment

The American Society of Anesthesiologists and American College of Surgeons have published guidelines related to orders for the limitation of treatment in the perioperative setting.<sup>114,115</sup> The ethics of perioperative DNR orders are discussed more comprehensively in Chapter 7.

## Prognosis

Multiple studies have examined the ability of clinical providers to prognosticate survival and prognosis for a given patient. In a meta- analysis of eight studies in patients with a median survival of 4 weeks, physicians overestimated the prognosis by approximately 30%.<sup>223</sup> Predictions tend to be more accurate in patients with poorer performance status or with proximity to death.<sup>223,224</sup> In a prospective cohort study of 365 physicians and 504 patients receiving hospice care, the physicians overestimated survival by a factor of 5, and 63% of their predictions were overestimates.<sup>225</sup> The longer a physician knew a patient, the more difficult it was for that physician to prognosticate.<sup>225</sup> Another study also demonstrated overestimation of survival from cardiac arrest.<sup>226</sup> Inaccurate prognostication is also supported in more recent studies but may depend further on the outcome studied (e.g. mortality versus cognitive decline), the time horizon of the outcome (imminent death vs. 6 month survival), and the provider being studied (nurse versus physician).<sup>227</sup> Physicians in the ICU tend to be overly pessimistic. In a study of 851 patients receiving mechanical ventilation, only 71% of patients for whom a physician estimated a likelihood of survival of less than 10% at some point during their stay in the ICU died in the ICU.<sup>228</sup> Another showed daily prognostications of death in the medical intensive care unit were often pessimistic as well.<sup>229</sup> A meta- analysis suggested that physicians are more accurate at predicting mortality than algorithmic scoring systems (described in the Prognostic Tools section, below) in the first 24 hours after ICU admission; however, neither physicians nor scoring systems are reliably accurate for individual patients.<sup>230</sup> Overall, physician estimates do correlate with survival<sup>231</sup> and are more accurate in predicting mortality than other outcomes such

### DISEASE TRAJECTORIES

Most disease trajectories can be included in one of the following functional categories: sudden extreme disability or near death; a period of good functionality, followed by a rapid, steady decline; a waxing and waning course with periods of decompensation followed by improvements; and a slow decline, starting at a low functional level (Fig. 48.10).<sup>237</sup> Disability trajectories in the latter categories have been explored in more detail and likely depend on the condition leading to death (e.g. cancer vs. dementia vs. organ failure).<sup>238,239</sup> Disability following critical illness has also been well- characterized and is particularly dependent on pre- ICU functional impairments.<sup>240</sup> These frameworks can be useful when discussing prognoses with patients, particularly in talking to patients with conditions that are difficult to predict such as COPD and congestive heart failure (CHF) or after a sudden dramatic acute or critical illness.

### PROGNOSTIC TOOLS

Multiple web- based prognostic tools exist (e.g., eprognos is.ucsf.edu<sup>241</sup> ), but are typically specific to particular cancers. An important consideration is that the type of cancer is actually less important than a variety of patient factors (e.g., functional status and laboratory values) as the disease becomes more advanced (Fig. 48.11).<sup>242</sup> In general, a patient with cancer who spends more than half of the day in bed has a median survival of 6 months (Box 48.5).<sup>243</sup>

Multiple tools, which are covered in depth in Chapter 80, have been proposed to help the clinician predict mortality in the ICU. These tools may be more useful in the research setting than for prediction of outcome in an individual patient.

### Renal Disease

Patients with chronic kidney disease on dialysis will live 16% to 33% as long as age- and sex- matched patients who are not on dialysis.<sup>254</sup> Probability of survival at 10 years for patients on dialysis who are older than 65 years of age is 3.1%.<sup>254</sup> Poor functional and nutritional status, as well as comorbid conditions, suggest a poor prognosis.<sup>254</sup> Withdrawal of dialysis accounts for up to 20% of deaths in patients on dialysis each year, and the average patient lives 8 to 12 days after dialysis withdrawal.<sup>255</sup>

### Frailty

Frailty represents a complex multicomponent syndrome of physiologic decline with increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes. While frailty is often felt to be aging- related, frailty phenotypes can present in patients below 65 years old.<sup>256</sup> Frailty (and particularly frailty assessment tools) typically measure physical frailty, defined as the multisystem biologic decline in older adults,<sup>257</sup> or utilize a deficit- accumulation model<sup>258</sup> whereby frailty represents the accumulation of illnesses and functional and cognitive declines. Regardless of how frailty is defined or measured, there is a growing recognition that frailty is highly associated with mortality.<sup>259–261</sup> Frailty has also been associated with a number of poor prognostic outcomes in surgical patients.<sup>262–265</sup> The increased functional, cognitive, and psychosocial needs associated with frailty likely support an increased role of palliative care in these patients and their caregivers.<sup>266</sup>

### SYMPTOM MANAGEMENT

### Alternative Routes of Drug Delivery

While the oral route is the most common for medication delivery, many patients near the end of life are unable to swallow oral medications because of oral lesions, nausea, the dying process, or other reasons. Many patients in palliative and hospice care have difficult intravenous access because of frequent medical care, dehydration, or other issues. To avoid multiple attempts at intravenous cannulation, palliative medicine practitioners and most hospices use subcutaneous infusions for the delivery of medications, especially opioids.<sup>267,268</sup> An assumption of safety is based, in part, on studies showing that many intramuscular injections are actually administered into the subcutaneous tissue.<sup>269</sup> In addition, other types of drugs such as benzodiazepines, some antiemetic medications, antibiotics, neuroleptics, and even fluids can be subcutaneously administered in the palliative care setting.<sup>267,268,270</sup> While the subcutaneous administration of opioids is safe, the optimal conversion ratio from one route to another is a subject of debate.<sup>271</sup> Other studies support the transmucosal, sublingual, and rectal administration of opioids.<sup>271</sup> Finally, neuraxial drug

delivery via continuous epidural infusions or intrathecal pumps can be utilized, particularly for intractable pain.<sup>272</sup>

### Pain

Pain management in patients who are seriously ill can be quite different from other patients with pain. Some of these patients have pain that is best managed by a pain management specialist, and some will need adjuvant medications (Table 48.3). In cancer patients, 15% to 90% with solid tumors have pain, depending on the type and stage of cancer, as well as the age, race, and sex of the patient.<sup>273</sup> Most cancer pain is due to the cancer itself, but approximately one-fifth of patients develop pain related to the cancer treatment.<sup>274</sup> The majority of cancer pain can be managed with the WHO “Cancer Pain Stepladder.”<sup>275</sup> Zech published a prospective study of 2118 cancer patients using this stepladder during which 76% of the patients received pain relief with minimal side effects.<sup>275</sup> However, pain management may be significantly more challenging for patients with advanced cancer and with actively progressing disease: 60% to 90% of this population report pain that significantly impacts function, mood, and sleep. Pain of this severity may benefit from more advanced techniques such as interventional pain medicine (see below and Chapter 47),<sup>276,277</sup> psychological interventions,<sup>278</sup> or palliative chemotherapy or radiation therapy. Additionally, with more cancer survivors, non- cancer pain and pain secondary to cancer treatment (e.g., chemotherapy- induced peripheral neuropathy, radiation neuritis) have become a more prominent concern in cancer patients.

### Bone Pain

Breast, lung, kidney, and prostate cancers frequently metastasize to the bones.<sup>279</sup> Patients with metastatic disease can have both osteoblastic and osteolytic bony lesions leading to severe pain.<sup>280</sup> Multiple targets for bone pain are possible, but no clear consensus has been reached on the best treatment methods.<sup>281</sup> Hormonal therapy is effective in breast, prostate, and endometrial cancers. Interventional techniques such as intrathecal pumps may be appropriate in some patients.<sup>282</sup> Palliative radiotherapy can be helpful for patients with bone metastases, although pain relief may take several weeks.<sup>283</sup> Dexamethasone orally, subcutaneously, or intravenously is commonly recommended for bone pain by expert consensus, although only a few small studies support its use.<sup>284</sup> Bone- modifying agents, such as zoledronic acid or pamidronate, have shown benefit for bony pain in some cancers and should be considered when appropriate from an oncologic standpoint.<sup>279,285</sup> Nonsteroidal anti- inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are helpful in alleviating cancer pain, although their advantage when combined with opioids is less clear.<sup>286</sup> More advanced interventions such as epidural steroid injections, vertebroplasty/ kyphoplasty, or surgery may also be indicated if patients are either having intolerable side effects or inadequate analgesia with systemic medications.<sup>287</sup>

### Pain in the Intensive Care Unit

In the ICU, pain is common and attributable to many factors such as surgical wounds, placement of invasive monitoring devices, and immobility.<sup>289</sup> When pain was rigorously assessed in prospective studies,<sup>290</sup> chest tube removal, wound drain removals, and arterial line insertions were considered the most painful ICU procedures. Pain incidence was recorded in nearly 40%–50% of ICU patients<sup>291,292</sup> and pain at rest was also common.<sup>293</sup> Psychological responses to pain in the ICU are well- described<sup>294</sup> and survivors have longer- term traumatic stress<sup>295</sup> associated with these events. Chronic pain is common in ICU survivors, even in those without a history of preexisting pain symptoms,<sup>296–298</sup> and represents an important component of post–intensive care syndrome (PICS).

A patient’s self- reported pain is the reference standard for assessing pain. However, quite often, pain in the ICU can be difficult to assess when patients are intubated, sedated, unable to speak, or if they have delirium. The Behavioral Pain Scale<sup>299</sup> and the Critical Care Pain Observation Tool<sup>300</sup> are validated methods of assessing pain in the ICU population.<sup>301</sup> The use of validated tools for pain assessment is supported by the pain, agitation/sedation, immobility and sleep disruption (PADIS) guidelines by the Society of Critical Care Medicine.<sup>302</sup> Commonalities among the tools include assessment of facial expression, body movement, and synchrony with ventilation. Artificial intelligence algorithms measuring facial expression and body movements<sup>303,304</sup> similar to those measured from validated manual observation tools could aid in automated pain detection in the future. In the SUPPORT trial, pain was poorly controlled among those who died in the hospital, with half of the surrogates indicating that their family member had moderateto- severe pain for at least half of the time in the three days before death.<sup>15</sup> Pain assessment and treatment in the ICU has improved since the SUPPORT trial.<sup>305</sup>

### OPIOID USE

Opioid use at the end of life is common, although consumption dramatically varies throughout the world.<sup>19</sup> In a 2012 study of 1068 patients at six US medical centers, 70% of patients received an opioid in the last week of life, and 47% received opioids in the last 24 hours.<sup>306</sup> Some physicians are hesitant to use opioids at the end of life for fear of hastening a patient’s death. A study by Morita in 2001 retrospectively analyzed opioid and sedative consumption of 209 patients in the 48 hours before death and found no difference in survival based on opioid or sedative dose.<sup>307</sup> A small study on the rate of increase of opioids found no correlation with time to death, although total morphine doses were relatively low overall in the study.<sup>308</sup> A larger study by Portenoy in 2006 showed a

weak association with total opioid dose and time to death, but this association appeared to account for less than 10% of the variance even when combined with other variables.<sup>309</sup> Most experts believe that appropriately administered opioids do not hasten death, can be safely used, and generally do not require invoking the doctrine of double effect.<sup>310</sup> Some authors have urged caution with the use of opioids in patients with cancer and uncertain prognoses to decrease the risk of opioid dependence and abuse in patients with the possibility of a long life after treatment.<sup>311</sup> However, recently, in the context of the opioid epidemic, opioid use has decreased among patients dying of cancer with rising pain- related ED visits.<sup>312</sup> While concerns for dependence exist, clinicians must also be careful to avoid undertreatment in dying patients and opioids should be titrated based on the clinician’s assessment of the pain.

### Interventional Pain Management at the End of Life

Despite the guidance provided by the WHO Stepladder,<sup>313</sup> 10% to 20% of cancer patients have pain that is refractory to parenteral management.<sup>314,315</sup> Select patients may benefit from interventional pain modalities.<sup>287</sup>

However, patients at the end of life may be more prone to bleeding from coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia and infection from immunosuppression. These issues need to be evaluated carefully by an interventional pain specialist. Abdominal pain, especially for pancreatic cancer, can be treated with celiac plexus blocks or neurolysis, while pelvic pain may be addressed with superior hypogastric blocks or neurolysis. These techniques are described in detail in Chapter 47. Less invasive modalities such as peripheral nerve blocks or trigger point injections may also be beneficial.

Neuraxial (i.e., intrathecal and epidural) analgesia can decrease numerical pain scores while significantly decreasing oral opioid intake, which may lead to fewer side effects such as constipation and sedation.<sup>272</sup> In a randomized controlled trial of 202 patients with refractory cancer pain comparing comprehensive medical management versus an implantable intrathecal drug delivery system, those in the intrathecal group had significant reductions in fatigue and depressed level of consciousness. Additionally, this study showed a trend toward improved survival, with 53.9% of patients receiving intrathecal analgesia remaining alive at 6 months compared with 37.2% of the control group ( _P_ = .06).<sup>316</sup>

### BOWEL OBSTRUCTION

Some patients with abdominal tumors may develop partial or complete bowel obstruction. Surgical outcomes for patients with bowel obstruction and a life expectancy of less than 2 months are poor,<sup>318</sup> and gastrointestinal selfexpanding metallic stents should be considered.<sup>319</sup> Placement of a nasogastric tube can provide immediate relief while other options are considered. A venting gastrostomy tube is an option in patients who are refractory to treatment and can allow the patients to enjoy the taste of food while allowing gastric decompression. Simultaneous use of antiemetics, including somatostatin analogues (e.g., octreotide), and corticosteroids may be particularly effective for nausea control in inoperable malignant bowel obstructions.<sup>320,321</sup>

### ARTIFICIAL HYDRATION AND NUTRITION

Many patients receiving palliative care are unable to take in food or fluids because of nausea, dysphagia, or obstruction. The decision of whether to administer artificial hydration and nutrition is often a difficult one for patients and physicians. Both parties may hold strong cultural or religious views, and many describe a fear of “starving” the patient.<sup>322</sup> Nonetheless, medically assisted nutrition and hydration can and perhaps should be considered medical interventions.

Symptoms of hunger are less common in advanced disease, and thirst can be treated with ice chips or mouth swabs.<sup>323</sup> The administration of artificial hydration and nutrition is associated with risks of fluid overload, leading to choking or edema, as well as diarrhea and nausea.<sup>324</sup> Additional risks associated with a gastrostomy tube include dislodgement and the possible increased need for restraints.<sup>178</sup> The administration of enteral or parenteral nutrition and hydration is considered a medical intervention, and, as such, the risks and benefits should be discussed with patients and families.<sup>322</sup> The benefit of artificial hydration and nutrition has been established for patients in a persistent vegetative state or those with acute stroke or head injury, short- term critical illness, oropharyngeal cancers, and possibly bulbar amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.<sup>325</sup> However, artificial hydration does not improve symptoms, quality of life, delirium, or survival in patients with advanced cancers at the end of life.<sup>326,327</sup> The use of percutaneous feeding tubes in patients with advanced dementia does not prevent pneumonia or improve survival and is not recommended.<sup>328,329</sup> For some patients, a time- limited trial of artificial hydration or nutrition may be appropriate to evaluate for benefit, such as improvement in delirium, while monitoring for side effects.

### DYSPNEA

Dyspnea is “a subjective experience of breathing discomfort that consists of qualitatively distinct sensations that vary in intensity,”<sup>330</sup> as differentiated from breathing that appears labored or fast to an observer. Dyspnea could be distinguished as a sensation (neural activation from a biologic receptor activation standpoint) and a perception (the reaction of the individual to the sensation<sup>330</sup> ), suggesting that observation- based tools assessing dyspnea may disagree with subjective reports of breathlessness. This was shown in one prospective multicenter trial assessing breathlessness by observers and patients during spontaneous breathing trials in mechanically ventilated patients.<sup>331</sup> The pathophysiology of dyspnea is multifactorial and consists of increased output from respiratory chemocenters, stimulation of

Finally, the affective dimension of dyspnea contributes to the sensation of discomfort, which also contributes to fear and anxiety.<sup>332</sup>

Dyspnea is common in patients with a high risk of death<sup>333,334</sup> and was identified as the most distressing symptom by patients who could self- report in the ICU, regardless of their mechanical ventilation status.<sup>335</sup> In intubated patients, dyspnea is common and is associated with posttraumatic stress disorder in ICU survivors.<sup>336</sup> Dyspnea increases closer to the time of death, even in patients without known cardiopulmonary disease.<sup>337</sup> Treatment is either aimed at reversing the cause of dyspnea (e.g., thoracentesis for an effusion) or at symptom management. Nonpharmacologic therapy such as a fan or pulmonary rehabilitation may be helpful.<sup>338</sup> Oxygen supplementation has not demonstrated benefit in symptoms in one metaanalysis.<sup>339</sup> Studies of low- dose opioids in patients with refractory dyspnea support their safety and efficacy.<sup>340,341</sup> A randomized, double- blind, crossover study of 48 COPD patients who were taking 20 mg of sustained- release morphine daily showed a significant improvement in dyspnea with minimal side effects.<sup>342</sup> Studies of the effect of benzodiazepines with and without opioids for dyspnea and its negative affective perceptions are small and mixed, with a trend toward possible benefit.<sup>343–345</sup> Acupuncture may also provide dyspnea benefit in two randomized studies in patients with lung cancer and COPD.<sup>346,347</sup> A recent metaanalysis confirmed these findings over a larger set of smaller studies.<sup>347</sup>

### DEPRESSION AND ANXIETY IN THE PATIENT RECEIVING PALLIATIVE CARE

Depression and anxiety are common at the end of life, with estimates ranging from 5% to 30% and 7% to 13%, respectively.<sup>348</sup> Many factors contribute to psychological distress, including social, economic, spiritual, and physical stressors associated with serious illness.<sup>349</sup> A rapid screening

test for depression consisting of two questions, “Are you depressed?” and “Have you experienced a loss of interest in things or activities you would normally enjoy?” has a sensitivity of 91% and a specificity of 68% and has been validated in the palliative care population.<sup>350</sup> Patients who screen positive for depression or who have suicidal or homicidal ideations should be referred to a psychiatrist or other experienced provider. Depression should be distinguished from delirium and normal grief (Table 48.4).<sup>351</sup> For the anesthesiologist, recognizing depression and making appropriate referrals is important.

Treatments for depression vary by life expectancy. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and monoamine oxidase inhibitors may be appropriate for patients with a longer life expectancy, since the time to onset is 1 to 2 months. For patients with a life expectancy of weeks to a few months, methylphenidate has been well studied in the cancer population. The onset of action is 1 to 3 days and is generally effective and well- tolerated for depression and fatigue.<sup>352,353</sup>

### DELIRIUM AT THE END OF LIFE

Delirium is covered in depth in Chapter 61. It affects 28% to 90% of patients who are terminally ill, with increasing incidence as death approaches.<sup>354–356</sup> The Confusion Assessment Method has been validated in the palliative care population.<sup>357,358</sup> Some patients may have more than one possible cause of delirium (Box 48.6). Delirium can have a major impact on a patient’s ability to make a well- informed decision about treatment options, including surgery, which may have ethical and legal implications. Most patients who recover remember being delirious, and those who do find it very distressing.<sup>359</sup>

As much as half of the delirium near the end of life may be reversible.<sup>354</sup> Increased likelihood of reversible delirium is associated with a younger age, less severe impairment, and lack of organ failure.<sup>360</sup> Patients with true terminal delirium have a very short life expectancy.<sup>356</sup> Attempts at workup should be based on the patient’s goals of care (Box 48.7). ### BLEEDING IN THE PATIENT RECEIVING PALLIATIVE CARE

Bleeding can be caused by numerous pathologic conditions, including coagulation disorders and cancers. Fibrinolytic inhibitors such as tranexamic acid, interventional radiologic procedures such as embolization, and surgery have been suggested for patients with compatible goals and life expectancy.<sup>369</sup> The decision for therapeutic transfusion of blood products are provider- and circumstancedependent<sup>370</sup> and should be guided by evidence- based and prognostic considerations as with all transfusions. Exsanguination in patients receiving palliative care is uncommon but can be upsetting to patients, families, and providers. No randomized trials of best treatments have been conducted. Some practical suggestions include the use of dark towels, suction, holding pressure, and staying with the patient. Medications such as benzodiazepines, opioids, and ketamine are frequently suggested during exsanguination to provide sedation and amnesia.<sup>371</sup>

### WITHDRAWAL OF LIFE SUPPORT

Some patients or family members may wish to discontinue mechanical ventilation or other forms of life support. Withdrawal of life support is ethically acceptable and respects patient autonomy. It is important to emphasize to patients and families that “withdrawal” or discontinuation of life support does not equate to the “withdrawal of care.”<sup>180</sup> Anesthesiologists can provide expertise in the management of pain and anxiety with the withdrawal of ventilation. ### Outcomes of Withdrawal of Life Support

Often vasoactive medications are also being administered during mechanical ventilation in the ICU. In one study of 471 patients, death occurred fastest (median 30 minutes) when mechanical ventilation and vasoactive medications were stopped together. When vasoactives were stopped without extubation, time to death was 45 minutes and when patients were extubated without cessation of vasoactives, time to death was 50 minutes.<sup>387</sup> Another study of 74 medical patients in an ICU who were expected to die shortly after extubation showed a mean morphine dose of 5.3 mg/h in the last hour of mechanical ventilation, followed by 10.6 mg/h during the hour before death.<sup>388</sup> The average time to death was 153 minutes after extubation, with a range of 4 to 934 minutes. ### Characteristics of the Pediatric Palliative Care Population

The variety of diagnoses for patients in pediatric palliative care are wider than those for adults,<sup>396</sup> with the most common disorders being congenital and neuromuscular in origin.<sup>396</sup> In the largest observational study to date, over onethird of pediatric patients referred for palliative care consultation services were 1 to 9 years of age, one-third were 10 to 18 years old, and less than 20% were younger than 1 year of age.<sup>396</sup> Children often have prolonged survival after the initial palliative care consultation compared to adults.<sup>396</sup> Some disorders, including chromosomal or severe developmental abnormalities, are rarely seen by adult palliative care physicians. The decision to forgo treatment with curative intent is generally difficult for families, and prognostication is similarly difficult for providers.<sup>395</sup> Moreover, multiple legal issues related to declining medical treatment in children exist that differ from those related to adults.<sup>397</sup>

### Symptom Management in Pediatric Palliative Care

Historically, symptoms in children with serious illnesses have been poorly managed. In a retrospective survey of parents of deceased children, parents noted that their children suffered “a lot” or “a great deal,” mostly from pain, fatigue, and dyspnea.<sup>397</sup> In contrast to most studies of adults near the end of life, a 2011 study noted neurologic symptoms being more prevalent,<sup>396</sup> suggesting a need for a better understanding and treatment of these symptoms in the pediatric population. Regional anesthesia has been reported as being of benefit to pediatric patients with pain that is difficult to manage with systemic treatment.<sup>398</sup> Data on specific therapies for children are limited, and many practitioners use medications based on adult studies.
