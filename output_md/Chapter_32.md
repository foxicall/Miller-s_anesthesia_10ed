JONATHAN MARK, BECKY SCHROEDER, ATILIO BARBEITO, and GRACE C. MCCARTHY

- **KEY POINTS** Monitoring of the electrocardiogram (ECG) provides continuous monitoring of heart rate, identification of arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities, and detection of myocardial ischemia.

   - Bedside ECG monitors often apply a filter to the ECG signal that reduces low-frequency artifact, such as baseline wander, and high-frequency artifact, such as 60-Hz electromagnetic interference. However, such filters may distort or exaggerate ST- segment deviations or obscure pacemaker spikes.

   - An anterolateral precordial lead (V3, V4, or V5) should be selected for the most sensitive detection of myocardial ischemia.

   - Demand- mediated subendocardial ischemia resulting in ST- segment depression is the most commonly observed form of perioperative ischemia. ST- segment depression is most commonly observed in an anterolateral precordial lead regardless of the coronary territory responsible.

   - Supply- mediated transmural ischemia resulting in ST- segment elevation is uncommonly observed intraoperatively except during cardiac operations. In contrast to ST- segment depression, STsegment elevation is indicative of the myocardial territory and coronary artery involved.

   - Most automated noninvasive arterial blood pressure measuring devices use an oscillometric measurement technique and rarely cause complications. Caution should be exercised in patients who cannot complain of arm pain, those with irregular rhythms that force repeated cuff inflation, and those receiving anticoagulant therapy.

   - The Allen test for palmar arch collateral arterial flow is not a reliable method to predict complications from radial artery cannulation. Despite the absence of anatomic collateral flow at the elbow, brachial artery catheterization for perioperative blood pressure monitoring is a safe alternative to radial or femoral arterial catheterization.

   - The accuracy of a directly recorded arterial pressure waveform is determined by the natural frequency and damping coefficient of the pressure monitoring system. Optimal dynamic response of the system will be achieved when the natural frequency is high, thereby allowing accurate pressure recording across a wide range of damping coefficients.

   - The preferred position for alignment (or “leveling”) of external pressure transducers for measuring arterial or central venous pressure (CVP), which eliminates confounding hydrostatic pressure artifacts, lies approximately 5 cm posterior to the sternomanubrial junction. The more conventional location for the reference level used for hemodynamic monitoring including central venous and pulmonary artery pressures, is the mid- thoracic level, which corresponds most closely to the mid- left atrial position and is located halfway between the anterior sternum and the bed surface in the supine patient.

   - Because of wave reflection and other physical phenomena, the arterial blood pressure recorded from peripheral sites has a wider pulse pressure than when measured more centrally.

   - Dynamic measures of cardiac preload, such as stroke volume and pulse pressure variation, are better predictors of intravascular volume responsiveness than static indicators, such as CVP and pulmonary artery wedge pressure.

   - Selecting the best site, catheter, and method for safe and effective central venous cannulation requires that the physician consider the purpose of catheterization, the patient’s underlying medical condition, the intended operation, and the skill and experience of the physician performing the procedure. Right internal jugular vein cannulation is preferred due to its consistent, predictable anatomic location and its relative ease of access intraoperatively.

   - Mechanical complications from central venous catheters can be decreased by the use of ultrasound vessel localization, venous pressure measurement before large-catheter insertion, and radiographic confirmation that the catheter tip lies outside the pericardium and parallel to the walls of the superior vena cava.

   - CVP is the result of a complex and diverse interplay among many different physiologic variables, the main ones being venous return and cardiac function. No simple relationship exists between CVP and circulating blood volume. Despite this, important pathophysiologic information can be obtained by careful assessment of the CVP waveform morphology.

- Catheter misuse and data misinterpretation are among the most common complications of central venous and pulmonary artery catheters.

- Pulmonary artery wedge pressure is a delayed and damped reflection of left atrial pressure. The wedge pressure provides a close estimate for pulmonary capillary pressure in many cases, but it may underestimate capillary pressure when postcapillary pulmonary vascular resistance is increased, as in patients with sepsis.

- Use of central venous, pulmonary artery diastolic, or pulmonary artery wedge pressures as estimates of left ventricular preload is subject to many confounding factors, including changes in diastolic ventricular compliance and juxtacardiac pressure.

- Pulmonary artery catheter monitoring has not been shown to improve patient outcomes. Reasons cited for these results include misinterpretation of catheter- derived data and failure of hemodynamic therapies that are guided by specific hemodynamic indices.

- Thermodilution cardiac output monitoring, the most widely used clinical technique, is subject to measurement errors introduced by rapid intravenous fluid administration, intracardiac shunts, and tricuspid valve regurgitation.

- Mixed venous oxygen saturation is a measure of the adequacy of cardiac output relative to body oxygen requirements. This measurement is also dependent on the arterial oxygen saturation and hemoglobin concentration.

- All minimally invasive and noninvasive cardiac output monitors have their own sources of potential error. Awareness of potential sources of error and clinical limitations is key.

## Heart Rate and Pulse Rate Monitoring

The ability to estimate the heart rate quickly by manual palpation is critically important despite near- universal use of electronic devices for continuous or intermittent monitoring. The electrocardiogram (ECG) is the most common heart rate monitoring method used in the operating room. Accurate detection of the R wave and measurement of the interval from the peak of one R wave to next (R- R interval) serves as the basis from which digitally displayed values are derived and periodically updated (e.g., at 5- to 15- second intervals)<sup>1</sup> (Fig. 32.1).

The distinction between heart rate and pulse rate lies in the difference between electrical depolarization with systolic contraction of the heart (heart rate) and a detectable peripheral arterial pulsation (pulse rate). Pulse deficit describes the extent to which the pulse rate is less than the heart rate in cases such as atrial fibrillation in which a truncated R- R interval compromises stroke volume to the extent that no peripheral pulse is detectable.

Electrical–mechanical dissociation and pulseless electrical activity are extreme examples of pulse deficit. The heart rate is reported from the ECG trace and the pulse rate from the pulse oximeter plethysmograph or arterial blood pressure monitor. Considering both in monitoring and clinical evaluation improves accuracy and reduces measurement errors and false alarms.<sup>2</sup>

## Electrocardiography (ECG) Monitoring

The value and importance of intraoperative ECG monitoring is evidenced by its requirement as a basic monitoring standard by the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA)<sup>3</sup> : “Every patient receiving anesthesia shall have the electrocardiogram (ECG) continuously displayed from the beginning of anesthesia until preparing to leave the anesthetizing location.” Similar recommendations are supported by the American Heart Association (AHA) for ECG monitoring hospitalized patients outside of the operating room.<sup>4</sup>

The three primary reasons for ECG monitoring are continuous monitoring of heart rate, identification of arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities, and detection of myocardial ischemia. Furthermore, with many patients coming to surgery with cardiovascular implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), proper functioning of these devices can only be determined with the aid of ECG monitoring. (Perioperative management of these devices is described in Chapter 34.) In order for bedside ECG monitoring to be accurate and effective, the clinician must attend to several technical details, including proper lead placement and selection, filter mode, and gain adjustment.

### ECG LEAD PLACEMENT AND SELECTION

Current operating room and intensive care monitoring systems have five leads that allow monitoring of the standard limb leads (I, II, III), the augmented limb leads (aVR, aVL, aVF), and a single precordial lead, either V1, V2, V3, V4, V5, or V6. Typically, two of these 12 standard leads are simultaneously displayed on the bedside monitor. Historically, the augmented limb leads and precordial leads were described as _unipolar_ , whereas the standard limb leads were described as _bipolar_ . An American Heart Association (AHA) scientific statement<sup>5</sup> discourages this distinction because in effect, all the lead configurations are effectively bipolar in their recording of surface electrical potentials.

Based on AHA guidelines, ECG monitoring leads have a standard color coding system: right arm (white), left arm (black), right leg (green), left leg (red), and precordial lead (brown). Of note, this color scheme is not the same used internationally or the one recommended by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC). Unlike recording a standard 12- lead ECG, where the limb leads are attached to electrodes placed on the wrists and ankles or arms and legs, when monitoring patients in the operating room or intensive care unit, the limb leads are typically placed on the torso, with the right and left arm leads placed just below the clavicles and the leg leads placed above the hips (Fig. 32.2). Of note, placement of the right leg lead (green lead) can be anywhere on the body because it is a ground electrode, and its location will not alter the display of any of the selected standard leads.<sup>6</sup>

Placement of the limb electrodes on the torso has been utilized since 1966, when Mason and Likar introduced a variation on positioning the standard limb electrodes of the 12- lead ECG during exercise stress testing to minimize artifacts in the limb leads caused by movement.<sup>7</sup> Although the limb lead QRS complexes are slightly different in amplitude and axis and the precordial leads may vary slightly from the standard 12- lead ECG recording, studies have shown that the ST- segment measurements during exercise stress testing were generally similar when the Mason- Likar 12- lead ECG system is used, as compared with the standard 12- lead ECG.<sup>7–10</sup> Consequently, torsopositioned limb leads have become standard for monitoring in the operating room and intensive care unit because of convenience and the potential to obviate motion artifact.

In addition to the standard lead locations, lead V4R, a mirror image of lead V4, positioned in the fourth intercostal space at the mid- clavicular line is the best lead for identifying right ventricular (RV) ischemia. Although never chosen for routine ischemia monitoring, this lead has proven valuable for detecting right ventricular ischemia and infarction and might be chosen to monitor patients at risk for inferior left ventricular ischemia, which is commonly accompanied by right ventricular involvement.<sup>5,16</sup>

Three-lead ECG monitoring systems, while less common today than five- lead systems, remain available particularly in non–operating room procedural settings and for patient transport. A three- lead system is also the standard ECG monitoring system incorporated in external defibrillator devices to allow the unit to provide transcutaneous pacing. The three leads (right arm, left arm, left leg) can be placed in similar locations to their five- lead system counterparts, and this allows reliable monitoring of heart rate, detection of R waves for synchronized direct current cardioversion, and monitoring for potentially life- threatening arrhythmias such as ventricular fibrillation.

Another modified three- lead system is a surrogate for precordial lead V1. This lead (MCL1) is recorded by placing the left arm lead in standard position, the left leg lead at the V1 position, and selecting lead III. This modified lead system is useful for monitoring in the intensive care unit or other circumstances where detection of P-wave morphology and arrhythmias is of paramount importance.

Following cardiac surgical procedures, recording the ECG signal from an epicardial atrial pacing wire may also be useful for detecting P waves that may not be as evident from skin surface ECG recordings (Fig. 32.5). This alternative lead recording is usually performed in the intensive care unit using a 12- lead ECG system and attaching one of the atrial pacing wires to a precordial lead wire.

### ECG FILTER SELECTION

The ECG signal is subject to artifacts (noise) both in the low-frequency and high-frequency ranges. Consequently, all ECG monitors use bandpass filters to narrow the signal bandwidth, preserve the signal of interest while reducing artifacts, and improve signal quality. As the name suggests, a bandpass filter allows signal frequencies within a certain range to pass or be displayed, while attenuating or functionally eliminating signal frequencies both below and above the bandpass.

Low-frequency artifact is typically caused by respiration or patient movement that causes the ECG tracing to wander above and below the baseline (Fig. 32.6). Often this will appear as the ECG tracing being cut off or only partially displayed on the bedside monitor channel and the low- frequency (also called high- pass) filter will eliminate this artifact and stabilize the tracing on the monitor screen. The heart rate forms a rough lower bound for the frequency content of the ECG signal and is measured in Hertz (Hz, cycles per second). Since heart rates slower than 40 beats per minute (0.67 Hz) are uncommon, typical lowfrequency analog filters attenuate frequencies below 0.5 Hz. However, while such filters stabilize the ECG baseline, they may also distort the ECG signal, particularly the ST segment. This results from phase nonlinearities that occur in areas of the ECG signal where frequency content and wave amplitude abruptly change, as occurs where the end of the QRS complex meets the ST segment. The 1975 American Heart Association (AHA) recommendations included a 0.05- Hz low- frequency cutoff for diagnostic ECGs. This recommendation preserves the fidelity of repolarization, but baseline drift can still be a problem. Current modern digital filtering provides more sophisticated methods for a higher cutoff for low- frequency filtering without the phase distortions observed with analog filtering. High- frequency ECG artifacts are typically caused by muscle fasciculations, tremors, and most importantly, the ever present 60 cycle (Hz) electromagnetic interference from other electrical equipment in the monitoring environment. These artifacts can be eliminated with high- frequency filters (also termed low- pass filters), but like low- frequency filters, the high- frequency filters may distort the ECG signal in undesirable ways. The higher frequencies in the ECG signal include features such as rapid upstroke velocity (QRS complex), peak amplitude (R wave), and waves of short duration. Most importantly, pacemaker spikes, which by definition are high frequency and low amplitude, are often eliminated by high- frequency filters and make bedside identification of pacemaker function challenging. A highfrequency cutoff of 100 Hz was considered adequate by the AHA in 1975 to maintain diagnostic accuracy during visual inspection of an ECG, although it has long been recognized that higher-frequency components of the QRS complex may have clinical significance in patients with various

forms of heart disease. According to current AHA recommendations, to measure routine duration and amplitudes accurately in adults, adolescents, and children, an upper frequency cutoff of at least 150 Hz is required, and an upper frequency cutoff of 250 Hz is more appropriate for infants.<sup>5</sup>

Current ECG monitors allow the clinician a choice among several filtering modes or bandwidths. The actual filter frequencies tend to vary among manufacturers, but in general there are three different filters that may be selected, termed diagnostic mode, monitoring mode, and filter mode. The _diagnostic mode_ typically has a bandpass of 0.05 to 150 Hz, and this filter should always be selected to display an ECG signal that best resembles a 12- lead ECG recording, including an undistorted ST segment and clear pacing spikes. The _monitor mode_ typically has a bandpass of 0.5 to 40 Hz, and while both low-frequency (baseline drift) and highfrequency (60 Hz) noise is reduced or attenuated, the ST segments are often distorted and typically show an exaggerated deviation that is artifactually introduced by the filter<sup>17</sup> (Fig. 32.7). The _filter mode_ bandpass is 0.5 to 20 Hz and may incorporate a notch filter aimed at further attenuating and eliminating 60 Hz interference from nearby electrical equipment.

### ECG GAIN SELECTION

In addition to lead and filter selection, bedside monitors also allow adjustment and selection of the ECG signal gain. A standard ECG is recorded at a gain of 10 mm/mV and is indicated by a 1 mV rectangular calibration signal on a paper recording or by a 1 mV vertical marker at the edge of the bedside monitor ECG waveform. Monitors may be set to an autogain mode, where the available display space is filled by the ECG tracing and the corresponding increase or decrease from standard gain is indicated by the vertical marker. Alternatively, the clinician may adjust the ECG signal gain manually. This is done when the monitor detection of heart rate is inaccurate. For example, one might reduce the ECG signal gain when the monitor is inappropriately counting a tall T wave as an R wave and displaying an artifactual heart rate that is twice the true rate.

Gain adjustment is important because all features of the ECG are equally amplified or reduced when the gain is changed from the 10 mm/mV standard. As a consequence, ST- segment deviations can be obscured when gain is reduced and thereby impair clinical observation of important ST- segment changes. Alternatively, if the gain is increased, ST deviations will also be amplified proportionally. While common clinical communication of ST- segment shifts is always described in terms of millimeters (mm) of depression or elevation, interpretation of bedside monitor ECG tracings must always be tempered by consideration of the display gain (Fig. 32.8).

### ECG PACING MODE

While older ECG monitors recorded analog (continuous) signals, current devices convert the analog ECG signal to digital

form by sampling the signal at very high rates up to 15,000 times per second.<sup>5</sup> This oversampling of the ECG signal was originally introduced to allow identification and recording of pacemaker stimulus outputs (pacemaker spikes), which are generally shorter than 0.5 ms, but current systems do not always detect these small-amplitude, high-frequency signals reliably. As a result, most bedside ECG monitors now include a _pacing mode_ selection, which when activated, employs an algorithm to detect and highlight these pacing spikes, making detection of pacemaker function easier. When this monitoring mode is selected, clinicians should recognize that the displayed ECG tracing shows regular markers, often in a different color than the ECG tracing, that indicate and highlight presence of a pacemaker stimulus output. These are not amplified pacemaker signals, but rather monitor- generated markers of their detection. While very helpful to the clinician at the bedside, pacemaker mode monitoring may not reliably detect pacemaker spikes in all patients. In other instances, the intrinsic features of the pacemaker stimulus in different leads might allow their detection in some leads but not others (Fig. 32.9).

### ECG MONITORING FOR MYOCARDIAL ISCHEMIA

The ST segment, representing myocardial repolarization, is the ECG component most sensitive to acute myocardial ischemia. ST elevation, with or without tall positive (hyperacute) T waves, indicates transmural ischemia and is most often the result of acute coronary artery occlusion either by coronary thrombosis or vasospasm (Prinzmetal variant angina). Reciprocal ST- segment depression may appear in the contralateral leads. Ischemia confined to the subendocardial area is usually denoted by ST- segment depression. Subendocardial, ST depression–type ischemia typically occurs during episodes of symptomatic or asymptomatic ( _silent_ ) stable angina pectoris, and is characteristic of ischemia occurring during exercise, tachycardia, or pharmacologic stress testing in patients with significant but stable coronary artery disease.

### Automated Real- Time ST- Segment Monitoring

Real- time ST- segment analysis first appeared in cardiac monitoring in the mid- 1980s and is currently standard in most ECG monitors. While ST- segment analysis is commonplace in the operating room, it is underutilized in other

monitoring settings.<sup>22</sup> Chief among the reasons for the underuse of ST- segment analysis are the frequent number of false alarms and the lack of education on how to use the technology. In addition, little evidence exists as to whether the addition of computerized ST- segment ischemia monitoring improves patient outcomes after surgery.

Computerized ST- segment analysis identifies the STsegment 60 or 80 ms after the J point (termed as J+60 or J+80 ms) and compares it with the isoelectric point measured during the PR interval (Fig. 32.11). At standard gain, one millimeter of ST- segment deviation equals 0.1 mV of ST elevation or depression. Changes in ST- segment deviation over time in each lead can be displayed as ST- segment trends, just like trend displays of other hemodynamic variables (Fig. 32.12). When the computer misidentifies the appropriate isoelectric or ST monitoring point, the clinician can manually adjust the J point or the ST- segment measurement point (Fig. 32.13).

One major advantage of continuous ST- segment monitoring is that the electrodes stay in place and do not vary as they might with intermittent 12- lead ECG recordings. However, for improved diagnostic accuracy of ST- segment monitoring, the following points should be recognized:

1.  Changes in body position may cause ST- segment changes and lead to false ST- segment alarms. However, changes in the QRS complex almost always accompany these positional ST- segment changes and therefore can be easily distinguished from true ST- segment deviations (Fig. 32.14). Changes in position of the heart in the mediastinum have also been shown to affect the ST segment. ### ECG Criteria for Acute Myocardial Ischemia

The ECG criteria most accepted for detecting myocardial ischemia during continuous ECG monitoring are those established and validated during exercise stress testing.<sup>24</sup> During stress testing and with acute subendocardial ischemia, the electrical forces responsible for the ST segment are deviated toward the inner layer of the heart, causing ST- segment depression or demand- mediated ischemia (Fig. 32.17). With acute transmural epicardial ischemia, the electrical forces in the ischemic area are deviated toward the outer layer of the heart, causing ST- segment elevation or supply- mediated ischemia in the overlying leads (Fig. 32.18). Note that, most commonly, intraoperative or perioperative ischemia is demand- mediated subendocardial ischemia, which is manifest as ST- segment depression.<sup>25</sup>

Although the anterolateral precordial leads are most sensitive for detecting these changes, they are non- localizing as to the coronary distribution responsible. In contrast, supply- mediated transmural ischemia, while much less common perioperatively, causes ST- segment elevation in leads overlying the involved coronary bed and thus are able to localize the responsible coronary territory, (e.g. ST elevations in inferior leads II, III, aVF suggest occlusion of the right or posterior descending coronary artery).

With demand- mediated ischemia, as heart rate increases, J- point depression and upsloping ST- segment depression occurs. As the severity of ischemia progresses, the ST segment typically becomes horizontal (flattens), the magnitude of ST- segment depression increases, and the ST segment becomes downsloping (see Fig. 32.17). Standard criteria for stress- induced ischemia are 1 mm (0.1 mV) or more of horizontal or downsloping ST- segment depression measured 60 or 80 ms after the J point.

During the perioperative period, ECG monitoring most commonly identifies stress- induced, ST- depression, demand- mediated ischemia, or other causes of supply– demand imbalance such as prolonged hypotension. Such ECG changes do not provide information about the location of the ischemic myocardial area. In contrast, ST- segment elevation indicating transmural or supply- mediated ischemia, observed particularly during cardiac surgery, provides useful information about the myocardial segment and coronary perfusion territory responsible for the ischemic episode. Because most patient- monitoring systems do not simultaneously monitor all 12 ECG leads, selecting which chest leads to monitor is of great importance, particularly in noncardiac surgery. During exercise stress testing, investigators have identified leads V4 and V5 as the most sensitive leads to detect exercise- induced ischemia (90%–100% sensitivity).<sup>26</sup> London and colleagues studied high- risk patients undergoing noncardiac surgery and showed that the greatest sensitivity for ischemia was obtained with lead V5 (75%), followed by lead V4 (61%).<sup>14</sup> Combining leads V4 and V5 increased the sensitivity to 90%, whereas with the standard lead II and V5 combination, the sensitivity was only 80%. They also suggested that if three leads (II, V4, and V5) could be simultaneously examined, the sensitivity would increase to 98%. Landesberg and associates monitored continuous 12- lead ST- segment changes greater than 0.2 mV from baseline in a single lead or more than 0.1 mV in two contiguous leads at J+60 ms, lasting longer than 10 minutes in patients undergoing major vascular surgery.<sup>15</sup> They showed that leads V3 and V4 were more sensitive than V5 in detecting perioperative ischemia (87%, 79%, and 66%, respectively). As a result of these and other investigations, it appears most appropriate to monitor lead V3, V4, or V5 for optimal detection of perioperative ST- segment depression, choosing the specific lead location to avoid interference with the surgical prep and procedure.

### Intermittent Techniques

The first indirect methods of blood pressure measurement utilized a sphygmomanometer and peripheral pulse palpation, as originally described by Riva- Rocci in 1896. This later evolved into both auscultatory and oscillometric methods in common use today.<sup>29</sup> All of these methods use a cuff placed around the upper arm that is inflated to the point that all blood flow ceases, and then slowly deflated while simultaneously monitoring some indicator of return of blood flow through the vessels underlying the cuff. The auscultatory method uses Korotkoff sounds, a complex

series of audible signals created by turbulent arterial flow. Put simply, the systolic pressure is assigned to the pressure at which the first sound is heard and the diastolic to the point when the sounds disappear (the transition from “noisy” turbulent flow to laminar “quiet” flow).<sup>30,31</sup> The oscillometric method is different in that it measures very small changes in pressure (or oscillations) detected in a slowly deflating cuff with the point of maximal oscillation reflecting the mean arterial pressure (MAP). Importantly, with this method, the systolic and diastolic pressures are calculated by proprietary algorithms rather than measured.<sup>31–33</sup> This is in contrast to the auscultatory method by which systolic and diastolic can be measured but MAP cannot. Both methods are highly dependent on any physiologic conditions that interfere with transmission of either sound or small pressure changes. Such confounders include ambient temperature, severe edema, obesity, abnormal compliance of the overlying tissue and conditions that affect blood flow (shock, intense vasoconstriction).<sup>31–35</sup> Significantly

The upper arm is the most common location and is the most accurate as it approximates the level of the heart most closely. If the cuff is above the heart, as in the nondependent arm in the lateral decubitus position, the result will be inappropriately low while the reverse is true for the dependent arm.<sup>36</sup> Obesity also poses a challenge to using noninva sive blood pressure (NIBP) measurement. A large amount of adipose tissue under the cuff reduces transmission of pulsations, sometimes requiring very high inflation pressures leading to patient discomfort and overestimation of the measured pressure.<sup>38</sup> Also, the conicity of the upper arm makes cuff fit and position problematic. Comparisons of the upper arm, forearm, and wrist in morbidly obese patients show the best results for the wrist and worst for the upper arm.<sup>39</sup> In general, the ankle is always a poor choice.<sup>40</sup>

Commercially available devices that measure NIBP are validated against auscultation methods due to the ethics of validation against an invasive technique. According to the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation (AAMI) and the British Hypertension Society (BHS), new devices must demonstrate average differences ≤ ± 5 mm Hg and standard deviations ≤8 mm Hg, which means that deviations of up to 20 mm Hg are still considered “acceptable performance.”<sup>41,42</sup>

A large number of clinical studies have assessed the level of agreement between indirect and direct measurement of blood pressure. Generally, the two techniques show the highest level of agreement for MAP values while systolic pressures are the most divergent.<sup>37,43–45</sup> As a rule, the systolic is underestimated while the diastolic is overestimated with the discrepancy increasing with worsening

hypotension.<sup>46,47</sup> Similarly, NIBP methods tend to underestimate high pressures and overestimate low pressures. Below an MAP of 65 mm Hg, NIBP does not appear useful as a guide to therapy.<sup>37,44,48,49</sup> Levels of agreement also decrease in the critically ill and the elderly.<sup>37,43–45</sup>

It is important to remember that the auscultatory method measures systolic and diastolic pressures, while oscillometric devices measure the mean and calculate systolic and diastolic values. Furthermore, directly measured arterial pressure measurements utilize another technique altogether. In some authors’ opinions, “current protocols for validating blood pressure monitors give no guarantee of accuracy in clinical practice.”<sup>42,50</sup> Expecting them to yield identical values is unrealistic, especially in complex and unstable clinical situations.

### Continuous Noninvasive Techniques

There are three primary approaches to continuous noninvasive blood pressure (C- NIBP) monitoring, all of which generate a continuous blood pressure waveform but from totally different physiologic signals. Some devices function independently while others require intermittent calibration, most often with a conventional cuff placed on the upper arm.<sup>51</sup> The widest commercially available and most studied method is the volume clamp (or vascular unloading) technique adapted by Jan Peňáz in 1973 from work done by earlier innovators.<sup>52</sup> A small inflatable finger cuff containing infrared plethysmography sensors measures changes in the size of the underlying digital arteries caused by propagation of the intravascular pulse wave. Ultrafast sensors continuously adjust the pressure in the cuff to maintain a constant counter- pressure on the finger and these adjustments correspond to changes in arterial size. In this way, changes in pressure within the cuff correspond to changes in blood flow beneath the cuff, allowing construction of a pressure waveform that approximates arterial blood pressure.<sup>53–55</sup> The second technique for C- NIBP monitoring is applanation tonometry, a technique whereby a tonometer is positioned over the radial artery and attached to a strain gauge. Analysis of temporal changes in pressure against the tonometer are rapidly translated into an arterial pressure waveform.<sup>56,57</sup> Lastly, there are a variety of devices that use specifically placed plethysmography sensors, sometimes combined with the ECG trace, to measure various characteristics of the propagated pressure waveform. Among these are pulse arrival time, pulse transit time, and pulse wave velocity.<sup>58–61</sup> This last group are the least developed and least studied.

Compared to invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring, the vascular clamping technique appears to have adequate precision but poor accuracy, making it most useful for trending changes in blood pressure.<sup>51,62–64</sup> This technique performs poorly during periods of hemodynamic lability and tends to overestimate MAP.<sup>51,62,63</sup> However, compared to NIBP, it detects hypotension more often, likely due to the very low sampling rate of intermittent devices.<sup>65,66</sup> Tonometry- based methods are highly dependent on sensor stability, while pulse wave propagation techniques are confounded by arterial stiffness and ambient light.<sup>53,58,67,68</sup> All continuous noninvasive technologies for blood pressure monitoring may be confounded by comorbid conditions such as arterial atherosclerosis and calcification, preexisting

hypertension, and neuropathy, as well as patient movement and ambient temperature.<sup>53</sup>

Two special populations in which C- NIBP monitoring may be of particular interest are the elderly and the obese. Unfortunately, all devices show decreased performance in both of these cohorts regardless of the clinical setting.<sup>51,67,69</sup> They tend to overestimate blood pressure and miss hypotensive episodes when compared to invasive monitoring.<sup>63,70</sup> Pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg position are especially problematic in the obese population.<sup>71</sup> However, their improved performance over intermittent methods may be valuable in cases in which invasive methods are problematic.

### Complications of Noninvasive Blood Pressure Measurement

Although automated, NIBP measurement is generally safe, there have been reports of rare but severe complications<sup>72</sup> (Box 32.2). Compartment syndrome is possible after prolonged periods of frequent cycling and is most likely related to direct trauma or impaired distal limb perfusion. Caution should be exercised in cases of peripheral neuropathy, arterial or venous insufficiency, severe coagulopathies, or recent use of thrombolytic therapy.

### DIRECT MEASUREMENT OF ARTERIAL BLOOD PRESSURE

Arterial cannulation with continuous pressure transduction remains the accepted reference standard for blood pressure monitoring despite its risk, cost, and need for technical and clinical expertise (Box 32.3). It is superior to oscillometric techniques in detecting intraoperative hypotension and has a low rate of complications.<sup>44,73–75</sup> More recently, though, use of waveform analysis has gained popularity as a tool to assist in a variety of monitoring and management modalities. These include use of the dicrotic notch in the arterial waveform as a trigger for intra- aortic balloon counterpulsation management and respiratory- induced variations in the waveform shape and amplitude to provide an array of directly measured and derived values that can identify preload reserve and volume responsiveness.<sup>76–79</sup>

### Percutaneous Radial Artery Cannulation

The radial artery is the most common site for invasive blood pressure monitoring because it is technically easy to cannulate and complications are rare.<sup>75,80</sup> Slogoff _et al._ described 1700 cardiovascular surgical patients who underwent radial artery cannulation without ischemic complications despite evidence of arterial occlusion after decannulation in more than 25% of patients.<sup>81</sup> Furthermore, most investigations of hand perfusion following radial artery harvest have reported no significant decrease relative to the contralateral hand in either the early or late postoperative periods.<sup>82–87</sup> The Allen test is sometimes used to judge the safety of radial artery catheterization or harvest despite a great deal of conflicting data regarding its value.<sup>81,82,88,89</sup> In general, it seems that while an abnormal modified Allen test may be useful in identifying patients unacceptable for radial artery use for bypass graft or coronary angiography, there is no evidence that it predicts clinical outcomes following cannulation for blood pressure monitoring.<sup>82</sup>

### Alternative Arterial Pressure Monitoring Sites

If the radial arteries are unsuitable or unavailable, there are multiple alternatives. The ulnar artery has been used safely even following failed attempts to access the ipsilateral radial artery.<sup>98,99</sup> Similarly, the brachial artery, while lacking collateral branches to protect the hand, has a long track record of safe use. Several investigators have reported large series of brachial artery catheters in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with very few vascular, neurologic, or thrombotic sequelae.<sup>100,101</sup> The axillary artery has the advantages of patient comfort and mobility, and complications appear to be similar in incidence to those for radial and femoral arteries.<sup>75</sup> A slightly longer catheter is preferred for the brachial or axillary sites due to their relatively deeper and more mobile anatomic locations. Clinicians should be aware, however, that the risk of cerebral embolization is significantly increased when more central vessels are utilized. To reduce the risk, prolonged, high-pressure flushing of these more central catheters should be avoided.

### Complications of Direct Arterial Pressure Monitoring

Although large clinical investigations confirm the safety of radial arterial pressure monitoring, factors associated with increased risk include vasospastic arterial disease, previous arterial injury, thrombocytosis, protracted shock, high- dose vasopressor administration, prolonged cannulation, and infection.<sup>75,80–82,106,107</sup> Rare but serious complications have been reported after arterial cannulation at all locations (Box 32.4). In most cases, catheter placement was technically difficult or there were contributory factors such as shock or coagulopathy. In a large observational study of 2000 untoward clinical events resulting from any kind of vascular access, only 13 were related to peripheral arterial cannulation, fewer than those associated with central venous (18) or even peripheral venous cannulation (33). The adverse arterial cannulation events involved equipment problems, unintentional arterial drug administration or disruption/kinking of the catheter itself. In only one case did transient vasospasm follow radial artery cannulation. An additional 10 cases were noted to involve problems with incorrect device calibration or erroneous data interpretation.<sup>73,108</sup> Data from the anesthesia closed claims reports have shown that claims related to arterial pressure monitoring constitute only 8% of all claims related to any vascular access (2% of total claims). ### Technical Aspects of Direct Blood Pressure Measurement

Most invasive blood pressure monitoring systems are underdamped second- order dynamic systems that demonstrate simple harmonic motion dependent on _elasticity, mass,_ and _friction_ .<sup>110–112</sup> These factors are highly dependent on the components of the monitoring system: elasticity is related to the physical system (e.g. tubing and stopcocks) while mass is related to the continuous column of fluid contained within the components. Both are involved in the friction associated with the entire system. All systems have a characteristic _natural frequency_ , the frequency at which the system oscillates, and a _damping coefficient_ , the speed of decay of oscillations when the system is disturbed. The ideal system has an optimal combination of these two factors, a combination that can profoundly affect the appearance of the waveform displayed.<sup>113</sup>

### Pressure Monitoring System Components

Arterial pressure monitoring systems have a number of components, including the intra- arterial catheter itself, stopcocks for blood sampling and transducer zeroing, in- line blood sampling ports, a pressure transducer, continuousflush device, and electronic cable. The flush device provides a continuous, slow (1–3 mL/h) infusion of saline to purge the monitoring system and prevent thrombus formation. Dextrose solutions should not be used as flush contamination of sampled blood may cause serious errors in blood glucose measurement.<sup>114</sup> The flush device not only ensures continuous slow flushing of the system but also includes a valve for periodic, high- pressure flushing following blood sample collection or to restore the system’s dynamic response to baseline.<sup>115</sup>

### Normal Arterial Pressure Waveforms

The systemic arterial pressure waveform results from ejection of blood from the left ventricle during systole followed by peripheral runoff during diastole. The systolic waveform immediately follows the ECG R wave and consists of a steep pressure upstroke, peak, and ensuing decline. The downslope of the arterial pressure waveform is interrupted by the dicrotic notch and reaches its nadir at end- diastole (Fig. 32.25). The dicrotic notch of a central aortic pressure waveform is sharply defined and thought to result from aortic valve closure.<sup>128</sup> In contrast, more peripheral arterial waveforms generally display a slightly delayed and mildly blunted dicrotic notch more dependent on properties of the arterial wall. Note that the systolic upstroke starts 120– 180 ms after the beginning of the R wave, reflecting the time required for left ventricular depolarization, isovolumic contraction, opening of the aortic valve, left ventricular ejection, and propagation of the pressure wave through the aorta to the pressure transducer.

The bedside monitor displays values for the systolic peak and end- diastolic nadir pressures. In simplest terms, MAP reflects the area beneath the arterial pressure curve divided by the beat period, averaged over multiple cardiac cycles, but is dependent on device- specific algorithms. MAP is sometimes estimated as diastolic pressure plus one- third of the pulse pressure, but this shortcut is only valid at slower heart rates because the duration of diastole decreases as the heart rate increases.<sup>129</sup>

The morphology of the arterial waveform and the precise values of systolic and diastolic blood pressure vary throughout the arterial system. Pressure waveforms recorded simultaneously from different sites have different morphologies

due to the physical characteristics of the vascular tree, a phenomenon known as distal pulse amplification<sup>130,131</sup> (Fig. 32.26). As the pressure wave travels toward the periphery, the arterial upstroke becomes steeper, the systolic peak rises, the dicrotic notch appears later, the diastolic wave becomes more prominent, and end- diastolic pressure falls. As a result, peripheral arterial waveforms have higher systolic, lower diastolic, and wider pulse pressures compared with central aortic waveforms. Interestingly, the displayed MAP is only slightly increased.

### Abnormal Arterial Pressure Waveforms

The morphologic features of individual arterial pressure waveforms can provide important diagnostic information (Table 32.1; Fig. 32.29A–D). Aortic stenosis produces a fixed obstruction to ejection, resulting in reduced stroke volume and a slowed rate of ejection. As a result, the waveform is small in amplitude ( _pulsus parvus_ ), has a slowly rising systolic upstroke ( _pulsus tardus_ ), and a delayed peak in systole (see Fig. 32.29B). A distinct shoulder, termed the anacrotic notch, often distorts the pressure upstroke and the dicrotic notch may not be discernible. These features may make the arterial pressure waveform appear overdamped. In aortic regurgitation, the arterial pressure wave displays a sharp rise, wide pulse pressure, and low diastolic pressure owing to diastolic runoff both antegrade and retrograde back into the LV. The arterial pressure pulse may have two systolic peaks ( _bisferiens pulse_ ), with the first peak resulting from antegrade ejection and the second from a wave reflected from the periphery (see Fig. 32.29C). In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the waveform assumes a peculiar bifid shape termed a “spike- and- dome” configuration. After an initial sharp blood pressure increase resulting from rapid, early systolic ejection, arterial pressure abruptly falls as mid- systolic left ventricular outflow obstruction interrupts stroke volume ejection. This is finally followed by a second, late- systolic increase associated with arrival of reflected waves from the periphery (see Fig. 32.29D).

Volume expansion does not result in a dichotomous outcome and the asymmetric nature of the Frank– Starling curve dictates that the cost–benefit ratio of acting in one direction will be different from acting in the other. For any given change in preload, the change in stroke volume depends on the direction of the preload change, with that direction being dependent on how close to the peak of the curve the patient is at the time of measurement. Consequently, the concept of a “gray zone” has been proposed that defines a range of values between which evidencebased decision- making is not possible.<sup>158</sup> For PPV, this zone appears to be 9%–13% such that those above 13% should receive volume expansion while those below 9% should not. Because PPV’s availability to bedside clinicians has facilitated its adoption into routine practice, it is especially important to appreciate the shortcomings of all respiratory- induced dynamic indicators and the clinical conditions under which they were originally studied and validated.<sup>160,161</sup> While PPV appears to be valid in the setting of liver failure, it is significantly affected by increased abdominal pressure, use of vasopressors, and patient position, including steep Trendelenburg, prone, or lateral decubitus.<sup>146,150,162–164</sup> Variability in diastolic filling causing variable stroke volume makes irregular heart rhythms highly problematic in the use of all dynamic preload reserve indicators.<sup>165,166</sup> Patients with severely impaired ventricular function pose a challenge as a fluid challenge does not consistently result in an increase in stroke volume. increased compliance of the myocardium and the chest wall in younger patients.<sup>168,169</sup> It has even been suggested that tachypnea (especially in the setting of respiratory failure), or significant bradycardia may disrupt the relationship between respiratory cycle–induced changes in intrathoracic pressure and cardiac chamber volumes, thus invalidating the theoretical basis for blood pressure variation analysis.<sup>146</sup>

Of special importance, though, is the profound effect of protective lung ventilation on the predictive power of respiratory- induced measures to identify a patient with residual preload reserve.<sup>170–172</sup> Mechanical ventilation with 8–10 mL/kg tidal volume, positive end- expiratory pressure ≥5 mm Hg, regular cardiac rhythm, normal intraabdominal pressure, and a closed chest are necessary to duplicate the experimental conditions under which most of these indices have been validated. These differ significantly from lung protective ventilation strategies, particularly the tidal volume limits, and clinical trials have found waveform analysis to be of limited utility in this setting.<sup>170–172</sup> However, measuring PPV or SVV during or immediately following a recruitment maneuver increased its sensitivity and specificity in predicting fluid responsiveness, although a broader gray zone of up to 26% should be considered.<sup>173,174</sup>

As noted above, respiratory cycle–induced arterial pressure variations are dependent on changes in LV preload and, to a lesser degree, afterload. Indeed, decreased arterial compliance and the resultant increased pulse pressure that accompanies both normal and pathologic vascular aging results in an exaggerated PPV response to any change in stroke volume. As such, it is possible that PPV thresholds should be higher in such patients than in those with more normal arterial compliance.<sup>175,176</sup>

## Central Venous Pressure Monitoring

Cannulation of a central vein and direct measurement of central venous pressure (CVP) are frequently performed in hemodynamically unstable patients and those undergoing major operations. A central venous catheter may be inserted to provide secure vascular access for administration of vasoactive drugs or fluids, CVP monitoring, transvenous cardiac pacing, temporary hemodialysis, pulmonary artery catheterization for more comprehensive cardiac monitoring, or aspiration of entrained air in patients at risk for venous air emboli. A central venous catheter may also be inserted when no peripheral access can be obtained, or when repeated venous blood sampling is required (Box 32.5).

The decision to perform central venous cannulation before or after induction of anesthesia is guided most often by individual patient and physician preferences or institutional practice.

The length of catheter inserted to position the catheter tip properly in the superior vena cava will vary according to puncture site, being slightly (3 to 5 cm) greater when the left internal or external jugular veins are chosen, compared with the right internal jugular vein.<sup>180</sup> Finally, a physician’s personal experience undoubtedly plays a significant role in determining the safest site for central venous cannulation, particularly when the procedure is performed under urgent or emergent circumstances.

strongly recommended for internal jugular vein cannulation and should be considered for subclavian or femoral venous access.<sup>181–183</sup> The reader is referred to other sources for detailed descriptions and tutorials on the various insertion techniques for different access sites.<sup>179,184–186</sup> Regardless of the insertion technique used or the cannulation site chosen, certain general principles should be emphasized. Ideally, a protocol or checklist describing the basic procedural steps for central line insertion should be in place at every institution, and all staff members should feel empowered to speak up when they witness a protocol violation. Standardized equipment, routine use of an assistant, hand washing, and maximal barrier precautions all contribute to the sterility of the procedure.<sup>182</sup> The use of real- time ultrasound guidance for vessel localization and venipuncture should be routine or at least strongly considered, especially when the internal jugular vein site is selected. Waveform manometry or pressure measurement should be used to confirm venous placement of the catheter before use. Finally, the position of the catheter tip should be verified as soon as clinically appropriate to avoid delayed complications.

### COMPLICATIONS OF CENTRAL VENOUS PRESSURE MONITORING

Although immediate complications are infrequent when central venous cannulation is performed by well- trained, experienced clinicians, serious complications may still occur, with as many as 15% of patients experiencing some sort of related adverse event.<sup>187,188</sup> Complications are often divided into mechanical, thromboembolic, and infectious etiologies (Box 32.6).

### Mechanical Complications of Central Venous Catheterization

The incidence of complications depends on a number of factors including the catheter insertion site and the patient’s medical condition. Large retrospective and observational studies provide the best estimates of incidence and frequency.

Vascular injuries from central venous catheterization have a range of clinical consequences. The most common minor complications are localized hematoma or injury to the venous valves.<sup>189</sup> More serious complications include perforation into the pleural space or mediastinum, resulting in hydrothorax, hemothorax, hydromediastinum, hemomediastinum, and or chylothorax.<sup>190–196</sup>

In general, unintended arterial puncture is the most common acute mechanical complication, ranging from 1.9% to 15%.<sup>197</sup> Many of these injuries result in localized hematoma formation, but on rare occasions even smallgauge needle punctures may lead to serious complications such as arterial thromboembolism.<sup>198</sup> Delayed vascular complications following central venous catheterization are uncommon but should be considered as consequences of this procedure. A number of these have been described in the literature, including aorto- atrial fistula, venobronchial fistula, carotid artery–internal jugular vein fistula, and pseudoaneurysm formation.<sup>199–202</sup>

### Thromboembolic Complications of Central Venous Catheterization

Catheter- related thrombosis varies according to the site of central venous catheterization, occurring in as many as

21.5% of patients with femoral venous catheters and 1.9% of those with subclavian venous catheters.<sup>210</sup> Catheters that are positioned low in the right atrium may be more prone to thrombus formation, possibly due to mechanical irritation of the right atrial endocardium by the catheter.<sup>211</sup> Thrombi that form at the catheter tip or adhere to the endocardium have the potential to become a nidus for infection, cause superior vena cava syndrome, or embolize into the pulmonary circulation.<sup>212–214</sup> Occasionally, surgical removal is required.<sup>215</sup>

In addition to thromboembolism, other reported embolic complications of central venous catheterization include embolism of portions of the catheter or guidewire, and air embolism.<sup>216,217</sup> Almost invariably, these are the result of misuse of equipment, highlighting the need for proper education and ongoing training.

### Infectious Complications of Central Venous Catheterization

By far, the most common major late complication of central venous cannulation is infection. Major progress has been made in the control of central line–associated bloodstream infections (CLABSIs), likely due to a focus on evidence- based best practices for catheter insertion and maintenance.<sup>218</sup> In fact, CLABSI rates declined by about 50% between 2008 and 2016.<sup>219</sup> Unfortunately, CLABSI rates increased by up to 91% during the COVID- 19 pandemic, especially in smaller community hospitals compared to large academic systems.<sup>220</sup> The majority of infections occur in inpatient wards and outpatients receiving hemodialysis, but approximately one- third of these cases still occur in the intensive care unit, a cohort that likely includes most of the catheters placed intraoperatively.<sup>219,221,222</sup>

As previously noted, the starting point for prevention of infection is meticulous attention to aseptic technique.<sup>223</sup> Multilumen catheters may carry a higher risk of infection than single- lumen catheters although the added clinical functionality of such catheters often mandates their use.<sup>188,224</sup> Catheters vary in their composition and surface characteristics, which may influence bacterial adherence.<sup>225</sup> Heparin- bonded central venous catheters have been shown to reduce the incidence of catheter- related thrombosis and infection in children and adults.<sup>226,227</sup> The incorporation of antimicrobial treatments such as silver (this metal has broad antimicrobial activity and is nontoxic), combinations of the antiseptics chlorhexidine and silver sulfadiazine, or the antibiotics minocycline and rifampin onto the catheter surfaces have been shown to reduce rates of catheter colonization and in some cases bloodstream infection.<sup>227–229</sup> The added expense has prevented more widespread adoption of these catheters, although an analysis has suggested their cost- effectiveness in settings in which the rate of catheterrelated infections remains high (more than 3.3 per 1000 catheter days).<sup>230</sup>

### Other Complications of Central Venous Catheterization

Although many complications of CVP monitoring relate to equipment misuse, the frequency of complications caused by data misinterpretation remains unknown. It is extremely likely, however, that clinicians misinterpret CVP measurements and have suboptimal understanding of CVP monitoring, just as has been demonstrated repeatedly for pulmonary artery catheter monitoring (see discussion later). Safe and effective use of CVP monitoring requires a detailed understanding of cardiovascular physiology, normal CVP waveforms, and common pathologic abnormalities in these measurements.

### PHYSIOLOGIC CONSIDERATIONS FOR CENTRAL VENOUS PRESSURE MONITORING

Cardiac filling pressures are measured directly from a number of sites in the vascular system. Central venous pressure monitoring is the least invasive method, followed by pulmonary artery and left atrial pressure (LAP) monitoring. Proper interpretation of all cardiac filling pressures requires knowledge of normal values for pressures in the cardiac chambers, great vessels, and other measured and derived hemodynamic variables (Table 32.2).

CVP is determined by the interaction of the venous return function of the circulatory system and cardiac function.<sup>235</sup> An increase in cardiac function with an increase in venous return will result in an increase in CO and rise in CVP. An increase in cardiac function without an increase in venous return will result in an increase in CO and a fall in CVP. In other words, an isolated CVP measurement has very little meaning unless the information is interpreted in the context of some estimation of cardiac function.

### CVP and Venous Return

The relationship among the multiple variables that affect blood return to the right atrium is complex. In short, venous return is mostly determined by the gradient between the _mean circulatory filling pressure_ (MCFP) and CVP.<sup>141</sup> MCFP results from the elastic recoil pressure from distended small veins and venules and is the force that drives blood back to the right atrium.<sup>235</sup> This pressure has been estimated to be between 8 and 10 mm Hg in healthy individuals at rest, but it cannot be measured in the clinical setting.<sup>236</sup> MCFP rises when volume is administered intravenously, but also when venous tone changes in response to venoconstrictors, endogenous catecholamines, or when there is a shift of blood volume from the splanchnic system into the systemic

circulation.<sup>141</sup> The CVP, typically 2 to 3 mm Hg in healthy individuals, is the downstream pressure. Two important corollaries emerge: right atrial pressure is key for maintaining CO, and the body will compensate through the mechanisms described above and others to preserve venous return. This explains why a patient may lose 10% to 12% of circulating blood volume without exhibiting changes in blood pressure or CVP. Secondly, the difference between MCFP and CVP is only 6 to 8 mm Hg, and hence small changes in CVP may have profound hemodynamic consequences.

### CVP and Cardiac Function

The exact same amount of blood returning to the heart can result in very different CVP values at different cardiac function states. This may be explained by the classic diastolic pressure–volume relationship. This curve is one limb of a pressure–volume loop that describes the relation between pressure and volume for the left or right ventricle during an entire cardiac cycle. When a ventricle is operating along the flat portion of its diastolic filling curve, it will exhibit only a small increase in filling pressure (CVP in the case of the right ventricle) after significant increases in filling volume or preload. The same increase in filling volume causes a significant increase in filling pressure when the ventricle is operating on the steep portion of its curve.<sup>237</sup> An even more confusing situation arises when the diastolic pressure–volume relation of the ventricle changes,

### CVP and Intrathoracic Pressure

Changes in intrathoracic and intrapericardial pressures further complicate interpretation of CVP.<sup>237,239</sup> Like all measured intravascular pressures, CVP is referenced to ambient atmospheric pressure. Thus, a cardiac filling pressure of 10 mm Hg is 10 mm Hg higher than ambient atmospheric pressure. To determine whether this pressure accurately reflects the end- diastolic distending force across the atrial wall, one needs to consider transmural pressure. The cardiac chambers are all contained within the pericardium and thorax. Changes in pressure surrounding the heart will influence pressures recorded within the heart. Transmural pressure is the difference between chamber pressure and juxtacardiac or pericardial pressure. This transmural pressure determines resting myocardial fiber length, end- diastolic volume, and thus ventricular preload. The same measured filling pressure, referenced to atmospheric pressure, can be associated with markedly different transmural pressures and chamber volumes, depending on whether juxtacardiac pressure is high or low. Because juxtacardiac pressure is not usually known, measured central vascular pressures may poorly reflect true transmural pressure.<sup>237,239</sup>

During spontaneous breathing, inspiration decreases pleural and pericardial pressures, which are transmitted, in part, to the right atrium, and result in a lower measured CVP. Although measured CVP decreases during the inspiratory phase of spontaneous ventilation, transmural CVP, the difference between right atrial pressure and juxtacardiac pressure, increases slightly as more blood is drawn into the right atrium. The opposite pattern is observed during positive pressure ventilation, in which inspiration increases intrathoracic pressure and the measured CVP, but decreases transmural CVP, because the elevated intrathoracic pressure reduces venous return. Consequently, in order to provide the best estimate of transmural pressure, end- expiratory values should be recorded. At the end of expiration, intrathoracic and juxtacardiac pressures approach atmospheric pressure regardless of ventilatory status and will have the least influence on measured CVP. The influence of changing intrathoracic pressure during the respiratory cycle is easily observed by visual inspection

of the CVP waveform on a calibrated monitor screen or paper recording.

The second prerequisite for interpretation of CVP values is correct measurement. Details about transducer zeroing and leveling are discussed elsewhere in this text (see Technical Aspects of Direct Blood Pressure Measurement, earlier). For accurate measurement of CVP, the zero reference point (the affixed stopcock rather than the transducer) should be aligned at approximately 5 cm below the sternal angle to identify the uppermost fluid level in the right atrium or one- third of the thoracic anteroposterior dimension to identify the mid- right atrial level. While common practice is to use the mid- thoracic level to align CVP transducers, these other reference points allow better estimation of the true right atrial pressure and may be utilized in both supine or head- elevated patients.<sup>126,240,241</sup> Regardless of the position chosen to level the transducer, it is most important to be consistent throughout the monitoring period to maintain the same reference point.

This normal physiologic delay reflects the times required for the spread of the electrical depolarization through the ventricle (≈60 ms), isovolumic left ventricular contraction (≈60 ms), transmission of aortic pressure rise to the radial artery (≈50 ms), and transmission of the radial artery pressure rise through fluidfilled tubing to the transducer (≈10 ms).<sup>128,246</sup>

The normal CVP peaks are designated systolic (c, v) or diastolic (a) according to the phase of the cardiac cycle in which the wave begins. However, one generally identifies these waves not by their onset or upstroke, but rather by the location of their peaks. For instance, the a wave generally begins and peaks in end- diastole, but the peak may appear delayed to coincide with the ECG R wave, especially in a patient with a short PR interval. In this instance, a and c waves merge, and this composite wave is termed an a–c wave. Designation of the CVP v wave as a systolic event may be even more confusing. Although the ascent of the v wave begins during late systole, the peak of the v wave occurs during isovolumic ventricular relaxation, immediately prior to atrioventricular valve opening and the y descent. Consequently, the most precise description would be that the v wave begins in late systole, but peaks during isovolumic ventricular relaxation, the earliest portion of diastole. For clinical purposes, it is simplest to consider the v wave to be a systolic wave.

Although three distinct CVP peaks (a, c, v) and two troughs (x, y) are discernible in the normal venous pressure trace, heart rate changes and conduction abnormalities alter this pattern. A short ECG PR interval causes fusion of a and c waves, and tachycardia reduces the length of diastole and the duration of the y descent, causing v and a waves to merge. In contrast, bradycardia causes each wave to become more distinct with separate x and x′ descents visible and a more prominent h wave. Although there are circumstances in which other pathologic waves may be evident in the CVP trace, one should resist the temptation to assign physiologic significance to each small pressure peak, as many will arise as artifacts of fluid- filled tubing- transducer monitoring systems. It is more useful to search for the expected waveform components, including those waveforms that are characteristic of the pathologic conditions suspected.

These patterns are interpreted best in conjunction with pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) monitoring, which is discussed below.

Perhaps the most important traditional application of CVP monitoring is to provide an estimate of the adequacy of the circulating blood volume. Several randomized trials and systematic reviews have demonstrated a very poor relationship between CVP and circulating blood volume,

as well as the inability of a static CVP value to predict the hemodynamic response to a fluid challenge.<sup>249–251</sup> This is not surprising in light of the physiologic factors described above. Consequently, other monitoring techniques have supplanted CVP monitoring to identify patients that will benefit from fluid administration.

## Pulmonary Artery Catheter Monitoring

In 1970, Swan, Ganz, and colleagues introduced pulmonary artery catheterization into clinical practice for the hemodynamic assessment of patients with acute myocardial infarction.<sup>252</sup> This device allowed accurate bedside measurement of important cardiovascular physiologic variables, and its popularity soared. While the PAC provides measurements of several hemodynamic variables that clinicians find hard to accurately predict from standard clinical signs and symptoms,<sup>253</sup> it remains uncertain whether PAC monitoring leads to improved patient outcomes.<sup>254</sup>

### PULMONARY ARTERY CATHETER INSERTION

The standard pulmo nary artery catheter (PAC) has a 7.0 to 9.0 Fr circumference, is 110 cm in length marked at 10- cm intervals, and contains four internal lumens. The distal port at the catheter tip is used for PAP monitoring, while the second is 30 cm more proximal and is used for CVP monitoring. The third lumen leads to a balloon near the tip which is used to float the catheter through the cardiac chambers, and the fourth houses wires for a temperature thermistor, the end of which lies just proximal to the balloon.<sup>255</sup> Contemporary PACs also have an additional lumen that allows continuous measurement of mixed venous oximetry at the catheter tip as well as a heating coil between the proximal and distal lumens that is used for continuous CO measurement.

Pulmonary artery catheters can be placed from any of the central venous cannulation sites described earlier, but the right internal jugular vein provides the most direct route to the right heart chambers. The balloon at the tip of the catheter is inflated with air, and the catheter is advanced into the right atrium, through the tricuspid valve, the right ventricle, the pulmonic valve, into the pulmonary artery, and finally into the wedge position. Characteristic waveforms from each of these locations confirm proper catheter passage and placement (Fig. 32.36). Length of insertion to reach the pulmonary artery and wedge position are directly related to the height of the patient and will be reduced in shorter patients.

After the pulmonary artery wedge pressure is measured, the balloon is deflated, and the PAP waveform should reappear. Catheter position is confirmed with a chest radiograph. The tip of the PAC should be within 2 cm of the cardiac silhouette on a standard anteroposterior chest film.<sup>256</sup>

If a right ventricular waveform is not observed after inserting the catheter 40 cm, coiling in the right atrium is likely. Similarly, if a pulmonary artery waveform is not observed after inserting the catheter to 50 cm, coiling in the right ventricle has probably occurred. The balloon should be deflated, the catheter withdrawn to 20 cm, and the PAC floating sequence repeated.

A few additional points aid successful positioning of the PAC. The air- filled balloon tends to float to nondependent regions as it passes through the heart into the pulmonary vasculature. Consequently, positioning a patient head down will aid flotation across the tricuspid valve, and tilting the patient onto the right side and placing the head up will encourage flotation out of the right ventricle, as well as reduce the incidence of arrhythmias during insertion.<sup>257,258</sup> Deep inspiration during spontaneous ventilation will increase venous return and right ventricular output transiently, and may facilitate catheter flotation in a patient with low CO. On occasion, a catheter may be floated to proper position when stiffened by injecting 10 to 20 mL of ice- cold solution through the distal lumen. In addition, counterclockwise rotation with the balloon inflated during insertion may help catheter flotation into the right atrium and through the tricuspid valve. Maneuvering of the PAC can also be guided by transesophageal or transthoracic echocardiography, demonstrating catheter passage through the right heart.<sup>259,260</sup>

### COMPLICATIONS OF PULMONARY ARTERY CATHETER MONITORING

Complications of PAC use may be divided into those resulting from catheter placement, those associated with the _in vivo_ presence of the catheter, and those resulting from catheter use and misuse. For the most part, problems encountered during catheter placement are the same for both PAC and CVP monitoring (see Box 32.6). However, catheterization of the right ventricle and pulmonary artery causes complications uniquely associated with PACs<sup>261</sup> (Box 32.7).

When all adverse effects from PAC use are considered, including self- limited arrhythmias observed during catheter insertion, it appears that minor complications occur in more than 50% of catheterized patients.<sup>262</sup> However, major morbidity specifically attributable to PAC use is uncommon.<sup>263</sup> In both an initial exhaustive review of the literature and in its 2003 update, the American Society of Anesthesiologists Task Force on Pulmonary Artery Catheterization emphasized that the reported incidence of complications from PAC monitoring varies widely, although it seems that serious complications occur in 0.1% to 0.5% of PAC- monitored surgical patients.<sup>262</sup> In 1984, Shah _et al._ reported use of PACs in 6245 patients undergoing cardiac

and noncardiac operations.<sup>197</sup> Quite remarkably, only 10 patients (0.16%) had serious complications resulting in morbidity and only 1 patient (0.016%) died as a result of pulmonary artery catheterization. Furthermore, a 1998 European report of PAC use in 5306 patients undergoing cardiac surgery confirms this low incidence of major morbidity, with injury of the right ventricle or pulmonary artery occurring in only 4 patients (0.07%).<sup>264</sup> Finally, only 1 of 2000 adverse events reported in the Australian Incident Monitoring Study of 1993 involved use of a PAC, in contrast to 64 adverse events involving access to the arterial or venous systems.<sup>108</sup> However, it is important to acknowledge that although these large studies indicate a low incidence of serious complications attributable to the use of PACs, the frequency of complications in a particular clinical setting or patient group remains unknown.

A more insidious but possibly more common complication of PAC use is misinterpretation of data.<sup>265,266</sup> Although the magnitude of the problem is not clear, studies show widespread knowledge deficits among practitioners who use PACs. ### NORMAL PULMONARY ARTERY PRESSURES AND WAVEFORMS

As the balloon- tipped PAC is floated to its proper position in the pulmonary artery, characteristic pressure waveforms are recorded (see Fig. 32.36). In the superior vena cava or right atrium, a CVP waveform with characteristic a, c, and v waves and low mean pressure should be observed. Right ventricular pressure is characterized by a rapid systolic upstroke, a wide pulse pressure, and low diastolic pressure. Entry of the PAC into the pulmonary artery is heralded by a step- up in diastolic pressure and a change in waveform morphology.

On occasion, it may be difficult to distinguish right ventricular pressure from PAP, particularly if only the numeric values for these pressures are examined. However, careful observation of the pressure waveforms, focusing on the diastolic pressure contours, allows differentiation. During diastole, the PAP will fall owing to continuous runoff flow to the lung, while the pressure in the right ventricle will increase due to filling from the right atrium<sup>272</sup> (see Fig. 32.36).

As noted above, the wedge pressure is an indirect measurement of pulmonary venous pressure and LAP and should therefore resemble these venous waveforms with characteristic a and v waves and x and y descents. However, owing to the pulmonary vascular bed interposed between the PAC tip and left atrium, wedge pressure is a delayed and damped representation of LAP.<sup>273</sup>

The terms pulmonary artery wedge pressure and pulmonary artery occlusion pressure are used interchangeably and refer to the same measurement obtained from the tip of a PAC following balloon inflation and flotation to the wedged position. However, pulmonary capillary pressure must not be confused with wedge pressure or LAP, nor should the term _pulmonary capillary wedge pressure_ be used at all. The hydrostatic pressure in the pulmonary capillaries that causes edema formation according to the Starling equation is different from LAP. This is the pressure that must exceed LAP in order to maintain antegrade blood flow through the lungs. Although the magnitude of the difference between pulmonary capillary pressure and wedge pressure is generally small, it can increase markedly when resistance to flow in the pulmonary veins is elevated.<sup>274</sup> In most situations, the major component of pulmonary vascular resistance occurs at the precapillary, pulmonary arteriolar level. However, rare conditions like pulmonary veno- occlusive disease may cause a marked increase in postcapillary resistance to

flow. Similar situations arise in conditions that disproportionately increase pulmonary venous resistance, such as central nervous system injury, acute lung injury, hypovolemic shock, endotoxemia, and norepinephrine infusion.<sup>275,276</sup> Under these conditions, measurement of wedge pressure will underestimate pulmonary capillary pressure substantially and thereby underestimate the risk of hydrostatic pulmonary edema.

### ABNORMAL PULMONARY ARTERY AND WEDGE PRESSURE WAVEFORMS

Pulmonary artery catheter monitoring is subject to the same technical artifacts inherent in all invasive pressure monitoring techniques as well as some additional problems unique to this method.<sup>277–279</sup> Because the PAC is longer and passes through the cardiac chambers, it is more prone to distortions from clot or air bubbles, and motion- related artifacts are more problematic. Artifactual pressure spikes may be distinguished from the underlying physiologic pressure waveform by their unique morphology and timing.

At the onset of systole, tricuspid valve closure accompanied by right ventricular contraction and ejection result in excessive catheter motion causing the most common PAC trace artifact.<sup>278,280</sup> This pressure artifact may produce an artificially low pressure, erroneously designated as the pulmonary artery diastolic pressure (Fig. 32.37). Repositioning the PAC often solves the problem.

Another common artifact in PAC pressure measurement occurs when the balloon is overinflated and occludes the distal lumen orifice. This phenomenon is termed overwedging and usually is caused by distal catheter migration and eccentric balloon inflation that forces the catheter tip against the vessel wall. The catheter now records a gradually rising, nonpulsatile pressure as the continuous flush system builds up pressure against the obstructed distal opening (Fig. 32.38). When observed, this should be corrected immediately by gentle catheter withdrawal to a more proximal location in the pulmonary artery.

As emphasized earlier, with each PAC balloon inflation and wedge measurement, the catheter tip migrates distally. When a wedge pressure tracing appears during partial balloon inflation, it suggests that the PAC is inappropriately located in a smaller, distal branch of the pulmonary artery. The catheter should be withdrawn slightly before overwedging can occur and result in vascular injury or pulmonary infarction.

Pathophysiologic conditions involving the left- sided cardiac chambers or valves produce characteristic changes in the pulmonary artery and wedge pressure waveforms. One of the most easily recognized patterns is the tall v wave of mitral regurgitation. Unlike a normal wedge pressure v wave produced by late systolic pulmonary venous inflow, the prominent v wave of mitral regurgitation begins in early systole. The CVP waveform in right ventricular infarction is similar to that from a patient with restrictive cardiomyopathy or pericardial constriction, including elevated mean pressure, prominent a and v waves, and steep x and y descents.<sup>291</sup> The cardinal feature common to these conditions is impaired right ventricular diastolic compliance, often termed “restrictive physiology.” In restrictive cardiomyopathy and right ventricular infarction, diastolic dysfunction impairs ventricular relaxation and decreases chamber compliance, whereas in constrictive pericarditis cardiac filling is limited by the rigid, often calcified pericardial shell. This results in a marked increase in CVP and a reduced diastolic volume, stroke volume, and CO. In tamponade, the venous pressure waveform appears more monophasic and is dominated by the systolic x pressure descent. The diastolic y pressure descent is attenuated or absent, because early diastolic flow from right atrium to right ventricle is impaired by the surrounding compressive pericardial fluid collection<sup>292,296,297</sup> (Fig. 32.44). ### PHYSIOLOGIC CONSIDERATIONS FOR PULMONARY ARTERY CATHETER MONITORING: PREDICTION OF LEFT VENTRICULAR PRELOAD

One reason to measure pulmonary artery diastolic and wedge pressures is to be able to estimate LV end- diastolic pressure, the closest surrogate to LV end- diastolic volume, which is the true LV preload. When a PAC floats to the wedge position, the inflated balloon isolates the distal pressure- monitoring orifice from upstream pulmonary artery pressure. A continuous static column of blood now connects the wedged PAC tip to the junction of the pulmonary veins and left atrium. Thus, wedging the PAC, in effect, extends the catheter tip to measure the pressure at the point at which blood flow resumes on the venous side of the pulmonary circuit. Because resistance in the large pulmonary veins is negligible, pulmonary artery wedge pressure provides an indirect measurement of both pulmonary venous pressure and LAP.<sup>302,303</sup> For the column of blood connecting the tip of the wedged catheter and the draining pulmonary vein to be continuous, however, external compression by surrounding alveoli should be negligible (i.e., the catheter needs to reside in the so- called West zone 3 of the lung)<sup>303</sup> (Fig. 32.46).

Pulmonary artery diastolic pressure (PADP) is often used as an alternative to PAWP to estimate left ventricular filling pressure. This is acceptable under normal circumstances because when pulmonary venous resistance is low, the pressure in the pulmonary artery at end of diastole will equilibrate with downstream pressure in the pulmonary veins and left atrium.<sup>304,305</sup> From a monitoring standpoint, PADP has the added advantage of being available for continuous monitoring whereas PAWP is only measured intermittently.

In many situations, left ventricular end- diastolic pressure can either be underestimated or overestimated by the PAWP and/or PADP. These situations are summarized in Fig. 32.47 and Tables 32.5 and 32.6. The interested reader is referred to several excellent references for further discussion of this topic.<sup>302,303,306</sup>

However, even when pulmonary artery diastolic pressure and wedge pressure accurately estimate left ventricular end- diastolic pressure, many factors can influence the relation between end- diastolic pressure and end- diastolic chamber volume, which is the true preload. Proper interpretation of filling pressures requires assessment of juxtacardiac pressure as well as ventricular compliance. When both are normal, a wedge pressure of 20 mm Hg is interpreted as hypervolemia, with an increased left ventricular enddiastolic volume causing the increased PAWP. However, if juxtacardiac pressure is increased (e.g., due to cardiac tamponade, pericardial constriction, or positive pressure ventilation), or ventricular compliance is decreased (e.g., with myocardial ischemia, hypertrophy, or cardiomyopathy), a wedge pressure of 20 mm Hg can coexist with a small, hypovolemic left ventricle (Fig. 32.48).

### PULMONARY ARTERY CATHETERIZATION: INDICATIONS AND OUTCOME CONTROVERSY

The PAC allows continuous tracking of hemodynamic variables, which seems particularly valuable in critically ill high- risk patients with circulatory dysfunction. Measurement of CO (see below section on Thermodilution Cardiac Output Monitoring) can separate shock states into hypovolemic etiology (low CO with low filling pressures), cardiogenic etiology (low CO and high filling pressures), and distributive etiology (high CO and low SVR). Through measurement of CO and left and right filling pressures (PAWP and CVP, respectively), it can separate out predominantly left or right ventricular dysfunction or global dysfunction. For right ventricular dysfunction, the PAC allows distinction of right ventricular dysfunction predominantly related to increased afterload (high PAP) versus dysfunction related mostly to pump failure (high CVP and low PAP).<sup>314</sup>

Despite these advantages, pulmonary artery catheterization has stimulated a great deal of vigorous controversy.<sup>315,316</sup> It is an expensive, invasive technique that is widely used but still not proven to improve patient outcomes. This controversy was much fueled by a study published by Connors and associates in 1996, showing that PAC- monitored patients in the intensive care unit had 20%

increased mortality, increased length of hospital stay, and incurred increased costs.<sup>317</sup> The publication of this study was accompanied by a strongly worded editorial calling for a moratorium on PAC use or a randomized controlled trial to define its efficacy.<sup>318</sup>

Surrounding that time, and in subsequent years, several large, randomized, adequately powered studies have been published regarding the use of the PAC in various settings: general noncardiac surgery,<sup>254</sup> vascular surgery,<sup>319</sup> coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) surgery,<sup>320</sup> nonsurgical patients with congestive heart failure,<sup>321</sup> patients with acute lung injury,<sup>322</sup> and critically ill patients in the intensive care unit.<sup>323</sup> Generally, these studies have shown no benefit to PAC use, but also no increase in mortality or in hospital or intensive care unit length of stay.

One common drawback to most of these large, randomized studies is that they have necessarily examined the routine use of PAC and enrolled a sequential cohort of patients, most of them with a relatively moderate risk of death or complications. Also, not all of them have employed a specific therapeutic intervention protocol.<sup>324</sup> Indeed, in especially high- risk patients, marked either by old age, severe comorbidity or increased disease acuity, several recent nonrandomized studies were indeed able to demonstrate a clinical benefit from PAC use.<sup>325–329</sup>

The most recent recommendations pertaining to the perioperative use of PACs are the American Society of Anesthesiologists practice guidelines published in 2003.<sup>262</sup> The task force considered PAC monitoring to be appropriate in highrisk surgical patients undergoing high-risk procedures. Furthermore, the specific practice setting as well as the proficiency and experience of clinicians should be considered.

PAC use must be tailored to the degree of risk for the patient and the risk posed by the procedure itself. For example, a patient with advanced ischemic cardiomyopathy who needs lower extremity amputation under regional anesthesia would not warrant PAC monitoring while a patient with stable ischemic heart disease scheduled for extensive abdominal cancer resection may benefit from perioperative use. Furthermore, practice setting must be considered such as the operator’s technical skill, knowledge, and experience in PAC use.<sup>262</sup>

A reasonable conclusion from the bulk of published research would be that PAC use should be limited, although a moratorium on use is ill- advised. Indeed, data demonstrate a significant and continuing decrease in PAC usage.<sup>330</sup> Use should be reserved to centers with significant experience and expertise. The PAC generally should be used to monitor and guide therapy in patients at high risk for hemodynamic instability, those who are judged more critically ill by a variety of clinical means, and those who are in shock, especially if elderly and suffering from other systemic diseases.

Obviously, the PAC itself has no potential for benefit unless it guides therapies that improve patient outcomes. Future research should focus on defining subgroups of patients who might benefit from use of the PAC, as well as defining effective therapeutic interventions based on the hemodynamic information gained from the PAC. Heart failure patients in cardiogenic shock are one such population for which data from clinical registries suggest benefit despite lack of confirmatory evidence from randomized trials.<sup>331,332</sup>

Recently, the technology to continuously measure oxygen saturation has been incorporated into central venous catheters as well. These catheters measure central venous saturation, measured in the superior vena cava. Normally, this saturation is around 70% versus 75% in the pulmonary artery.<sup>334</sup> Low central venous saturation has been associated with increased complications both in trauma patients and in major surgery patients.<sup>335,336</sup>

The real value of measuring venous oxygen saturation lies in its ability to guide therapeutic interventions. Because one of the body’s physiologic compensations for anemia is increased oxygen extraction, low venous hemoglobin saturation has been used to guide the need for blood transfusion.<sup>337</sup> Several studies have used venous hemoglobin saturation to guide interventions aimed at increasing CO: a goal- directed approach. A study in patients undergoing cardiac surgery has shown better outcomes in patients randomized to protocol- driven interventions aimed at achieving mixed venous hemoglobin saturation above 70% (and blood lactate &lt;2 mg/dL).<sup>338</sup> Similarly, optimizing the central venous oxygen saturation has been shown to improve outcome in high-risk noncardiac and off- pump cardiac surgical patients.<sup>339,340</sup> Although an early study showed similar benefit in patients with early sepsis,<sup>341</sup> a more recent study could not confirm these results.<sup>342</sup>

It is important to note that these studies have employed strict protocol- driven therapeutic interventions. In contrast, a large Veterans Affairs observational trial of 3265 cardiac surgical patients noted that 49% of patients received continuous mixed venous oximetry PACs, and use of this catheter was associated with increased cost but no better outcome than the standard PAC group.<sup>343</sup> In this study, however, no protocol was used to guide therapeutic interventions based on monitoring results.

### Right Ventricular Ejection Fraction Pulmonary Artery Catheter

Although cardiovascular monitoring has focused predominantly on left ventricular performance, in some instances right ventricular dysfunction may be the more important factor limiting circulation. Patient populations at increased risk for right ventricular dysfunction include those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, adult respiratory distress syndrome, pulmonary hypertension, and right ventricular ischemia and infarction.<sup>344</sup>

Measurement of right ventricular ejection fraction (RVEF) with a specially designed PAC offers another method for evaluating right ventricular function. This method uses a standard PAC equipped with a rapid response thermistor that detects and quantifies the small changes in pulmonary artery blood temperature that occur with each heartbeat, in a manner somewhat analogous to a standard continuous CO PAC. The CO computer measures the residual fraction of thermal signal following each heart beat and derives the RVEF.<sup>345</sup> Right ventricular end- diastolic volume can be calculated from RVEF and stroke volume, and can be used as an index of right ventricular preload. Clearly, all factors that confound standard thermodilution CO measurement (described later) will also interfere with accurate determination of RVEF. In addition, because the temperature changes measured by the RVEF PAC are small beat- tobeat changes, the method will not work if the ECG R waves

## Cardiac Output Monitoring

Cardiac output is the total blood flow generated by the heart, and in a normal adult at rest ranges from 4.0 to 6.5 L/min. Measurement of CO provides a global assessment of the circulation, and in combination with other hemodynamic measurements (heart rate, arterial blood pressure, CVP, PAP, and wedge pressure), it allows calculation of additional important circulatory variables, such as systemic and pulmonary vascular resistance and ventricular stroke work (see Table 32.7).

Three factors have driven efforts to measure CO in clinical practice. The first is the recognition that in many critically ill patients, low CO leads to significant morbidity and mortality.<sup>350</sup> Secondly, clinical assessment of CO is often inaccurate; for example, seriously ill patients with decreased CO might have normal systemic arterial blood pressures.<sup>351</sup> Finally, newer techniques for CO measurement are becoming less invasive and thus might provide benefit to many patients without the attendant risks of invasive monitoring.<sup>352</sup> The advantages and disadvantages of each technique must be appreciated for proper clinical application.

### THERMODILUTION CARDIAC OUTPUT MONITORING

The thermodilution technique is considered the gold standard for measuring CO because of its ease of implementation and the long clinical experience with its use in various settings. It is a variant of the indicator dilution method, in which a known amount of a tracer substance is injected into the bloodstream and its concentration change is measured over time at a downstream site.<sup>353</sup> For thermodilution, a known volume of room temperature fluid is injected as a bolus into the proximal (right atrium) lumen of the PAC, and the resulting change in the pulmonary artery blood temperature is recorded by the thermistor at the catheter tip. As in all other forms of cardiovascular monitoring, it is important to have a real- time display of the thermodilution curve resulting from each CO measurement.<sup>354</sup> This allows the clinician to discern artifacts that would invalidate the CO measurement, such as unstable blood temperature, recirculation, or incomplete indicator injection.

Usually three CO measurements performed in rapid succession are averaged to provide a more reliable result. If only a single injection is used to determine CO, a difference between sequential CO measurements of 22% is required to suggest a clinically significant change. In contrast, when

three injections are averaged to determine the thermodilution measurement, a change greater than 13% indicates a clinically significant change in CO.<sup>355</sup>

Even when carefully performed, some studies have found that thermodilution CO measurements may not agree with other reference methods.<sup>356,357</sup> However, few complications are directly attributable to the technique itself, and following the trend in CO is probably more clinically useful than emphasizing any absolute value.

### Sources of Error in Thermodilution Cardiac Output Monitoring

Several important technical issues and potential sources of error must be considered to interpret thermodilution CO measurements properly (Box 32.8).<sup>353,354</sup> The thermodilution technique measures RV output. With intracardiac shunt, RV and LV outputs will not be equal.

Patients with tricuspid or pulmonic valve regurgitation pose additional problems for thermodilution CO measurement owing to recirculation of the indicator across the incompetent valve. Although minor degrees of valvular regurgitation have little effect on thermodilution CO monitoring, these values are unreliable, with more severe valvular regurgitation, either underestimated or overestimated, depending on the severity of valvular regurgitation and the magnitude of the CO.<sup>354,358</sup>

Unrecognized fluctuation in blood temperature may also influence CO measurement. In most patients, pulmonary artery blood temperature falls in the initial minutes following cardiopulmonary bypass, due to the rewarmed vascular- and vessel-rich tissues redistributing the heat gained to the less well perfused body core. This causes the thermal baseline to be unstable and measurements made in the minutes following bypass are notoriously unreliable, most often leading to marked underestimation of the true CO.<sup>359</sup> Pulmonary artery blood temperature can also change due to rapid fluid infusion.<sup>360</sup>

One controversy surrounding intermittent bolus thermodilution CO monitoring is the proper timing of measurement in relation to the respiratory cycle, particularly in patients receiving positive pressure mechanical ventilation, because RV stroke output varies as much as 50% during the respiratory cycle. ### Continuous Thermodilution Cardiac Output Monitoring

Newer technologies applied to PAC monitoring allow nearly continuous CO (CCO) monitoring using warm thermal indicator.<sup>353,363</sup> In brief, small quantities of heat are released from a 10- cm thermal filament incorporated into the RV portion of a PAC, approximately 15 to 25 cm from the catheter tip, and the resulting thermal signal is measured by the thermistor at the tip of the catheter in the pulmonary artery. The heating filament is cycled on and off in a pseudorandom binary sequence, and the CO is derived from cross- correlation of the measured pulmonary artery temperature with the known sequence of heating filament activation.<sup>363</sup> Typically, the displayed value for CO is updated every 30 to 60 seconds and represents the average value for the CO measured over the previous 3 to 6 minutes. On the one hand, this will lead to a delayed response during unstable hemodynamic conditions.<sup>364</sup> On the other hand, reproducibility and precision appear to be better compared with the single instantaneous bolus thermodilution technique, especially during positive pressure ventilation.<sup>365,366</sup>

The CCO PAC has been widely accepted into clinical use for a number of practical reasons.<sup>333</sup> Although these catheters are more expensive than standard PACs, obviating the need for bolus injections reduces nursing workload and the potential risk of fluid overload or infection. However, like intermittent bolus thermodilution techniques, warm thermal CCO has certain methodologic pitfalls that must be recognized and avoided. The CCO computer and catheter require a significant amount of time to warm up and may work poorly in an environment where there is a great deal of thermal noise, such as the cardiac operating room. Additionally, the use of pneumatic compression devices may introduce artifacts that appear as large variations (oscillations) in CCO values in otherwise stable patients.<sup>367</sup> As already emphasized, CCO monitors have an inherent 5 to 15 minute delay in responding to abrupt changes in CO, and the magnitude of this delay depends on the type of physiologic perturbation, as well as the CCO computer monitor algorithm.<sup>364</sup> Although modifications of CCO algorithms provide a “STAT CCO” updated every 30 to 60 seconds, acute changes in CO are still detected more slowly by CCO monitoring than by other methods, such as direct arterial pressure or mixed venous oximetry. In effect, the CCO technique involves a fundamental tradeoff between rapid response time and overall accuracy and stability of the displayed value.<sup>365</sup> While it might be useful for clinical decision- making, no studies exist that demonstrate improved patient outcomes from use of the CCO PAC compared to a standard PAC.

### Transpulmonary Thermodilution Cardiac Output

In the early 2000s, the transpulmonary thermodilution technique emerged. This technique provides intermittent measurements of CO using a modified Stewart–Hamilton formula, similar to standard pulmonary artery

thermodilution. For transpulmonary thermodilution measurement, ice- cold saline is injected into a central venous line while the change in temperature is measured in a large peripheral artery (femoral, axillary, or brachial artery) via a special arterial catheter equipped with a thermistor.<sup>368</sup> In contrast to the pulmonary artery thermodilution method, the transpulmonary thermodilution measurement is averaged over several respiratory cycles and thereby obviates the respiratory effects on stroke volume and measured CO.<sup>369</sup> Taking the average of three transpulmonary CO measurements, similar to intermittent bolus PAC CO measurements, is recommended to increase precision.<sup>370</sup> Transpulmonary thermodilution has been validated against pulmonary artery thermodilution and is considered a clinical reference method for CO measurement.<sup>353,371</sup>

Transpulmonary thermodilution devices are less invasive than PAC monitoring but still require insertion of a central venous catheter and a thermistor- tipped arterial catheter in a large peripheral artery. A limitation of transpulmonary thermodilution for measuring CO is that only intermittent measurements can be performed. Devices which use this technique combine transpulmonary thermodilution with pulse contour analysis (discussed below), which allows real- time monitoring of CO. To improve accuracy, the devices calibrate pulse contour analysis CO to the values obtained by transpulmonary thermodilution.<sup>372</sup> Transpulmonary thermodilution is more prone to errors due to drift and indicator recirculation. Other sources of error are the presence of intracardiac or intrapulmonary shunts, and valvular regurgitation.

Mathematical derivation from the transpulmonary thermodilution curve can produce several additional useful indices besides CO: (1) global end- diastolic volume (GEDV) and intrathoracic blood volume (ITBV); (2) cardiac function index (CFI) and global ejection fraction (GEF); (3) extravascular lung water (EVLW); (4) and pulmonary vascular permeability index (PVPI). Both GEDV and ITBV are volumetric parameters of global preload and have been shown to be a better measure of cardiac preload than traditional measurements, such as CVP or PAWP, in several different patient populations.<sup>373–378</sup> CFI and GEF are indicators of cardiac systolic function. In patients with normal and impaired cardiac function, CFI and GEF have been shown to correlate with LV ejection fraction and its response to inotropic therapy.<sup>379–381</sup> EVLW is a measure of pulmonary edema, and has been shown to assist in the diagnosis, and predict progression of acute lung injury and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). EVLW can also be used to guide fluid therapy in patients with acute lung injury or sepsis.<sup>382–386</sup> PVPI is a marker of lung capillary leak. It is the ratio between EVLW and the pulmonary blood volume and can be used to differentiate between cardiogenic and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema.<sup>387</sup>

### TRANSPULMONARY LITHIUM DILUTION CARDIAC OUTPUT

The transpulmonary lithium dilution technique is another CO monitoring method that derives its fundamental basis from indicator dilution principles.<sup>388</sup> In this technique, a small amount of lithium chloride is injected through a peripheral or central venous catheter and changes in

lithium levels in the blood are detected by an ion- selective electrode attached to an arterial catheter. Cardiac output is calculated from analysis of the lithium dilution curve. As with PAC and transpulmonary thermodilution, three measurements should be averaged to achieve good precision.<sup>389</sup> Several studies have shown that this is an accurate technique compared with standard PAC thermodilution.<sup>390–393</sup>

The lithium dilution system allows intermittent measurement of CO with only a radial artery catheter and peripheral intravenous access.<sup>394</sup> To allow for beat- to- beat measurement of CO, the monitor applies an algorithm using pulse power analysis (discussed below and different than pulse contour method) with external reference calibration using transpulmonary lithium dilution. ### MINIMALLY INVASIVE AND NONINVASIVE METHODS FOR MONITORING CARDIAC OUTPUT AND PERFUSION

In recent years, much work has been dedicated to developing minimal or noninvasive methods to measure CO. While it seems that overall agreement between these newer techniques and standard pulmonary artery thermodilution is not very high, they do offer other advantages.<sup>396</sup> Methods to assess or estimate CO can be classified as invasive, minimally invasive, or noninvasive and will be discussed next.<sup>352,397,398</sup>

### Arterial Pulse Waveform Analysis Cardiac Output Monitoring

Many recent developments in CO monitoring have focused on continuous CO measurement derived from analysis of the arterial pressure waveform, generally termed pulse contour analysis or pulse wave analysis. These techniques are based on the principle of ventriculo- arterial coupling, where the arterial pulse pressure and its contour are primarily determined by left ventricular stroke volume and arterial impedance.<sup>398</sup> By analyzing the systolic portion of the arterial pressure curve (from the beginning of systole to the dicrotic notch), stroke volume can be estimated by dividing the integral of the change in pressure over time by the value of aortic impedance. Measurement or estimation of total arterial impedance (a dynamic physical property) is a crucial element for the calculation of stroke volume from the arterial pressure waveform.<sup>399</sup> This variable is measured by pulse wave analysis systems that use either external reference calibration, internal calibration based on biometric and demographic data, or analysis of wave reflections to estimate arterial impedance.

Systems which use external calibration of CO in combination with pulse wave analysis utilize transpulmonary

thermodilution or transpulmonary lithium dilution (mentioned above). Externally calibrated devices tend to be more accurate than internally calibrated or uncalibrated devices but require frequent recalibration.<sup>399</sup> By mathematically analyzing the shape and characteristics of the arterial pressure waveform, and by using biometric and demographic data for internal calibration, continuous cardiac output can be estimated without external reference calibration.<sup>400–402</sup> Most of these minimally invasive systems (that just require an arterial catheter) are optimized to analyze radial arterial pressure waveforms. Each system uses different physiologic assumptions and a unique proprietary algorithm to analyze the arterial pressure waveform. This measurement is then combined with patient biometric and demographic data to estimate stroke volume.<sup>352</sup> Pulse contour analysis systems also provide automatic calculation of dynamic indices of fluid responsiveness such as pulse pressure variation (PPV) and/or stroke volume variation (SVV).<sup>403</sup> (see Arterial Pressure Monitoring and Waveform Analysis for Prediction of Fluid Responsiveness, earlier)

A nonmorphology- based method, called pulse power analysis, uses nomograms that consider the patient’s age, height, and weight to calculate CO. This method is based on the law of conservation of mass/power and assumes that the net power change in the heartbeat is the balance between the input of a mass (stroke volume) of blood minus the blood mass lost to the periphery during the heartbeat.<sup>398</sup> The algorithm looks at the power of the whole pulse contour, not just the systolic area, and is therefore independent of the position of the reflected wave. Autocorrelation, a timebased method, is used to define the beat period and the net power change across the whole beat, and thereby avoids the use of a frequency- based approach (such as Fourier transformation) to measuring power. This reduces the effect of damping on the transducer system since damping changes the frequency response.<sup>404</sup> Externally calibrated (by lithium dilution) and internally calibrated minimally invasive systems are available which use pulse power analysis to derive continuous CO measurements.

Uncalibrated minimally invasive systems estimate CO solely based on arterial pressure waveform features recorded from a radial or femoral arterial catheter. The device’s proprietary algorithm, called the Pressure Recording Analytical Method, uses a high signal- sampling rate to identify specific pressure trace points which represent wave reflections and allow the system to estimate vascular impedance beat- by- beat.<sup>405</sup> The technology has been validated in different clinical settings and has shown good agreement with PA thermodilution.<sup>119,406–408</sup> Measurement errors from under- or overdamping of the arterial pressure waveform signal are one major limitation to this technique.<sup>405</sup>

Pulse contour methods offer the potential for noninvasive, continuous, beat- to- beat CO monitoring utilizing finger cuffs or sensors placed directly over the radial artery (see above text on techniques for continuous noninvasive blood pressure monitoring). The finger cuff method uses vascular unloading technology, also known as the volume clamp method, to record the arterial blood pressure waveform and then estimates CO using pulse contour analysis.<sup>397</sup> This method derives the finger arterial pressure

<!-- PDF page 44 -->

waveform from the cuff pressure that is needed to keep the blood volume in the finger arteries as measured by photoplethysmography constant throughout the cardiac cycle. Different devices scale finger- cuff- derived arterial blood pressure to brachial arterial blood pressure using distinct approaches such as using a heart reference system or using intermittent upper arm cuff oscillometry.<sup>409,410</sup> Although this technique is appealing owing to its noninvasive nature, a recent meta- analysis of studies comparing finger cuff–derived CO measurements with invasive measurements do not show interchangeability in surgical or critically ill patients.<sup>64</sup>

Radial artery applanation tonometry uses a mechanical sensor placed over the radial artery. The sensor maintains a transmural pressure of zero by slightly compressing the radial artery, thereby allowing continuous recording of the arterial blood pressure waveform. The radial artery blood pressure signal recorded using applanation tonometry also needs to be scaled to match brachial arterial pressure.<sup>411</sup> Different systems use unique algorithms and mathematical models that incorporate arterial blood pressure waveform characteristics, biometric, and demographic data to estimate CO. Several small studies have shown reasonable agreement between radial artery applanation tonometry CO measurements compared with PA thermodilution in post–cardiac surgery patients and transthoracic echocardiography- derived CO in patients before cardiac surgery.<sup>412,413</sup>

General limitations are similar between invasive, noninvasive, and minimally invasive pulse wave analysis methods. All pulse contour analysis systems rely on an optimal arterial waveform signal with a discernible dicrotic notch for accurate identification of systole and diastole. Inaccurate measurements may result from over- or underdamped traces, as well as arrhythmias, aortic regurgitation, use of intra- aortic balloon pumps, and changes in systemic vascular resistance. Additional technical limitations of the finger cuff–based system are its use in patients with severe vascular disease, severe peripheral vasoconstriction or peripheral edema, or patients in circulatory shock or receiving high- dose vasopressor therapy.<sup>409,414</sup> The main limitation of radial artery applanation tonometry is motion artifact which impairs the arterial blood pressure waveform signal and therefore makes pulse wave analysis unreliable.

With respect to other derived variables from these monitoring methods, such as SVV or PPV, other conditions must exist for accurate measurement, including controlled mechanical ventilation with a tidal volume ≥8 mL/kg body weight and a regular cardiac rhythm.<sup>415</sup> In situations in which PPV and SVV are not valid, such as patients who are spontaneously breathing, have arrhythmias, low tidal volume, or low lung compliance, monitoring continuous pulse contour CO during volume challenges such as passive leg raising can reliably predict fluid responsiveness.<sup>416</sup>

Multiple studies have compared different pulse wave analysis systems with pulmonary artery or transpulmonary thermodilution.<sup>353</sup> The heterogeneity between study designs, clinical settings, patient populations, and results makes comparison of measurement performance of the investigated pulse wave analysis system challenging. Though some studies show clinical interchangeability between pulse- wave analysis–derived CO measurements

and indicator dilution methods, the overall pooled results do not show interchangeability. Minimally invasive and noninvasive pulse wave analysis systems inconsistently predict CO, stroke volume, and fluid responsiveness in hemodynamically compromised patients receiving fluid boluses or vasopressor therapy and should be used with caution in patients with hemodynamic instability.<sup>417,418</sup>

### Esophageal Doppler Cardiac Output Monitoring

All of the ultrasound- based methods for CO monitoring employ the Doppler principle as described in detail in Chapter 33. While CO can be intermittently measured by the Doppler technique during transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography examinations, an esophageal Doppler probe allows for minimally invasive continuous monitoring of CO by measuring the Doppler shift of the interrogated blood flow in the descending thoracic aorta. The Doppler probe is inserted into the esophagus to a depth of approximately 35 cm from the incisor teeth and is adjusted to optimize the audible Doppler signal from the descending aorta, which lies in close proximity and runs essentially parallel to the esophagus at this location.<sup>419</sup>

Several limitations of the esophageal Doppler technique must be appreciated to avoid pitfalls in data interpretation. This monitoring method interrogates blood flow in the descending thoracic aorta and therefore only measures a fraction of total CO. To report total CO, either the esophageal Doppler measurement must be “calibrated” by some alternative method, or an empirically determined correction constant of 1.4 is used.<sup>419</sup> This constant is accurate for most patients but does not apply universally, especially in the presence of conditions that redistribute blood flow, such as pregnancy, aortic crossclamping, and following cardiopulmonary bypass.<sup>420,421</sup> In addition, the descending thoracic aorta diameter is either measured using A- mode ultrasound or calculated from a nomogram based on the patient’s age, sex, height, and weight.<sup>419</sup> When calculated, it is assumed that the aortic diameter does not change throughout the cardiac cycle. In addition to these considerations, the technique is likely to be inaccurate in the presence of aortic valve stenosis or regurgitation, or in patients with thoracic aortic disease. Finally, like all ultrasound techniques, the acoustic window needed to acquire the Doppler signal may not be adequate in some individuals, thereby precluding use of this method.

While CO measurement with the esophageal Doppler has been noted to correlate well with PAC thermodilution CO measurements,<sup>420</sup> widespread adoption of the esophageal Doppler is limited by the fact that the technique is prone to motion artifacts, operator- dependent, and not easily applied in non- tracheally intubated, nonsedated patients. One of the more important benefits of this monitor may be focusing clinical attention on optimizing stroke volume rather than total CO. Indeed, in critically ill patients, complications may be better predicted by low stroke volume than by low CO.<sup>422</sup> Perioperative fluid management with esophageal Doppler monitoring and protocol- driven fluid administration have been shown to reduce perioperative morbidity, length of stay, and overall health care costs, but similar improvements have been seen with more recent Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) programs.<sup>419</sup>

The role of esophageal Doppler monitoring has diminished as other minimally invasive and noninvasive CO monitoring devices have become more popular.

### Bioimpedance and Bioreactance Cardiac Output Monitoring

The technique of bioimpedance CO monitoring was first described by Kubicek and associates and is based on changes in electrical impedance of the thoracic cavity or the whole body occurring with ejection of blood during cardiac systole.<sup>423</sup> As blood has a significantly lower electrical resistance compared with tissues, changes in impedance to electrical flow reflect a change in blood volume, and this can be used to calculate stroke volume.<sup>424</sup>

For bioimpedance measurement, disposable electrodes are applied to the skin surface along the sides of the neck and lateral aspect of the costal margin (thoracic bioimpedance) or to the four limbs (whole-body bioimpedance), a high-frequency, low-amplitude electrical current is applied, and the voltage change is measured. Patient height, weight, and sex are used to calculate the volume of the thoracic cavity. Bioimpedance CO is computed for each cardiac cycle and continuously displayed as an average value over several heart beats.

Although many studies suggest that the bioimpedance method is accurate in healthy volunteers, its reliability deteriorates in surgical and critically ill patients.<sup>425</sup> This led to attempts at improving the technology used. A newer technique, termed bioreactance CO monitoring, measures not only the amplitude change of the received signal, but also its phase shift compared to the applied electrical signal. It utilizes four dual- electrode patches, placed two on each side of the body.<sup>424</sup> Compared to the standard bioimpedance method, the bioreactance method shows somewhat better agreement with traditional CO calculations. Several clinical scenarios where this new method has been validated include the prediction of fluid responsiveness when using the passive leg raising test in patients after cardiac surgery and evaluating CO change during exercise stress testing.<sup>424</sup>

Sources of error for bioimpedance and bioreactance monitors include motion artifacts, electrical interference, arrhythmias, anatomic shunts, mechanical ventilation, pleural and pericardial effusions, and pulmonary edema.<sup>398</sup> Bioimpedance has been found to have a wide percentage error when compared to bolus thermodilution.<sup>426</sup> Despite modifications to the bioimpedance algorithms, clinical validation has been poor.<sup>427,428</sup> Due to a higher signal- to- noise ratio, bioreactance monitoring results in less interference from motion artifacts, electrical noise, lead placement, and respiratory effort, however, the technology continues to struggle with poor accuracy and precision.<sup>398,429,430</sup>

Initial clinical trials suggested reasonably good agreement between the partial rebreathing CO2 CO method and other techniques, such as thermodilution. Due to the mandatory increase in arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), the technique is relatively contraindicated in patients with increased intracranial pressure or pulmonary hypertension.

While invasive methods for monitoring CO still have a role in high- risk and critically ill patients, minimally invasive and noninvasive methods are now available which allow continuous measurement of CO in patients across a broader risk spectrum.
