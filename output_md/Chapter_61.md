# Geriatric Anesthesia

<!-- PDF page 1 -->


ELIZABETH MAHANNA GABRIELLI, KATIE J. SCHENNING, and STACIE DEINER

- **KEY POINTS** Normal aging is associated with changes in physiology and an increase in many pathologic conditions.

   - The number and impact of normal and pathologic conditions varies significantly across older individuals.

   - Preoperative screening recommendations and guidelines for older patients can provide a useful starting point to evaluate and optimize care.

   - Best intraoperative practices follow from an understanding of geriatric physiology and awareness of medications, which are contraindicated in the older population.

   - Postoperative care tailored to the needs of high- risk adults may benefit the highest-risk patients such as palliative care consultation and delirium prevention measures.

In 2019, the number of people over age 65 was 703 million. The global number of older adults is expected to increase to 1.5 billion people in 2050 (Table 61.1).<sup>1</sup> In the United States alone, more than 16 million patients over age 60 underwent surgery in 2006. The larger presence of older surgical patients has significant implications for anesthesiologists. Age- dependent physiologic changes occur in every organ system, many of which result in a decrease in functional capacity.

There is also considerable variability in the extent of agedependent changes. Indeed, a general principle of geriatric medicine is that older adults are a heterogeneous group. In addition to age- related physiologic change, older patients may also have a greater burden of comorbid disease. The process of aging involves a plethora of biological pathways (Fig. 61.1) which proceed at varying rates across patients. For example, two 80- year- old patients may show very different telomere lengths, accumulation of genetic mutations, and cumulative oxidative stress. Differences in the pace of biology vs. chronological time are referred to as biological aging and chronological aging.

In this chapter, we discuss common age- dependent physiologic and pathophysiologic changes and implications for preoperative assessment, intraoperative management, and postoperative care of older adults. While age- dependent physiology and pathophysiology may not be amenable to change, awareness of the processes and implications for anesthetic management can help providers make better choices. While there have been significant increases in quality programs and biomedical research focused on older adults, recent studies suggest that there is still a gap between best practice guidelines and clinical care.<sup>2</sup> Older age continues to be a risk factor for complications, readmission, and mortality after many types of surgery.<sup>3–5</sup>

## Organ- Specific Age- Related Physiologic and Pathologic Changes

Except for those who exclusively treat pediatric or obstetric patients, most anesthesiologists provide care for older adults at least some of the time. Therefore, understanding the numerous physiologic changes of aging is critical for caring for the older population. Here we discuss these changes by organ system.

### CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM

Aging is associated with a decrease in white matter density in the prefrontal cortex and anterior corpus callosum.<sup>6</sup> Decrease in synaptic function, especially in the prefrontal cortex, may also contribute to cognitive decline. Contrary to earlier thought, aging is not associated with significant neuronal loss in the neocortex or hippocampus.<sup>7</sup> Similarly, there is significant variability in the decrease of dendritic branching, also previously thought to decline with aging.<sup>6</sup> Decreased neuronal connectivity occurs particularly in the hippocampus, frontal/prefrontal cortex, and the temporal lobe.<sup>8</sup> Age- related dysregulation of neuronal transmission, baseline neuronal firing, calcium metabolism, and gene expression diminishes both connectivity and plasticity.<sup>6</sup> Functionally, these physiologic changes contribute to normal age- related cognitive decline in many domains such as executive function, cognitive processing speed, working and spatial memory, and maintenance of circadian rhythm.

### CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM

Age is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease.<sup>17</sup> The major processes include oxidative stress, chronic low-grade inflammation, and limited capacity to repair and regenerate.<sup>18</sup> In healthy individuals, normal aging manifests as changes in the vasculature, sympathetic tone, the myocardium, the cardiac conduction system, the cardiac valves, and the baroreceptor system.

Diastolic dysfunction is rare prior to age 50, however, a recent study demonstrated that by age 70 half of the participants had diastolic dysfunction, and more than two- thirds had diastolic dysfunction by age 80.<sup>20</sup> The consequence of diastolic dysfunction is that geriatric patients are increasingly dependent on preload and atrial contraction (“kick”) to maintain cardiac output. Small decreases in circulating blood volume or arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation can lead to inadequate ventricular filling and decreased cardiac output.<sup>21</sup> The right side of the heart also undergoes physiologic changes with age. Pulmonary artery pressure and resistance also increase with age. Analogous to the left side of the heart, right- sided diastolic dysfunction increases with age with similar physiologic implications and preserved right ventricular ejection fraction.<sup>22</sup>

### Cardiac Valves

The pathologic condition of aortic stenosis is more common with aging.<sup>23</sup> Normal aging results in a thickened and calcified aortic valve. Age- related degenerative aortic disease is present in over 25% of patients over 65 years of age; 2%–5% of these patients have clinically significant left ventricular outflow.<sup>24</sup> Severity and prevalence of aortic stenosis increases with age. For patients over age 75, one in eight patients has moderate to severe aortic stenosis.<sup>25</sup> Patients with aortic stenosis depend on adequate diastolic volume and normal sinus rhythm to maintain myocardial perfusion. Furthermore, patients with aortic stenosis have increased left ventricular diastolic pressure and develop left ventricular hypertrophy, which means that they are susceptible to myocardial ischemia in the presence of decreased coronary perfusion pressure. To avoid myocardial ischemia in patients with aortic stenosis, it is important to prevent hypotension and tachycardia, which reduces the length of diastole and further impairs coronary perfusion. Even minor left ventricular dilation or a relatively small decrease in left ventricular systolic function can increase the likelihood of intraoperative decompensation.<sup>26</sup>

### Sympathetic and Autonomic System

The ability of the sympathetic and autonomic systems to respond to physiologic derangement decreases with age. Baroreceptor impairment increases the incidence of orthostatic hypotension.<sup>27</sup> Decreased β- adrenergic sensitivity leads to a lower maximal heart rate, decreased cardiac output, and limited responsiveness to beta agonists.<sup>28</sup> Maximal heart rate can be estimated as HR (bpm) = 220 − age (years).<sup>29</sup> This decreased maximal heart rate may limit cardiac output. In the absence of arrhythmia, aging of the cardiac conduction system and autonomic system leads to decreased heart rate variability and an increased incidence of ectopic beats.<sup>28</sup> Arrhythmia can dramatically decrease cardiac output in older adults. Atrial fibrillation is most common, affecting 1 in 10 patients 80 years of age or older.<sup>30</sup>

### Vascular Changes

With age, arterial stiffening results in increased afterload, which increases myocardial oxygen consumption and wall stress. Comorbid pathology such as atherosclerosis and decreased β2- adrenergic vasodilation may compound this

effect. Partially because of age- related vascular changes, the incidence of venous thromboembolism (VTE) increases exponentially with age, affecting up to 600 people over the age of 80 years per 100,000 annually.<sup>31</sup> All three parts of Virchow’s classic triad (venous stasis, hypercoagulability, and aberrant blood flow) may affect older populations and contribute to this increased risk of VTE. For example, venous stasis may result from decreased vascular compliance, a low- flow state such as congestive heart failure, immobility, varicose veins, postmenopausal estrogen replacement therapy, and/or smoking.

### RESPIRATORY SYSTEM

As the pulmonary system ages, the lung compliance increases, and the muscles involved in respiration weaken, affecting multiple respiratory mechanics (Table 61.2).<sup>32</sup> The chest wall stiffens because of calcification of intercostal cartilage. There are also arthritic changes in the costovertebral joints, weakening and atrophy of the intercostal muscles, and vertebral body height loss due to osteoporosis and/or kyphosis.<sup>33</sup> Lung compliance increases as elastic recoil decreases despite increased elastin production. Therefore, while total lung capacity (TLC) remains unchanged, functional residual capacity increases 1% to 3% per decade, leading to an increase in residual volume (5%–10% per decade) and overall decrease in vital capacity.<sup>32</sup> By age 70, the ratio of

Closing capacity, the volume at which small airways close, increases with age which may result in atelectasis, pulmonary shunting, and hypoxemia. Common intraoperative maneuvers, such as increased intra- abdominal pressure due to carbon dioxide insufflation or Trendelenburg positioning, may compound these effects. Strategies to minimize atelectasis in the postoperative period include early mobilization/ambulation after surgery, chest physiotherapy, and incentive spirometry.<sup>34</sup>

Compared to younger patients, older patients also have weaker pharyngeal muscles, decreased clearance of secretions, decreased mucociliary transport, less efficient cough, decreased esophageal motility, and less effective protective upper airway reflexes.<sup>32</sup> These factors place the older population at increased risk for aspiration and postoperative pneumonia.

In addition to mechanical changes, older adults have an approximate 50% decrease in the respiratory response to hypoxia and hypercarbia, which is even more pronounced during sleep.<sup>35</sup> Older patients also have decreased hypopharyngeal and genioglossal muscle tone, which predisposes them to upper airway obstruction, particularly during sleep.<sup>32,36</sup> The most common sleep- related respiratory derangement in older adults is sleep apnea, which may affect up to 50%.<sup>37</sup> Older age is recognized as an independent risk factor for postoperative opioid- related respiratory depression. The PRODIGY trial was a prospective observational blinded trial of continuous capnography and oximetry.<sup>38</sup> The trial generated a risk prediction score and the five variables identified which predicted postoperative respiratory depression were: age ≥60, sex, opioid naivety, sleep disorders, and chronic heart failure. Given these risk factors, older patients should be screened preoperatively for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) using one of several screening questionnaires such as the STOP- Bang, Berlin, or American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) questionnaires. Patients with OSA should be recognized as having a potentially difficult mask airway and postoperatively may benefit from continuous positive airway pressure and opioid- sparing pain management.<sup>39</sup>

Anesthesiologists can use physiology of aging and the individual patient’s comorbid disease burden to make choices to minimize the risk of pulmonary complications. Neuraxial or regional anesthesia as a primary technique with minimal sedation or supplemental technique to improve pain control can reduce the risk of aspiration by reducing anesthetic- induced interference with the cough reflex and encouraging mobilization and pulmonary toilet. In some cases, it may be appropriate to avoid neuromuscular blocking agents or, if they are required, ensure adequate reversal to improve postoperative respiratory exchange and reduce aspiration risk. Sugammadex reversal is an attractive option, and is effective and safe in older adults, although the time to recovery of train-of-four (TOF) ratio to 0.9 was found to be 0.7 minute slower in adults age 65 or older.<sup>40</sup> Anesthesia providers should consider non- narcotic medications to further avoid risk of respiratory compromise.<sup>13</sup>

### RENAL SYSTEM

Kidney function decreases with age and these changes may be exacerbated by pathophysiology. A decrease in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) begins after age 40 but may begin as early as age 20.<sup>41</sup> Aging is associated with an increase in fibrotic glomeruli that decreases renal function. Renal blood flow decreases after age 40 and continues to decline approximately 10% per decade.<sup>42</sup> Chronic diseases frequently found in the older population (e.g., hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and atherosclerosis) and exposures such as smoking and lead can exacerbate this normal age- related decline in renal function.<sup>41</sup> Although older patients typically have normal serum creatinine levels, they also tend to have decreased lean muscle mass and lower creatinine overall. Therefore, a “normal” serum creatinine in an older patient may belie a reduced GFR. In the setting of reduced GFR, medications that are excreted through the kidney or have active metabolites that are excreted through the kidney may accumulate if the dose is not appropriately adjusted.

Age- related decreased responsiveness to renin, aldosterone, and vasopressin reduces the older patient’s ability to regulate their volume status and may result in electrolyte and acid–base derangement.<sup>43</sup> The decrease in nitric oxide via decreased endothelial nitric oxide synthase may lead to endothelial dysfunction. The compound effects of glomerulosclerosis and decline in GFR and renal plasma flow predisposes older adults to dysnatremia.<sup>44</sup> Hyponatremia affects 11% of the geriatric ambulatory community and 5.3% of hospitalized geriatric patients.<sup>45,46</sup> Hypernatremia affects 1% of hospitalized patients age 60 and older.<sup>47</sup> Furthermore, because older adults often have inappropriate sodium excretion, they are particularly prone to hypotension and acute kidney injury in the setting of hypovolemia.<sup>48</sup> Medications such as serotonin reuptake inhibitors and loop diuretics may further predispose older adults to dysnatremia.<sup>48</sup>

The incidence of urologic disease (e.g. bladder and prostate) also increases with age. The incidence of postoperative urinary retention increases in older men and women.<sup>49</sup> This is an important point to recognize because discomfort due to urinary retention can be a common cause of postoperative agitation. Urinary tract infections (UTIs) also increase in incidence in older men and women and are associated with delirium.<sup>50</sup>

### GASTROINTESTINAL AND HEPATIC SYSTEMS

Older adults have 20%–40% decrease in liver size and 40%–60% decrease in blood flow that affects hepatic drug clearance.<sup>51</sup> Lower albumin levels may reduce the total amount of drug- protein binding, which has the potential to increase free drug levels if clearance is also reduced. Phase I metabolism is the process by which water- insoluble drugs are converted into soluble molecules including but not limited to utilizing the family of catalyst enzymes CYP450. More recent studies suggest that the effect of aging on phase I metabolism is variable and may vary by drug. The effect of aging on phase II metabolism, which involves acetylation and conjugation, is also variable. The presence of frailty is associated with liver dysfunction, although the

<!-- PDF page 5 -->

directionality of the association is not clear.<sup>52–55</sup> The concept of flow vs. capacity limited hepatic clearance is also important. Drugs that are flow limited (i.e. high hepatic extraction) are cleared more slowly in older adults, up to 30%–40% in proportion to reduction in liver blood flow. Examples of these drugs include: ketamine, flumazenil, morphine, fentanyl, sufentanil, and lidocaine.<sup>56</sup> The effect of age on capacity limited drugs (low extraction) is more variable and their clearance is related to protein binding.<sup>57</sup>

The incidence of non- alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) increases with age, affecting nearly half of geriatric patients; however, in contrast to younger populations, it is unclear whether NAFLD is associated with metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, or another pathology. Younger obese diabetic patients with NAFLD are at increased risk of complications as they age, such as severe hepatic fibrosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and cryptogenic cirrhosis.<sup>58</sup>

Risk of postoperative nausea and vomiting decreases after age 50, which is fortunate because many antiemetics act through anticholinergic pathways which can promote delirium.<sup>59,60</sup> The Beers criteria recommend against most of these medications including prochlorperazine, promethazine, metoclopramide (except in the setting of gastroparesis), and corticosteroid prophylaxis.<sup>61</sup> The 5- HT3 receptor antagonists (e.g., ondansetron) are a better choice for older adults.

### MUSCULOSKELETAL SYSTEM

Muscle mass and strength decrease with age; mass decreases 3%–8% for every decade after 30, and the rate of decline increases with age.<sup>62</sup> Maintaining strength in the perioperative period is critical in older people. Declining muscle strength is associated with increased mortality risk, and older adults who are immobile lose muscle mass much faster than their younger counterparts.<sup>63</sup> For example, healthy older adults who were placed on bed rest for 10 days experienced greater loss of muscle mass than healthy younger adults who were placed on bed rest for 28 days.<sup>64</sup>

Decreased lean muscle mass may not be obvious in older patients, whose weight may remain stable, while their total body fat increases and their subcutaneous (insulating) fat reserves decrease. A 10- year longitudinal study of older adults showed a 23% per decade decline in subcutaneous fat with a concurrent average 11% per decade increase in total body fat.<sup>65</sup> Diminished subcutaneous fat thickness and age- related thermoregulatory dysfunction partially explain why older patients are prone to temperature dysregulation intraoperatively.<sup>66</sup> Age- related dysregulation of the cutaneous microcirculation also contributes to impaired wound healing. Anesthesiologists can optimize surgical healing in this population by maintaining adequate hydration, normothermia, and tissue oxygenation.<sup>67</sup>

Along with age- related changes in the muscles and skin, aging affects the skeletal system as well. Osteoarthritis is the most common joint disorder in the United States and leads to limited joint mobility in older patients.<sup>68</sup> Anesthesiologists should be aware of this and inquire about mobility limitations during preoperative evaluation to avoid exacerbating preexisting joint issues while positioning the patient for surgery in the operating room.<sup>69</sup>

## Preoperative Assessment of Older Surgical Patients

The preoperative assessment and optimization of the geriatric patient incorporate components specific to geriatric patients into a standard preoperative evaluation, as detailed in Chapter 28. In 2014, the American College of Surgeons (ACS) and the American Geriatrics Society (AGS) convened an expert panel and published best practice guidelines for the perioperative management of geriatric patients.<sup>34</sup> Their recommendations include elements that are best practice in the preoperative care of all adult patients, such as using the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) algorithm for patients undergoing noncardiac surgery,<sup>70</sup> as well as determining the risk for postoperative pulmonary complications, and ordering appropriate laboratory tests based on details of the specific patient and procedure. For example, electrocardiograms and advanced cardiac testing should be based on the patient’s medical history rather than using an “age cutoff.” Geriatric- specific preoperative recommendations include screening for geriatric syndromes and other high- risk characteristics to determine potential areas of vulnerability such as frailty, fall risk, polypharmacy, depression, alcohol, and other substance abuse. The ACS/AGS guidelines also recommend determining a patient’s social support system and understanding a patient’s expectations and advanced directives. The ACS National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) Surgical Risk Calculator has recently been enhanced with functionality to predict geriatric- specific outcomes by including geriatric- specific risk factors in the model. This tool can be used to enhance shared decisionmaking to optimize perioperative care of the geriatric surgical patient.<sup>71</sup> To prepare for the aging surgical population, the ACS established the Geriatric Surgery Verification (GSV) program, which includes 32 perioperative standards for hospitals to adhere to in order to become a certified center of geriatric surgery.<sup>72</sup> The program is designed to improve surgical outcomes for geriatric patients, and the standards include various preoperative considerations, including screening for common geriatric syndromes and complexities. Specifically, the GSV recommends screening for the following high- risk characteristics: age ≥85 years, impaired cognition, delirium risk, impaired functional status, impaired mobility, malnutrition, difficulty swallowing, and need for a palliative care assessment. We expand on essential aspects of geriatric screening below.

### COGNITIVE ASSESSMENT AND DELIRIUM RISK

Postoperative delirium is one of the most common postoperative complications in older patients, and it is associated with adverse outcomes, including increased risk of longterm cognitive decline, discharge to a care facility, loss of functional independence, and increased length of stay, cost, and mortality. Preoperative cognitive impairment is the most consistent risk factor for perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PND), including delirium and longer- term cognitive decline, which are described in detail in Chapter 78. Occult preoperative cognitive impairment in older adults is common, and present in more than 20% of patients over

<!-- PDF page 6 -->

the age of 65 presenting for presurgical testing, with the highest prevalence in the oldest patients.<sup>73</sup> In 2016, the ASA launched the Brain Health Initiative with a vision that “All Physician Anesthesiologists implement current guidelines and scientific advances to protect and preserve the brain function of every patient undergoing a surgical procedure or intervention.”<sup>74</sup> One of the main principles of the program is screening for preoperative cognitive impairment, frailty, and other PND risk factors. Cognitive screening is important for delirium risk stratification and is an objective way to establish a patient’s preoperative baseline to assess for perioperative changes. Furthermore, cognitive impairment is associated with other adverse outcomes, including postoperative pneumonia, renal insufficiency, stroke, and mortality.<sup>75</sup>

Many delirium risk indices exist and consist of varying risk factors. Though there is a wide range of components in the indices, most include age, cognitive status, and measures of multimorbidity.<sup>76–78</sup> Examples of two delirium prediction tools are shown in Table 61.3. Recently, machine learning approaches have been used to leverage electronic health record data for postoperative delirium prediction.<sup>79,80</sup>

In- depth neuropsychiatric testing to obtain a nuanced understanding of cognitive abilities across domains is not practical for most pretesting centers since it often involves over an hour of testing administered by trained providers. Recent guidelines and best practice statements recommend screening for cognitive impairment with a brief, validated screening tool to identify high- risk patients<sup>13,81–83</sup> (Table 61.4). Despite recommendations to routinely screen for cognitive impairment in geriatric patients, anesthesiologists report poor adherence to these recommendations.<sup>84</sup> Commonly cited barriers to cognitive screening are the lack of a gold standard test, clarity on how screening will change patient management, and feasibility concerns. Regarding feasibility, studies have shown that cognitive screening in a pretesting clinic or even on the day of surgery is practical and wellaccepted by patients and staff.<sup>73,85,86</sup> In addition, although there is no gold standard test, several screening tools have been validated in the perioperative setting. A recent review

summarizes existing tools, considerations for tool selection, and a guide on using these measures.<sup>87</sup>

Finally, there are several actions anesthesiologists can take to optimize patients with impaired cognition. These actions are critical, considering that up to 40% of delirium is thought to be preventable.<sup>88</sup> Communicating the delirium risk to relevant perioperative teams is essential and may include surgery, medicine, geriatrics, physical and occupational therapy, intensive care, and nursing. The anesthesiologist should also discuss the risk of postoperative delirium with the patient and family members. Patients and their families and friends should be educated regarding the role they can play in delirium prevention, such as having someone stay with the patient or frequently visit during recovery, and the importance of environmental cues, sleep hygiene, and maintaining the sleep–wake cycle. Patients should be reminded to bring assistive devices to the hospital, including hearing aids, glasses, and dentures, all of which should be made available as soon as possible after the procedure. Patients can also pack a family photo, clock, calendar, ear plugs, eye masks, and other objects from home to help maintain orientation. It is also important to review a patient’s list of home medications to evaluate for potentially deliriogenic medications that could be discontinued. Finally, if the patient or their family members are concerned about the presence of preexisting cognitive impairment, consultation with a neurologist or geriatrician can be offered. The above strategies, summarized in Box 61.1, have the best chance of success as part of a comprehensive, multidisciplinary delirium prevention program encompassing the entire perioperative period. In addition to the above strategies, multicomponent interventions in the intraoperative and postoperative period might include minimizing restraint use, targeted anesthesia protocols, avoiding potentially inappropriate medications, early mobilization, pain management, and sleep enhancement.

### MALNUTRITION AND DYSPHAGIA

Malnutrition is common and underrecognized in older patients. Malnutrition is associated with an increased risk of adverse postoperative outcomes, particularly infections and wound complications.<sup>110,111</sup> The ACS NSQIP/AGS Best Practice Guidelines recommend screening for severe nutritional risk using body mass index (BMI) &lt;18.5 kg/m<sup>2</sup> , serum albumin &lt;3 g/dL without evidence of hepatic or renal dysfunction, or unintentional weight loss >10%–15% within 6 months.<sup>34,100</sup> The ACS Strong for Surgery program also recommends screening for malnutrition using BMI, unintentional weight loss, poor appetite, or the inability to take food orally. ### DEPRESSION AND ALCOHOL SCREENING

Depression and alcohol abuse are relatively common in older adults and are associated with adverse postoperative outcomes. Depression is associated with greater pain perception, increased need for postoperative analgesics, poor functional recovery, increased incidence and duration of delirium, and increased likelihood of discharge to a facility.<sup>118–120</sup> Screening for depression can be performed using the Geriatric Depression Scale<sup>121</sup> or the Patient Health Questionnaire- 2 (PHQ- 2),<sup>122</sup> which asks:

- “In the past 12 months, have you ever had a time when you felt sad, blue, depressed, or down for most of the time for at least 2 weeks?”

- “In the past 12 months, have you ever had a time lasting at least 2 weeks when you didn’t care about the things you usually cared about or when you didn’t enjoy the things that you usually enjoyed?”

“Yes” to either constitutes a positive screen which needs further evaluation.

It is important to screen for alcohol abuse and dependence, which are associated with increased postoperative complications such as pneumonia and sepsis, delirium, and prolonged hospital length of stay.<sup>100,123,124</sup> The classic screening tool for alcohol use is the modified CAGE questionnaire, which has been validated for older adults.<sup>125,126</sup> The questionnaire consists of four questions:

- “Have you ever felt you needed to cut down on your drinking?”

- “Have people annoyed you by criticizing your drinking?”

- “Have you ever felt guilty about drinking?”

- “Have you ever felt you needed a drink first thing in the morning (Eye Opener) to steady your nerves or get rid of a hangover?”

“Yes” to any question triggers consideration for perioperative prophylaxis for withdrawal syndromes, supplementation of folic acid and thiamine, and consideration of the need for a detoxification protocol supervised by an addiction specialist.<sup>34</sup>

## Intraoperative Management Considerations for Older Adults

Once the comprehensive preoperative assessment and preprocedure consent have been completed, the anesthesiologist develops the intraoperative anesthetic plan for the individual older patient. The formulated plan is based on the individual older patient’s specific comorbid illnesses, organ system reserve, and overall functional status. Due to decreased physiologic reserve, many older adults require more careful intraoperative management than younger, healthier patients with greater physiologic reserve. General recommendations by organ system are described below and summarized in Table 61.7.

### DRUG PHARMACOLOGY

There are pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic changes associated with aging. During induction the prolonged

circulation time may delay the onset of intravenous drugs, but speed induction with inhalational agents.<sup>56</sup> Diminished cardiac reserve may manifest as exaggerated hypotension during anesthesia induction.<sup>21</sup> A delay in the onset of neuromuscular blockade occurs due to decreased cardiac output and reduced muscle blood flow.

During maintenance of anesthesia, volatile anesthetics need to have age- adjusted minimum alveolar concentration (MAC). Older patients have decreased binding sites for serotonergic, adrenergic, and gamma- aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors. The MAC and MAC- awake decline by 6% per decade after age 30.<sup>129,130</sup> Intravenous anesthetics, propofol, etomidate, opioids, and benzodiazepines should be given at a lower dose in older adults compared to their younger counterparts. Twentytwo percent of older patients experience severe postinduction and pre- incision hypotension with mean arterial pressures less than 55 mm Hg.<sup>131</sup> Propofol doses greater than 1.5 mg/kg are significantly associated with severe hypotension, and doses should be reduced in older adults.<sup>131</sup> Older patients are also more sensitive to midazolam and have an increased occurrence of hypotension, hypoventilation, and desaturation.<sup>132</sup> Opioid sensitivity increases with age; reducing opioid dosage may reduce postoperative respiratory depression.

With decreased kidney function or decreased hepatic excretion from loss of liver mass, there is decreased ability for drug excretion.<sup>51,57</sup> Decreased hepatic excretion prolongs the elimination half- life and duration of action of rocuronium and vecuronium. Pancuronium depends on kidney excretion. Consider use of drugs that do not extensively depend on hepatic or renal metabolism such as cisatracurium and succinylcholine. Slower clearance of drugs dependent on hepatic blood flow include ketamine, flumazenil, morphine, fentanyl, and lidocaine.<sup>56</sup>

The volume of distribution is altered in older patients.<sup>133</sup> A decrease in muscle mass and increase in body fat leads to decreased total body water. A reduced volume of distribution for water-soluble drugs leads to greater plasma concentrations. An increased volume of distribution for lipid-soluble drugs leads to decreased plasma concentrations. Altered plasma protein concentrations can alter distribution and elimination. Albumin binds acidic drugs such as benzodiazepines and opioids. Albumin is often decreased in older patients and therefore lower doses of benzodiazepines and opioids are needed. Alpha- 1- acid glycoprotein binds drugs such as local anesthetics. Epidurals have a more extensive spread of given volume, and there is longer duration of action per dose of spinal local anesthetic.

During emergence, the increased volume of distribution due to increased body fat, and decreased pulmonary gas exchange may lead to slower recovery from anesthesia with a volatile agent. In addition, older patients display a longer time to return to consciousness after total intravenous anesthesia with propofol as compared to younger patients.<sup>134</sup> Care should be taken to reduce dosage of neuromuscular blocking agents, and ensure that they are fully reversed (i.e., TOF ratio >90%) prior to extubation to reduce postoperative pulmonary complications.

### HEMODYNAMIC MANAGEMENT

Older patients have decreased cardiovascular reserve. Due to increased diastolic dysfunction, small decreases in circulating blood volume or arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation can lead to inadequate ventricular filling and decreased cardiac output.<sup>20,21</sup> Profound hypotension may occur post induction if dose reductions of induction agents are not utilized.<sup>131</sup> In addition, aortic valve disease is present in over 25% of patients over 65 years of age, with 2%–5% of these patients having clinically significant left ventricular outflow.<sup>24</sup> It is imperative to prevent hypotension and tachycardia to prevent myocardial ischemia in patients with aortic stenosis. Even minor left ventricular dilation or a relatively small decrease in left ventricular systolic function can increase the likelihood of intraoperative decompensation.<sup>26</sup> Arrhythmias including atrial fibrillation are very common, and care should be taken to maintain adequate electrolyte levels.<sup>28,30</sup>

The recent Perioperative Quality Initiative consensus statement on intraoperative blood pressure risk and outcomes was not specific for older adults. However, the three consensus points from this Delphi process can be regarded as a starting point for best practice.<sup>150</sup> These are:

- Consensus statement 1: Intraoperative mean arterial blood pressures below 60–70 mm Hg are associated with myocardial injury, acute kidney injury, and death. Systolic arterial pressures below 100 mm Hg are associated with myocardial injury and death. Injury is a function of hypotension severity and duration. This statement also mentioned that “there is evidence that hypotension in association with tachycardia enhances organ specific risk.”

- Consensus statement 2: For adults having noncardiac surgery, there is insufficient evidence to recommend a general upper limit of blood pressure at which therapy should be initiated.

- Consensus statement 3: During cardiac surgery, intraoperative systolic blood pressure greater than 140 mm Hg is associated with increased 30- day mortality. Injury is a function of severity and duration.

The application of these three consensus statements should be contextualized for the specific patient’s baseline blood pressure, heart rate, and comorbid conditions.

### VENTILATION STRATEGIES

Respiratory physiology and reserve changes with age, leading to increased risk of perioperative pulmonary complications (see Table 61.2). Age is one of the risk factors associated with the development of postoperative pulmonary complications.<sup>151,152</sup> In a recent meta- analysis of risk prediction models for postoperative pulmonary complications, the common predictive variables in the 29 identified models were age (55.2% of models), a diagnosis of chronic respiratory disease (58.6% of models), type of surgery and surgical location (72.4% of models), and emergent procedure (48.3% of models).<sup>152</sup> Older adults have an increase in residual volume and overall decrease in vital capacity.<sup>32</sup> Aging is also associated with alveolar airspace disease, increased closing capacity, and reduced functional residual capacity (FRC) leading to atelectasis, pulmonary shunting, and hypoxemia. Prolonged preoxygenation, adequate mask ventilation, and positive end- expiratory pressure should be utilized to reduce the risk of hypoxia. Common intraoperative maneuvers, such as increased intra- abdominal pressure due to carbon dioxide insufflation or Trendelenburg positioning, further reduce the FRC and lung compliance. Older adults also have decreased respiratory muscle strength and protective reflexes, predisposing them to aspiration and postoperative pneumonia.<sup>32</sup> Older adults have increased risk of postoperative respiratory depression due to decreased respiratory drive response to hypoxia and hypercarbia and increased prevalence of OSA.<sup>35,37,38</sup>

Lung protective strategies are recommended in patients with risk factors for postoperative pulmonary complications.<sup>151</sup> In a large observational study, Ladha _et al._ showed in nearly 70,000 patients that lung protective strategies, defined as low tidal volumes (&lt;10 mL/kg), adequate positive end- expiratory pressure (PEEP) (≥5 cmH2O), and plateau pressures &lt;30 cmH2O, were significantly associated with reduced postoperative pulmonary complications.<sup>153</sup> A PEEP of 5 cmH2O and plateau pressures of less than or equal to 16 cmH2O were associated with the lowest risk of postoperative pulmonary complications.<sup>153</sup> To reduce risk of aspiration, consider neuraxial or regional anesthesia with minimal sedation. Consider avoiding intermediate and long- acting neuromuscular blocking agents and ensure adequate reversal prior to emergence. Residual neuromuscular blockade is particular concerning in older patients with reduced pulmonary function. Postoperative strategies to minimize postoperative pulmonary complications include early mobilization/ambulation after surgery, chest physiotherapy, incentive spirometry, opioid- sparing management, and continuous positive airway pressure in patients with OSA.<sup>34,39</sup>

### RENAL

The goals for intraoperative fluid management are similar in older adults as younger adults. Central euvolemia should be maintained.<sup>154</sup> Under-rescitation and intravascular hypovolemia lead to the risk of acute tubular necrosis, especially in older adults.<sup>48,154</sup> Conversely, hypervolemia and fluid overload predispose patients with cardiac disease, including heart failure and atrial fibrillation, to acute decompensation. In the RELIEF (restrictive versus liberal fluid therapy

<!-- PDF page 14 -->

for major abdominal surgery), a large, pragmatic, international trial, patients with at least one risk factor for perioperative complications were randomized to restrictive versus liberal intravenous fluid regimen during and up to 24 hours after surgery.<sup>155</sup> The population of RELIEF was mostly composed of older adults with a mean age of 66 years. There was no difference in the primary outcome of disability free survival at one year for all age groups. However, restricted fluid administration had a risk ratio of 2.2 (95% confidence interval: 1.3–3.7) for acute kidney injury in patients older than 75 years.<sup>155</sup> The Optimisation of Perioperative Cardiovascular Management to Improve Surgical Outcome (OPTIMISE) trial similarly had an older patient population (mean age 72 years) and cardiac output–guided fluid and inotrope management did not reduce a composite outcome of complications and 30- day mortality.<sup>156</sup> However, the first OPTIMISE trial was underpowered as the primary outcome incidence was less than expected from the power analysis. The OPTIMISE II trial is currently recruiting to address this weakness and to provide a more definitive answer as to whether minimally invasive cardiac output–guided fluid and inotrope management will improve outcomes.<sup>157</sup>

### POSITIONING

The skin, muscle, and joints atrophy and degenerate in aging.<sup>62,68,69</sup> Older adults are more susceptible to trauma from positioning and adhesives. Osteoarthritis is common in older adults, and arthritic joints can lead to positioning difficulty or injuries.<sup>68</sup> Care must be taken during positioning to ensure adequate padding of pressure points and support of joints.<sup>69</sup> In addition, degenerative cervical spine disease can limit neck extension, potentially making intubation difficult.

## Postoperative Concerns

Best practice guidelines from the American College of Surgery for the postoperative period includes adequate pain control and a geriatric-focused checklist which includes: delirium, pulmonary complications, falls, postoperative urinary tract infections/urinary retention, pressure ulcer prevention, and care transitions.<sup>34</sup> There are no specific recommendations for care of the older adult in the recovery room. However, the physiology of aging and common diseases suggest that these patients are at higher risk for desaturation (e.g., due to decreased closing capacity and a tendency toward atelectasis) and aspiration (e.g., due to a less vigorous ability to cough). Maximizing the patient’s ability to take deep breaths by elevating the head of the bed and providing adequate but not excessive pain management may combat these issues.

Pain is often undertreated in older adults.<sup>158</sup> It is important to aggressively monitor and treat older adults for pain and regularly reassess to avoid oversedation. In the case of patients with dementia and/or nonverbal patients it may be important to employ tools which can help identify pain behaviors such as the Pain Assessment in Impaired Cognition (PAIC), the Critical Care Pain Observation Tool (CPOT), or the Faces Pain Scale, which quantifies pain based upon facial expression.<sup>158</sup> Measures for adequate pain control

include the judicious use of multimodal therapy and regional anesthesia.<sup>158,159</sup> Acetaminophen may be used without age- related dose reduction, but reduced doses are needed in liver insufficiency. Nonsteroidal anti- inflammatory drugs should have a 25%–50% dose reduction, and they should be avoided in patients with impaired renal function and peptic ulcer disease.<sup>158</sup> Intraoperative ketamine has not been shown to reduce postoperative pain or delirium in older surgical patients and was associated with an increase in hallcuinations.<sup>141,160</sup> Dexmedetomidine should be considered in the ICU for patients who are mechanically ventilated. Intravenous lidocaine may be considered in the ICU, but a dose reduction is recommended and caution taken in patients with liver insufficiency.<sup>158</sup> Systemic opioids should have a 25%–50% dose reduction.<sup>161,162</sup> Care must be taken to avoid use of Beers list medications, which include gabapentin and long- acting opioids.<sup>61</sup>

Delirium prevention is discussed above in the “Preoperative” section. Some general strategies are listed in Box 61.1. In brief, the use of multidisciplinary nonpharmacologic programs have been the most successful in reducing delirium. The most widespread of these is the Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP), which includes reorientation, mobilization, and promotion of regular sleep–wake cycles. HELP has been shown to reduce delirium and cognitive and functional decline and be cost-effective.<sup>163,164</sup> Antipsychotic medication should only be used in agitated delirium when the patient is a danger to self or others and a sedative is needed. It is not a treatment for delirium and does not shorten its time course.<sup>165,166</sup> Antipsychotic medication should not be used as prophylaxis.<sup>165,166</sup> Benzodiazepines are contraindicated for this situation and may worsen a delirium episode. Regional analgesia has been associated with reduced delirium when compared to systemic analgesia.<sup>159</sup>

## Outcomes

The goal of surgery for the older adult often includes preserving independence and function while treating the presenting condition. Large administrative data sets are beginning to collect in- depth information to help practitioners understand granular outcomes in addition to the standard 30day mortality. The National Surgical Quality Improvement Project (NSQIP) launched a geriatric- specific data collection in January 2014. This enhanced collection includes risk factors and outcomes in areas such as cognition, decisionmaking, function, and mobility. More work is needed to employ preoperative geriatric screening as detailed above to understand how preexisting states impact postoperative outcomes beyond the immediate postoperative (48-hour) period. For example, the risk of death after surgery is relatively low after major noncardiac surgery even in frail older patients. However, patient- centered and longer- term outcomes may be quite poor: 15%–50% of independent but frail older adults are unable to return home after surgery.<sup>167</sup> Even after elective surgery, up to a third of frail adults die, are institutionalized, or develop a new disability 90 days.<sup>167</sup> Care also should be taken when using risk calculators on all older adult subgroups. The NSQIP risk calculator has been shown to both under- and overestimate the risk of morbidity and mortality in patients over 80 years.<sup>168–170</sup> The

<!-- PDF page 15 -->

Successful Aging After Elective Surgery (SAGES) cohort has collected preoperative cognitive, functional, physiologic, and psychological data with years of follow- up. Longitudinal cohort studies of older patients are needed to better understand clinical and patient- centered outcomes. Overall, the literature suggests that general anesthesia is not associated with longer- term cognitive decline.<sup>171</sup> However, there may be high-risk subpopulations who are at risk for cognitive decline. While some preoperative risk factors may not be amenable to change, understanding risk and best practice is an important part of creating an anesthetic plan, performing informed consent, engaging in shared decisionmaking, and developing a postoperative plan.
