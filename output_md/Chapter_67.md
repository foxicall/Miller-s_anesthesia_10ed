# Anesthesia for Robotic Surgery

<!-- PDF page 1 -->


A. SASSAN SABOURI and YASMINE TAMEZE

- **KEY POINTS** Robotic surgery has witnessed exponential growth: since 2000, more than 8.5 million procedures have been performed using the da Vinci system worldwide.<sup>1</sup>

- Robotic surgery has been successfully adopted for patients receiving urologic, gynecologic, abdominal, otolaryngologic, cardiac, thoracic, neurosurgery, and orthopedic procedures.

- Robotic surgical systems can be classified into three types: (1) conducting robotic system; (2) robotic remote system, or (3) combined robotic system.

- 1.  Conoducting robotic system: the surgeon plans and downloads the surgical plan to the robot’s software. Then, the robot performs the procedure while the surgeon oversees the procedure.

- 2.  Robotic remote system: the surgeon controls the robot remotely and in real- time using haptic interface technology. The surgeon will receive feedback from the robotic interface and the three- dimensional views of the surgical field.

- 3.  Combined robotic system: the robot joins the surgeon in the surgical field to enhance the accuracy of manipulating the surgical instruments.

- Robotic surgical systems allow precise and accurate geometric movements and can enhance a surgeon’s ability to visualize pathologies and perform complex procedures.

- There is a great learning curve in adaptation to robotic surgical systems, which leads to increased time and the cost of robotic surgeries.

- Robotic surgery presents unique challenges for anesthesiologists, including physiologic changes associated with steep Trendelenburg positioning and difficulty accessing the patient once the robot is docked, which can lead to patient injury.

- Due to the expanding technology of robotic systems, the area of robotic surgical systems is still evolving, and many of the short- term and long- term outcomes of different robotic surgical systems still need to be clarified.

## Introduction

Robots were developed in the 1980s by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) as remotely controlled devices that allow tasks to be performed in spaces removed from human presence, such as space. While the initial concept of robotic science was useful in space exploration, the US government sought to find other applications of the technology. The United States Department of Defense (DOD) found a way to translate robotic technology from space to the battlefield to save wounded soldiers. The DOD used the technology in surgical theaters to have a surgeon remotely operate on patients in difficult- to- reach locations, such as battlefields. At the same time, the world’s first laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed in France. This landmark procedure altered the course of traditional surgery by marking the beginning of the era of minimally invasive surgical procedures.

In the 1990s, various medical robots were created, each contributing to what is now known as modern robotic surgery: from devices used to pulverize bone in orthopedic

surgery to voice recognition software to control laparoscope positions and aid in organ retraction during traditional laparoscopic surgery.

Arguably the greatest advancement in robotic surgery occurred in 1991, when a remotely located surgeon version of a robot was developed, which allowed a surgeon to sit apart from their patient and remotely control the articulating arms. Eventually, high- definition, three- dimensional cameras were added to robots, allowing surgeons to explore a patient’s anatomy and access traditionally difficult- toreach surgical sites from a console next to the operating table. Today, one of the most commonly used robots in robotic surgeries is the da Vinci Robotic Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). We will refer to the da Vinci system to describe the robot- assisted technique.

The da Vinci robot has three primary components (Fig. 67.1)

1.  Surgical console: surgeon viewing and control console (see Fig. 67.1A)

2.  Patient side- cart: a mobile cart with three or four movable arms (see Fig. 67.1B)

## Advantages of Robotic Surgery

The robot is preferred to open procedures for several reasons: similar to laparoscopic surgery, it offers less tissue manipulation, small incisions, a lower risk of infection, and a faster recovery than its open counterpart.<sup>2</sup> Additionally, robotic surgery allows for better precision and dexterity than laparoscopy, which is helpful in microsurgical dissection and re- attachment of tissues. Compared to human arms, robotic arms permit seven degrees of free movement. These movements can be categorized into gross arm movement by the da Vinci robot, fine movements by the articulating arms, and surgical actions performed by the articulating arms (Table 67.1, Fig. 67.3). These articulating arms are not limited by constraints associated with human wrist joints. Additionally, the robot allows larger, more coarse movements to be miniaturized in the operating

field. For example, moving the controls by 5 mm may move the articulating arms by only 1 mm. This miniaturization permits finer control. Furthermore, robotic software can reduce or eliminate hand tremors, thereby improving the safety and precision of surgery.<sup>3</sup>

The robot is used in various specialties, including gynecology, urology, abdominal, head and neck, thoracic, cardiac, orthopedic, and neurosurgery. Robotic surgery provides increased dexterity, image resolution, and degrees of freedom, making it an attractive technique for microanastomoses. One drawback of this technology is the lack of haptic feedback. However, in most surgical specialties, there is a lack of high- quality evidence showing robotic surgery as superior to laparoscopic surgery concerning longterm patient outcomes.<sup>4,5</sup>

## Robotically Assisted Intubation

There have been reports of successful robotic intubations. The Kepler Intubation System (KIS), developed by Thomas Hemmerling, has been shown to intubate mannequins successfully by an operator with either a direct or indirect view of the patient.<sup>6</sup> KIS is a low- cost system consisting of a joystick, a robot arm, the Pentax video laryngoscope, and software control. The intubations occurred within 40 to 60 seconds, with a 100% success rate on the first attempt. This system also allowed for semiautomated (a computer system replayed prior operator- driven movement sequences) intubations that occurred in less than 45 seconds and had a 100% success rate.<sup>7</sup> The system has also been used on 12 patients and was successful with the first- pass intubation in 11 of the 12 patients. The intubations were done in approximately 93 seconds.<sup>8</sup> It remains uncertain if a robot intubation system will have widespread use. However, it may have applications in settings where it would be difficult to transport a trained anesthesia provider to the location, such as battlefields or deep space exploration.

## Anesthesia for Robotic Surgery: Urology, Gynecology, and Abdominal Surgery

Although similar in some respects to laparoscopic surgery, robotic surgery introduced a different set of challenges for the anesthesia provider. In addition to the physiologic changes associated with (1) pneumoperitoneum, robotic

surgery adds the challenge of (2) extreme positioning and (3) limited access to the patient. Understanding these nuances will allow the anesthesiologist to be prepared for the complications that these types of procedures can cause and therefore create compensatory plans.

### PNEUMOPERITONEUM

For most robotic surgical procedures, inert gas must be insufflated into a patient’s body cavity to visualize the surgical field. Carbon dioxide (CO2) is the inert gas of choice for laparoscopy due to its high diffusion coefficient and its lower risk of gas emboli since it is easily excreted from the body through the respiratory system.<sup>9</sup> Most complications from pneumoperitoneum stem from hypercarbia due to (a) respiratory, (b) systemic effects, and (c) gas extravasation.

**Pulmonary Vasoconstriction.** Pulmonary vasoconstriction during pneumoperitoneum is secondary to (a) hypercarbia and (b) hypoxia. To optimize gas exchange, the pulmonary vasculature constricts in order to preferentially shunt blood away from less ventilated portions of the lungs. In addition to pulmonary vasoconstriction, pneumoperitoneum results in compression atelectasis, creating an intrapulmonary shunt. Nasogastric or orogastric tubes may facilitate gastric decompression and help reduce, albeit not eliminate, increased intra- abdominal pressures. In addition, recruitment maneuvers, low tidal volume, and adequate positive end- expiratory pressure (PEEP) can prevent the development of atelectasis.<sup>11</sup> Finding the optimal PEEP for patients undergoing robotic surgery in the Trendelenburg position is a fine balance between preserving the arterial oxygenation and avoiding hemodynamic effects and

barotrauma.<sup>12</sup> While applying higher PEEP improves respiratory elastance and compliance, it causes increased peak airway pressure.<sup>12,13</sup> However, this increase in airway pressure may not reflect true transpulmonary pressure, which is a better measure of the risk of lung injury.<sup>13</sup> Changes in the chest wall and lung elastance during pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg positioning determine the partition of driving pressures (driving pressure = Pawplateau − PEEP) between the chest wall in the lungs.<sup>13</sup> The driving pressure is not entirely transmitted to the lungs; therefore, high driving pressures do not translate to increased transpulmonary pressures during the docking stage.<sup>13</sup> Therefore higher limits of plateau pressure can be tolerated without lung injury.<sup>13</sup> The esophageal balloon can clarify the transpulmonary pressure, allowing for individual PEEP titration and improvement in oxygenation and respiratory system mechanics.<sup>13,14</sup> This phenomenon, combined with vasoconstriction due to hypercarbia, increases the ventilation/ perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, which can lead to hypoxia.

<!-- PDF page 5 -->

The resulting hypoxia caused by V/Q mismatch also leads to hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV). This compensatory mechanism allows the body to preferentially divert blood from oxygen- poor regions of the lungs to oxygen- rich regions and improves gas exchange by shunting blood to areas of the lung that are ventilating normally.<sup>10</sup>

In addition to pulmonary vasoconstriction, hypercarbia leads to a shift in the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve to the right (the Haldane effect), which results in easier unloading of the oxygen molecules from hemoglobin, thus further attenuating hypoxia.<sup>15,16</sup>

### Systemic Effects of Hypercapnia

Carbon dioxide (CO2) insufflation may result in hypercarbia and respiratory acidosis, especially in patients with difficulty removing CO2 secondary to intrinsic lung disease or difficulty ventilating intraoperatively due to positioning or body habitus. Furthermore, hypercapnia will augment anesthetic effects. Acute hypercapnia also results in depressed consciousness when PaCO2 exceeds 80 mm Hg.<sup>17</sup> Increased CO2 decreases myocyte contractility and can increase myocardial susceptibility to arrhythmias.<sup>18</sup>

**Cerebral Vascular Effects.** Hypercarbia also leads to cerebrovascular dilation. Although CO2 leads to blood being preferentially shunted away from the pulmonary vasculature, CO2 in the cerebral circulation leads to cerebral vascular dilation. Anesthesiologists must be mindful of the potential increase in intracranial pressure (ICP) that may arise from elevated CO2 levels.<sup>19,20</sup> Additionally, several robotic procedures require steep Trendelenburg positioning to allow surgical visualization of pelvic structures, resulting in increased ICP. Direct measurement of ICP is invasive and not without risks; thus, sonographically measured optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) has been used as a noninvasive way to measure ICP. ONSD has been shown to increase with pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg.<sup>21,22</sup> Some studies have investigated whether a total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) approach leads to less diameter changes than inhaled anesthetic, but the results thus far are mixed.<sup>23–25</sup> Therefore, careful patient selection is necessary to avoid neurologic complications.

### Gas Extravasation

The lack of external visualization and the haptic feedback with the robot can lead to increased gas extravasation, leading to pneumomediastinum or subcutaneous emphysema.<sup>26,27</sup> Although this finding typically has no clinical consequences, it may be associated with prolonged CO2 excretion postoperatively, causing hypercarbia and acidosis. There have been reported cases of pneumothorax caused by the extension of insufflated gas through congenital diaphragmatic channels into pleural cavities.<sup>27</sup> A higher risk of pneumothorax is associated with an increased number and size of trocars, longer surgical time, higher gas flow rate, intensified gas pressure, loose trocars, and difficult trocar placement.<sup>27</sup>

**Gas Embolism.** Gas embolism is another potential untoward effect of CO2 insufflation.<sup>10</sup> Although rare, a gas embolus may have catastrophic effects on the

cardiopulmonary system if the embolus becomes lodged in the pulmonary system. Additionally, patients with an atrioseptal or ventriculoseptal defect may develop a gas embolus in the cerebrovasculature with potentially devastating complications.<sup>10</sup>

**Vagal Response.** Lastly, keeping intra- abdominal pressures below 20 cmH2O during initial insufflation is important to avoid the vagal stimulation that often results from elevated intra- abdominal pressures. If the patient has a particularly pronounced resting vagal tone or a significant vagal response to peritoneal insufflation, pharmacologic intervention by the anesthesia provider or reduction of pneumoperitoneum may be necessary.

### PATIENT POSITIONING

Patient positioning is particularly challenging during robotic surgery for several reasons. First, in order to get adequate visualization during certain types of surgeries (i.e., urologic and gynecologic), the patient is often required to be in a steep Trendelenburg position with or without lithotomy. First, this extremely steep Trendelenburg position is associated with various physiologic changes. Second, given that the robot is removed from the operators, it is crucial that the patient does not move or slide, as the robot would not recognize the patient’s motion quickly. Lastly, the presence of the bulky robot can apply excess pressure on various parts of the patient’s body if not properly padded.

### Physiologic Changes Associated with Positioning of the Patient

The patient will be in extreme reverse Trendelenburg for upper abdominal procedures, such as bariatric surgery. In this case, the main physiologic consequence is venous pooling of the blood, leading to an increased risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and hypotension.<sup>28</sup> For mid- abdominal procedures such as colectomies, the table remains neutral or varies during the case. Urologic, gynecologic, and lower abdominal surgeries require steep Trendelenburg positioning to improve visualization of the organs. This positioning is associated with cardiopulmonary and intracranial physiologic changes that should be considered when planning the anesthesia (Fig. 67.4).<sup>29</sup>

**Cardiopulmonary Effects.** When patients are in a steep Trendelenburg or “head- down” position, blood is funneled from the lower extremities to the right atrium, thereby increasing pre- load. Patient comorbidities, anesthetized state, and preoperative medications may alter the cardiac output changes induced by Trendelenburg positioning. In general, healthier patients with a robust cardiovascular system are better able to adjust for hemodynamic changes and tend to have unaltered cardiac output.<sup>30,31</sup>

Steep Trendelenburg positioning also leads to cephalad movement of the diaphragm by the abdomen. Combined with pneumoperitoneum, this leads to decreased functional residual capacity (FRC) and vital capacity (VC), peak and plateau airway pressures increase, and a V/Q mismatch worsens.<sup>32</sup> Furthermore, pulmonary compliance is decreased, leading to a decrease in tidal volume and difficulty ventilating. ### Need for Complete Immobility During Procedure and Padding for Nerve Injuries

The robot arms and the patient’s operating room table may not move together. Therefore, once the robot is docked and the robot arms are inside the patient, it is paramount to avoid dyssynchronous movement of the operating room table to avoid tearing tissue injuries inside the patient.

Given the need for patient immobility during a procedure, continuous neuromuscular blockade is paramount, as patient movement resulting from intense surgical stimulation in inadequate anesthesia or insufficient neuromuscular blockade may lead to significant patient injury. As a result, continual monitoring of neuromuscular blockade is imperative.

Furthermore, to ensure immobility, it is important that the patient does not slide during cases where extreme Trendelenburg or reverse Trendelenburg is required. If the patient is not well secured, the patient risks friction injuries, nerve injuries, or a fall from the operating table.<sup>37</sup> To avoid sliding, the patient is usually placed on an anti- skid foam that prevents shifting (Fig. 67.5).<sup>38</sup>

To prevent nerve injuries, it is preferable to have both of the patient’s arms tucked to their sides to minimize brachial plexopathy from hyperextension of the arms. This also gives the robot more access to the patient. While proper padding of the pressure point is necessary, adjuncts such as shoulder braces and iliac support have been associated with neuropathic injury and should be avoided.<sup>39,40</sup> Additional paddings may be necessary to protect a patient’s face, head, and neck from robotic arm movements, and vigilance by an anesthesia team is mandatory.

### BULKY ROBOT AND LIMITED ACCESS TO PATIENT

As the patient’s arms are tucked at their sides, they are often inaccessible to the anesthesia provider during an operation (see Fig. 67.5). Therefore, if an anesthesia provider is considering additional intravenous/arterial access, these lines should be placed after anesthesia induction and before the robot’s docking. The ideal practice is to place at least two intravenous catheters. This allows more flexibility with intraoperative patient management even during periods of very minimal access.

Patient positioning during robotic surgery can present a challenge as much of the normal feedback between surgeon and patient is altered and as surgeons are removed from their patients and replaced by bulky steel instrumentation. Therefore, patients are at a much higher risk of iatrogenic injury than their nonrobotic peers.<sup>41</sup> This separation from the patient adds to the complexity of the room setup and requires efficient communication between the team members. This is particularly important during situations that call for quick undocking of the robot, such as during codes. Many institutions have used simulation with the operating room team members to improve efficiency during these high-acuity situations.

### OTHER CONSIDERATIONS

Providers have begun performing robotic procedures on morbidly obese patients, given the physical surgical demands associated with traditional<sup>42</sup> laparoscopies

and the greatly increased morbidity associated with an open abdominal approach. In general, the advantages of a robotic approach on perioperative outcomes are not clearly defined.<sup>43</sup> However, positioning morbidly obese patients in the steep Trendelenburg position can be very challenging. Steep Trendelenburg positioning, combined with the pneumoperitoneum necessary to facilitate surgical exposure, can create cardiovascular and ventilation challenges. While it is possible that this subgroup of patients, who would otherwise require an open abdominal approach, could benefit from minimally invasive robot surgery, these cases involve many anesthetic challenges. Consideration can be made to incrementally transition to the Trendelenburg position to see if the patient tolerates the position prior to surgery initiation.

## Types of Robotic Surgery

Robotic surgery has gained popularity over the last decades and continues to expand to cover an increasing number of surgical specialties. For example, the da Vinci robot has been Food and Drug Administration (FDA)- approved for various urologic, gynecologic, abdominal, otolaryngologic cardiothoracic, orthopedic, and neurosurgical procedures.

### UROLOGIC PROCEDURES (SEE ALSO CHAPTER 55)

Urologists have led the way in integrating robotic surgery into their clinical practice, with robotic- assisted retropubic prostatectomy (RARP) being the most common robotic procedure performed worldwide.<sup>44</sup> Beginning with the use of robot assistance for transurethral resection of prostatic tissue (TURP), robotic surgery is now used in a variety of cases, including prostatectomies, nephrectomies, and cystectomies.

### Robotic- Assisted Radical Prostatectomies

Robotic- assisted radical prostatectomies (RARP) are the most common robotic procedure worldwide.<sup>44</sup> While there have not been any randomized clinical trials to date to show the superiority of this approach compared to the open approach, RARP has been associated with a shorter hospital stay, less estimated blood loss, fewer perioperative complications, and equivalent oncologic outcomes.<sup>44</sup>

The anesthetic plan for a RARP is similar to that for a laparoscopic prostatectomy. A standard intravenous induction followed by tracheal intubation is required. Most clinicians advocate for two intravenous (IV) lines, as the patient’s arms will be tucked at his sides after he is placed in the lithotomy position. A blood bank sample is necessary, as trocars may invade large blood vessels unexpectedly. Neuromuscular blockade is also of extreme importance, as the movement by the patient while the robot is docked may lead to significant and serious complications.

Patients are often placed in a steep (25 to 45 degrees) Trendelenburg position to facilitate surgical exposure of deep pelvic organs.<sup>36,45</sup> As described above, insufflation of the abdomen with steep Trendelenburg positioning leads to increased ICP and intraocular pressures. As a result, several cases of postoperative blindness and visual field defects have been reported during RARP.<sup>46,47</sup> Gastric contents

may drain via gravity over the face and onto the eyes and potentially cause ocular damage. Although the exact mechanism of ocular damage is not completely understood, decompressing the stomach with an orogastric tube may help minimize the risk of a trocar advancing into the stomach in addition to protecting the patient’s eyes. In addition, increased ICP can lead to strokes or hemorrhages in vulnerable populations.

Fluid management is also crucial during robot prostatectomies and requires a delicate balance. While a liberal fluid strategy is associated with an increased risk of postoperative anastomotic leak<sup>48</sup> and worsening of glottic and periocular edema, a restrictive fluid approach has been associated with acute tubular necrosis. Lower extremity compartment syndrome risk is associated with longer cases, obesity, and poor positioning.<sup>49</sup>

### Robotic- Assisted Radical Cystectomy (RARC)

Radical cystectomy is the gold standard treatment for muscle invasive and high grade non-invasive bladder cancer. Cystectomies are traditionally difficult operations, as fluid shifts, fluid management, and patient comorbidities present unique challenges. Perioperative management of these patients is sufficiently challenging that nearly 25% of patients are readmitted to the hospital following radical cystectomy.<sup>50</sup> The RARC, while not inferior to open radical cystectomy (ORC) regarding disease progression and recurrence, has lower estimated blood loss, shorter length of stay, and longer operative times than ORC.<sup>51</sup> Of note, there does not appear to be a difference in two- week readmission rates.<sup>52</sup>

Anesthetic considerations for patients receiving roboticassisted radical cystectomy mirror those for patients receiving robotic- assisted radical prostatectomy. The patient will be positioned in a steep Trendelenburg position, and pneumoperitoneum will be obtained. Some surgeons prefer a fluid restrictive management approach until the ureteral anastomosis is completed. Given the patient population’s frailty, the surgery’s length, and the potential for fluid shifts, anesthesia providers can consider an arterial line for frequent gas sampling to guide fluid therapy.

### Robotic- Assisted Nephrectomy

Nephrectomies are performed to address a multitude of kidney pathologies. In general, a simple nephrectomy is a preferred method for non- neoplastic disease, whereas radical nephrectomies are mainly used for neoplastic disease.<sup>53</sup> This procedure can be done using an open, laparoscopic, and robotic technique, each with its indications and advantages. In tumors amenable to minimally invasive surgery, the robotic approach is gaining popularity as it provides similar outcomes to its laparoscopic counterpart while giving the surgeon finer manipulation, higher definition display, and an increased degree of articulation of the surgical arms.<sup>54,55</sup> Anesthetic considerations in robotic- assisted nephrectomy are similar to other robotic urologic surgeries except for positioning. The patient will be in lateral decubitus with various degrees of Trendelenburg and flexing; therefore, particular attention should be placed on padding pressure points, preventing nerve injury, and maintaining a neutral spine.

### GYNECOLOGIC SURGERY

Gynecologists were also early adopters of robotic surgery, with the first tubal anastomosis done with robotic assistance in 2000.<sup>56</sup> The FDA approved robotic surgery for gynecologic procedures in 2005, and since then, the applications of robotic surgery in gynecology have expanded to include hysterectomy, salpingo- oophorectomy, vesicovaginal fistula repair, sacrocolpopexy, and ovarian cystectomy.<sup>56</sup>

### Robotic- Assisted Hysterectomy

After the first gynecologic robot- assisted surgery, robotic assistance was quickly applied to hysterectomies, the most common gynecologic procedure in the United States.<sup>57,58</sup> However, despite the large number of patients receiving robotic hysterectomies, it is unclear whether robotics is superior to traditional laparoscopic approaches.<sup>59</sup>

Robotic surgery for endometrial cancer resection requires more surgical operating time, particularly as surgical teams become proficient in performing the surgery; however, the surgical time appears to improve after 20 to 30 cases.<sup>60,61</sup> There has been increased concern about the utilization of the robot during endometrial cancer resection surgery since the outcomes appear similar to laparoscopic hysterectomies, but the cost and operative times are greater.<sup>43,62</sup> The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recommends vaginal hysterectomy as the preferred approach for benign disease, where feasible, due to its lower complication rate and faster recovery time. ACOG states that robotic surgery needs to be better studied to discover if this approach would best serve a particular subgroup of patients.<sup>43</sup>

### Other Gynecologic Surgeries

Due to the steep learning curve for sacrocolpopexy, robot assistance is believed to facilitate this technically difficult procedure. Randomized controlled trials have shown similar outcomes with robotic versus laparoscopic surgeries. However, robotic cases can have increased operative time, postoperative pain, and cost.<sup>43</sup> Myomectomies with robot assistance may allow for more minimally invasive surgeries that would otherwise occur due to fibroid location and high patient body mass index (BMI).

Robot- assisted surgery for gynecologic malignancies has been poorly studied, with only retrospective comparisons available. A recent meta- analysis on endometrial cancer concluded that robotic surgery, compared to laparoscopy, had a similar duration of surgery but a shorter hospital stay, less blood loss, fewer conversions to laparotomy, and fewer overall complications despite a higher cost.<sup>63</sup>

ACOG does not recommend using the robot for shortduration and low- complexity surgeries such as tubal ligation, simple ovarian cystectomy, ectopic pregnancy, or bilateral salpingo- oophorectomy.<sup>58</sup>

Anesthetic management for robotic gynecologic surgery is similar to the urologic procedure: arms tucked and inaccessible after robot docking in addition to steep Trendelenburg. Like in urology, it is preferable to have two IV lines as it is very difficult to place a second IV once the robot is docked.

### GENERAL SURGERY (SEE ALSO CHAPTER 14)

Robotic surgery in general surgery has continued to grow since the first robotic cholecystectomy in 1997.<sup>64</sup> While robotic surgery has gained traction with urology and gynecology, the growth in general surgery has been limited mainly due to an overall increase in cost without significant improvement in outcomes over laparoscopy.<sup>65</sup>

Procedures performed with robotic assistance today include inguinal/ventral hernia repairs, cholecystectomies, hepatobiliary, colorectal, and bariatric surgery. For most abdominal surgeries the arms will be tucked to allow for the docking of the robot, and the head of the patient will be on the same side as the anesthesiologist (Fig. 67.6).

### Inguinal Hernia Repair

For simple unilateral inguinal hernia repair, the robotic approach was more expensive and longer in duration without providing any advantage regarding postoperative pain, quality of life, and complications.<sup>66</sup>

### Cholecystectomy

Robot- assisted cholecystectomy can be done with only one incision on the abdomen compared to laparoscopy.<sup>67</sup> While this allows for less surgical scarring, data on postoperative pain are heterogeneous.<sup>68</sup> However, robotic- assisted cholecystectomy is associated with a significantly higher risk of re- operation and incisional hernia.<sup>69–71</sup> Furthermore, depending on the surgeon’s experience, robotic cholecystectomy showed increased operative times compared to laparoscopy.<sup>67</sup>

### Colorectal

Robotic- assisted colorectal dissection is considered safe and feasible with similar outcomes to the laparoscopic approach: no difference in hemoglobin change or conversion rate between the two approaches while showing equal oncologic radicality.<sup>72</sup> While some surgical steps – such as rectal mobilization – are facilitated by the robot, surgical time for some surgeries has been particularly long due to the need to reposition the robot and its arms for other steps (i.e., splenic flexure mobilization). Robotic colectomies have similar postoperative outcomes to their laparoscopic counterpart but were associated with statistically significant increases in operative costs and operative times.<sup>73</sup> Furthermore, robotic lower anterior resections (LAR) were associated with a higher rate of diverting ostomy but with lower conversion rates and lower septic complications.<sup>74</sup>

### Hepatobiliary

The liver’s unique location in the abdominal cavity and abundant blood supply initially slowed surgeons from adopting laparoscopic techniques.<sup>75</sup> The benefits of laparoscopic liver resections are well known and include less blood loss and shorter hospital stay than its open counterpart without compromising tumor recurrence, tumor spread, or patient survival.<sup>76</sup> While robotic surgeries are associated with increased intraoperative costs, overall costs of these robotic procedures may be decreased as they generally are associated with shorter lengths of stay compared to open procedures.<sup>77</sup> Robotic surgery in managing patients requiring hepatobiliary surgery remains an evolving field.

### Gastrectomy

Gastrectomy is the main treatment for gastric cancer. Anesthetic management for robotic upper abdominal surgery has different challenges: the patient will be positioned in reverse Trendelenburg,<sup>2</sup> which has the advantage of increased head and neck venous drainage and reduction in ICP, and less likelihood of passive regurgitation. The FDA approved transoral robotic surgery (TORS) in 2009 for treating all benign and selected malignant head and neck tumors. TORS allows for a three- dimensional

### ROBOTIC- ASSISTED THORACOSCOPIC SURGERY (SEE CHAPTER 49)

In the last two decades, robotic- assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) has been more frequently used as a new modality in thoracic surgery. RATS is considered a minimally invasive version of video- assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). RATS has been used for cancerous tumor resection (wedge resections and lobectomies), thymoma resection, fundoplication surgery, esophagectomy, hiatal hernia, and lung volume reduction.<sup>87–94</sup> VATS is preferred to open procedures when possible to reduce the length of stay, blood loss, pain, and morbidity as long as clinical outcomes are equivalent.<sup>95</sup> However, it is difficult to perform complex surgeries that contain confounded anatomic barriers

<!-- PDF page 11 -->

(e.g., a pneumonectomy or a thymectomy) using minimally invasive techniques such as VATS.

The growing popularity of RATS is largely attributed to the three- dimensional stereoscopic image of the robotic binocular eye, which increases precision and allows for more complex surgeries with similar benefits as open procedures.<sup>96,97</sup>

### Anesthetic Considerations

Preoperative consideration of RATS, including preoperative assessment, rehabilitation, and medical optimization, is similar to the VATS approach. The most common system that is used for RATS is the da Vinci Robotic Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) with four arms, as described at the beginning of this chapter.

Intraoperative consideration is also similar to VATS, but as the robotic device has to be positioned in close vicinity of the surgical table and considering the size and complexity of the robotic equipment and its arms, there is limited accessibility to the patient for both anesthesiologist and the surgeon. Positioning of the anesthesia machine, breathing circuit, and venous and arterial lines must adapt to this limitation<sup>98</sup> (Fig. 67.8).

In general, the airway management of RATS is similar to VATS and generally performed under one- lung ventilation, achieved either by double- lumen tubes or bronchial blockers. The anesthesiologist should be aware of the displacement of the airway tube during positioning; even small changes may impair one- lung ventilation. Intraoperative confirmation of lung isolation will be difficult, so a plan for accessing the airway with a fiberoptic bronchoscope should be established before initiating the robotic portion of the case. It is important to note that a failure of lung isolation will result in an inability to complete the surgery via RATS.<sup>93,99–101</sup>

Patient positioning is particularly important to expose largely inaccessible areas, as mediastinal structures are heavy and mobile and change position in response to gravity. To access the anterior mediastinum, the patient should ideally be placed in a supine or slight lateral decubitus position (raising one side 15–30 degrees). This position requires the elevated arm to be at the patient’s side as far back as possible, allowing the robot to dock successfully. However, hyperabduction of the elevated arm can cause a brachial plexus injury.<sup>102,103</sup> For lobectomies and hilar masses, the patient should ideally be positioned in a full lateral decubitus position (90 degrees). Alternately, a prone or slightly modified prone position can create better exposure for posterior mediastinal masses. The operating table might be extended up to 30 degrees for optimal exposure. The head and neck should be supported appropriately to avoid cervical spine injuries.<sup>88,89,104</sup> The robotic instruments are inserted through trocars between ribs using intercostal incisions.

Since the patient will generally be rotated 90 degrees to allow the robot to dock, it is important to achieve lung isolation before rotation. This unique 90- degree rotation, not required in VATS procedures, puts the patient’s airway away from the anesthesiologist, and therefore the fiberoptic bronchoscope may need to be located on the other side of the table. ### Intrathoracic CO2 Insufflation

CO2 insufflation helps achieve adequate surgical exposure by mobilizing the mediastinum and simultaneously compressing the lung away from the operative site. Intrathoracic CO2 insufflation can produce similar physiologic effects as tension pneumothorax, and therefore the anesthesiologist should know the hemodynamic and respiratory effects of intrathoracic CO2 insufflation.<sup>105,106</sup> Intrathoracic CO2 insufflation can lead to increased intrathoracic pressure and therefore, added to ineffective ventilation, may cause respiratory acidosis. This effect is more pronounced with one- lung ventilation and possible absorption of CO2. Like laparoscopic procedures, CO2 is insufflated with a flow rate of 5–10 L/min and maximum intrapleural pressure of 5–10 mm Hg.<sup>107</sup> High intrathoracic insufflation can compress the mediastinal vessels and cause hemodynamic instability. CO2 insufflation can also cause venous gas embolism, decreased venous return, and cardiac collapse if right heart failure develops.<sup>108,109</sup> To prevent barotrauma, a tidal volume of 5–6 mL/kg (ideal body weight) and peak pressure of 30 cmH2O with permissive hypercapnia with a pH of 7.25 have been suggested.<sup>107,110,111</sup>

The anesthesiologist must also be ready for potential conversion to open thoracotomy due to bleeding or an inability to obtain adequate surgical exposure. In this case, undocking the robot immediately and being ready for open thoracotomy is needed.<sup>112–114</sup> In a recent meta- analysis, the incidence of conversion from RATS to open thoracotomy

<!-- PDF page 12 -->

during pulmonary resections was 0% to 19%.<sup>115</sup> An increase in operative issues must be anticipated for surgeons learning to use the robot.<sup>116,117</sup>

Another possible pitfall of RATS is that damage to the pleura may allow CO2 insufflation to spread to the ventilated lung, causing difficult ventilation and possibly causing a tension pneumothorax or severe subcutaneous emphysema – both of which can produce a hemodynamic compromise.<sup>116</sup> Hypotension and ventricular arrhythmias occur during robotic arm manipulation and usually concern manipulating the cardiac structure. Therefore, close observation of the surgical field on the monitor and communication with the surgical team is essential.

RATS’ extubating criteria and pain control modalities do not differ from other thoracic surgery. Multimodal analgesia with a combination of regional anesthesia and oral and IV analgesic medications seems to be the best strategy for post- RATS analgesia.<sup>97,107,118</sup> The need for neuraxial blocks such as thoracic epidural has decreased due to the minimally invasive nature of RATS. Surgeons’ subpleural paravertebral intercostal block has been used routinely for postoperative analgesia.<sup>118</sup>

Studies comparing outcome data from VATS versus RATS in more common procedures are increasing. As might be expected for any complex procedure, outcomes for RATS lobectomies are better at high- volume centers.<sup>119</sup> Despite increasing interest in teaching and improving outcomes of RATS, this educational path is still evolving. The presence of the second optional console (see Fig. 67.1) permits the presence of the second surgeon or trainee without compromising the patient’s outcome.<sup>120</sup>

While several centers are exploring RATS lobectomies, segmentectomies, and mediastinal mass resections, RATS pneumonectomies have only been reported at a few pioneering centers.<sup>121,122</sup> RATS is associated with increased operative time and cost compared to its alternative.<sup>93,123–127</sup> However, it reduces the length of hospital stay and postoperative pain.<sup>128–130</sup> In the case of esophagectomies, RATS has a similar outcome profile while having a longer surgical duration than a video- assisted minimally invasive approach.<sup>131</sup>

Although the cost of robotic surgery and the potential for serious malfunction of the robot remain a concern, robotic approaches in thoracic surgeries are being used in more complex surgeries, including pediatric patients.<sup>132,133</sup> It is expected that RATS will improve patient outcomes and possibly reduce costs.<sup>122,134</sup>

### CARDIAC (SEE ALSO CHAPTER 50)

Since the first reported robotic cardiac surgery (RCS) in 1998,<sup>135,136</sup> cardiothoracic applications of robotic- assisted surgery have expanded to involve congenital cardiac surgery, including atrial septal defect closures and patent ductus arteriosus ligations,<sup>137–145</sup> mitral valve repairs or replacement,<sup>146–151</sup> totally endoscopic coronary artery bypass grafting (TECAB) and minimally invasive direct coronary artery bypass (MIDCAB),<sup>152–156</sup> minimally invasive atrial fibrillation surgery and left ventricular pacemaker lead placement,<sup>157–162</sup> and other atrial procedures like tricuspid valve repair and myxoma resection.<sup>163–165</sup> Although minimally invasive surgery may eventually make surgical

sternotomy obsolete, surgeons must still be prepared to convert to an open sternotomy if needed.

The main difference between RCS and sternotomy cardiac surgery is the requirement of prolonged one- lung ventilation (OLV) and thoracic CO2 insufflation. Therefore, anesthesiologists must be familiar with cardiac (including intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography [TEE]) and thoracic anesthesia when performing robotic- assisted cardiac procedures. Poor pulmonary function test results or pulmonary hypertension may be contraindications to robotically assisted cardiac surgery since prolonged OLV may not be tolerated. Other relative contraindications of RCS include decompensated congestive heart failure (New York Heart Association [NYHA] class III–IV) or cardiomyopathy with ejection fraction (EF) &lt;30% with or without pulmonary edema, associated ventricular or aortic aneurysm, significant peripheral vascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, and high BMI (>35).<sup>166</sup>

Anesthesia preparation of RCS is similar to a cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) with sternotomy, except it involves preparation for OLV using different lung isolation techniques (see Chapter 49). The effects of CO2 thoracic insufflation were addressed earlier in this chapter during robotic- assisted thoracic surgery.<sup>109</sup> It is essential to use TEE during CPB placement for appropriate transthoracic aortic root cannulation, venous cannula, and mitral valve assessments.

Due to the proximity of the robotic arms and side cart to the patient’s torso, the anesthesiologist’s accessibility to the patient is limited. In the case of serious airway or cardiovascular events, the robot’s position will interfere with effective cardiopulmonary resuscitation or airway management. The surgical and anesthesia team should be able to disengage the robot if those emergencies arise.<sup>167</sup> The patient is usually in the left or right semi- lateral decubitus position. The side- up arm is flexed at the elbow and abducted from the torso. Caution is required to avoid brachial plexus stretch and injury in this position.<sup>102</sup>

After the achievement of the OLV, the robotic arms will access the thoracic cavity. As with most robotic- assisted surgeries, the operating table movement is locked after the robotic docking with the patient. Access to the heart is limited due to minithoracotomy, so external cardiac defibrillation may be used. Due to CO2 pneumothorax, the transthoracic electrical impedance and defibrillation insulators should be increased.<sup>168</sup> Occasionally, the chest cavity must be deflated to improve the defibrillator threshold.<sup>169</sup> Communication between the surgery and anesthesia teams is crucial for a successful RCS. The postoperative course of the RCS is similar to traditional cardiac surgery, with patient transfer to the cardiac surgery ICU and postoperative hemodynamic management.<sup>170</sup>

Unlike other robotic surgeries, the robot in robotic orthopedic surgery is used as a navigation- assisted device, which affords an interface through which the surgeon receives feedback from the robotic device regarding the threedimensional position of instruments in the surgical field to aid their coordination and guide the surgeon to perform the procedure more accurately. Robotic orthopedic surgery has focused on joint surgeries, especially knee and hip joint arthroplasties.<sup>183,184</sup>

### Robotic- Assisted Knee Surgeries (RAKS)

RAKS aims to provide more accurate surgical incisions, osteotomies, and soft tissue alignment to restore the best knee kinematics during total knee arthroplasty (TKA).<sup>185</sup> Multiple studies have shown that RAKS can restore the knee’s mechanical axis more accurately and improve the balance and position of the implant.<sup>185–189</sup> However, like other robotic or robotic- assisted surgeries, it will increase the duration of the surgeries (probably related to the learning curve among orthopedic surgeons) and cost.<sup>190–195</sup> Although RAKS improved early functional recovery and hospital discharge, there is insufficient data to support its long- term outcomes.<sup>196–202</sup>

In RAKS, predetermined parameters are set in the preoperative plan, and the semiactive robotic system stops the saw when the bone resection exceeds those parameters (Fig. 67.12A). Most systems use CT images to create a threedimensional (3D) image of the anatomic landmarks of the knee, or a digital knee representation will be generated. This image will allow intraoperative adjustments and alignments using the navigation system (like Stryker OrthoMap Express Knee Navigation System – Rio; Mako Stryker, Fort Lauderdale, FL).<sup>203</sup>

Anesthesia management of a patient having RAKS is very similar to a patient who is having a non- robotic TKA. Preoperative analgesia after the TKA emphasizes

multimodal analgesia with or without regional anesthesia methods like adductor canal block (see Chapter 42). In addition, the surgeon may use periarticular infiltration of the mixture of local anesthetic (like ropivacaine 0.2%) with morphine, ketorolac, and epinephrine. Key injection sites are the posterior capsule, medial and lateral capsular flaps, subcutaneous tissue, pin site, and drain site (if applicable).<sup>204</sup> Intraoperative management is similar to TKA, with some modifications in the patient arm positioning to generate space for the robot. This means that the arm and hand on the opposite side of the surgery are flexed and rotated over the thorax to create space for the robot to approach the surgical site.

### Robotic- Assisted Hip Surgeries (RAHS)

Unlike TKA, RAHS versus conventional total hip arthroplasty (THA) has produced mixed short- term and long- term outcomes regarding the functional status and disability.<sup>205–210</sup> It is believed that optimizing the positioning, alignment, and accurate implant fit will prevent artificial hip joint malfunction and revision.<sup>208,211–214</sup>

The basic concept and execution of RAHS are very similar to RAKS and started with preoperative planning using a CT- based 3D digital image of the hip joint to optimize acetabular cup implantation. The robot uses a semi- active system that allows surgeons to keep the depth and size of the acetabulum ream (Fig. 67.12B).<sup>215–217</sup> Anesthetic management of the RAHS is essentially similar to the traditional THA, considering that using a robot usually extends the time of the surgery due to the existing learning curve and increases cost.<sup>218–220</sup> However, compared to traditional THA, blood loss might be similar or more (especially in fem ales).<sup>207,219,221</sup>

### Other Orthopedic Surgeries

Robotic technology has been applied in orthopedic trauma, shoulder surgeries, and foot and ankle surgery, with insufficient or conflicting evidence to recommend for or against using robots for the abovementioned surgeries.<sup>184,222</sup>

Neurosurgery has also adopted robots to enhance the accuracy of specific neurosurgical procedures, especially robotic- assisted stereotaxy, deep brain stimulation (DBS), stereoelectroencephalography, and tumor biopsy or resection.<sup>223–230</sup>

### Stereotactic Neurosurgery

Stereotactic neurosurgery has been one of the main uses of robotic neurosurgeries. It has been used to optimize the accuracy of the lead placements for DBS and functional neurosurgery.<sup>228,231–235</sup> The ROSA Brain System (Medtech, Zimmer Biomet, Montpellier, France) for DBS stereoelectroencephalography is one of the most recent robots, which gained FDA approval for cranial surgery in 2012.

**Surgical and Anesthesia Workflow Description.** Anesthesia care for ROSA- assisted DBS (RADBS) is similar to the placement of DBS without a robot.<sup>236,237</sup> The procedure can be done under monitored anesthesia care (MAC), also called awake, or under general anesthesia (GA) with an endotracheal tube (GAET) with an arterial line for tight blood pressure control during the procedure. The patient may also need a Foley catheter as the procedure may take hours to perform. For the MAC patient, moderate or deep sedation can usually be planned for the pinning frame, Foley placement, skin incision, and eventually burr holes. The sedation agent, like propofol, is ideal as the patient needs to wake up later in the procedure and follow commands for neuromonitoring. However, despite controversies, it has been shown that GA compared to awake/MAC, does not affect the microelectrode recording.<sup>145,228,229,236,238,239</sup>

The procedure is usually performed in an operating room with a CT scan built in- place or with a CT scan close to the operating room. The Lekshell frame (Elekta) is the metal frame pinned on the patient’s skull and is used to register the ROSA system using four points on the frame. Enough local anesthetics are used at the site of the four screw points for moderate/deep sedation during pinning. Immediately after placement of the frame, a thin- sliced, volumetric CT scan is obtained. The CT scan images are combined with the ROSA software’s preoperative MRI. Then target planning, including trajectory angle and distance to the target, is accomplished with the same software (Fig. 67.13A). The patient position is usually a beach- chair position with the neck slightly extended. The height of the ROSA system limits the bed’s height. As soon as the ROSA Brain System is engaged, the operative bed position controls are disconnected to prevent accidental movement, which can cause serious injuries (Fig. 67.13B). Once the ROSA system is registered to that patient, the DBS entry points are marked, and 14- mm burr holes are produced using the small incisions. In the next step, ROSA’s Microdrive will be fixed for the DBS lead placement. At this point, the sedation level will usually be reduced to minimal or conscious sedation or awake stage, as the patient’s participation in the examination is needed. ### Robotic Spine Surgery

Robots have been used for intraoperative image- guided navigation during minimally invasive spinal surgeries.<sup>243–246</sup> The aim is to provide robotic assistance combined with intraoperative fluoroscopy to provide 3D reconstruction images for intraoperative verification of implant/screw placement or spine biopsy.<sup>243,247</sup> Traditionally, a non- robotic navigation system with or without computer- assisted navigation has been used. Many robotic spine surgery platforms have been introduced, but their acceptance has been limited. More evidence is needed to support the efficacy, safety, and value of adding a robot to supine surgeries.<sup>247–251</sup>

## Summary

Anesthesia for robotic surgery is an exciting and dynamic field. As surgeons and patients look for innovations and technologically rich approaches to health care, it is reasonable to expect that more robotic procedures will be performed.
