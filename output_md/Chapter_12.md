### KEY POINTS

- Removal of carbon dioxide (CO2) is determined by alveolar ventilation, not by total (minute) ventilation.

- Dead space ventilation can be dramatically increased in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and pulmonary embolism to more than 80% of minute ventilation.

- Respiratory drive is regulated by central and peripheral chemoreceptors and by stretching/ irritation receptors in the lungs and rib cage.

- Hypoxemia can be caused by alveolar hypoventilation, diffusion impairment, ventilation– perfusion mismatch, and right- to- left shunt.

- Almost all anesthetics reduce skeletal muscle tone, which decreases functional residual capacity (FRC) to levels close to the awake residual volume.

- Atelectasis during anesthesia is caused by decreased FRC and the use of high fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), including breathing oxygen before induction of anesthesia.

- General anesthesia causes ventilation–perfusion mismatch (airway closure) and shunts (atelectasis).

- Venous admixture is due to V<sup>˙</sup> A/Q<sup>˙</sup> mismatch (response to increased FiO2) and shunts (unresponsive to increased FiO2).

- Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction is blunted by most anesthetics, and this results in increased ventilation–perfusion mismatching.

- Respiratory work is increased during anesthesia as a consequence of reduced respiratory compliance and increased airway resistance.

## Respiratory Physiology is Central in the Practice of Anesthesia

The profound comprehension of respiratory physiology is a fundamental requisite for every anesthesiologist. Anesthesiologists must be aware of the effects of both general and regional anesthesia on respiratory function and lung physiology to adequately prevent and treat the adverse respiratory events that can occur during anesthesia.<sup>1</sup> These events range from intractable hypoxemia caused by loss of airway patency to postoperative respiratory depression from opioids or regional anesthesia.<sup>2,3</sup>

Improved appreciation of anesthesia- induced physiologic alterations (e.g., mechanisms of bronchospasm,<sup>4</sup> impact of mechanical ventilation),<sup>5</sup> as well as pioneering developments in monitoring (e.g., pulse oximetry and capnography),<sup>6</sup> together are associated with the specialty of anesthesiology’s emergence as a leader in patient safety.<sup>7</sup> Finally, integrative measures of respiratory function, ranging from exercise capacity,<sup>8</sup> spirometry to tissue oxygenation,<sup>9</sup> to global O2 consumption,<sup>8</sup> may help predict outcomes following anesthesia and surgery.

## Ventilation

_Ventilation_ refers to the movement of inspired gas into and exhaled gas out of the lungs. The driving force for this gas movement, during spontaneous breathing, is provided by an interplay between the respiratory muscles (mainly diaphragm and intercostal muscle) and the rib cage. During inspiration the diaphragm pulls down the basal part of the lung while the external intercostal muscles elevate the rib cage, moving forward the sternum and increasing the anteroposterior diameter of the chest. The synergistic effect of diaphragm and the external intercostal muscle contraction reduces the pleural pressure (the pressure between the chest wall pleura and the lung pleura) from −5 cmH2O to −7.5 cmH2O (average pleural pressure oscillation during normal breathing), creating the pressure gradient necessary to move air inside the lung.

During quiet breathing, exhalation is a passive phenomenon since the pressure gradient necessary to exhale is generated by the elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall. During heavy breathing, however, the elastic forces are not enough to cause the necessary rapid expiration, so extra

force is achieved mainly by contraction of the abdominal muscles and internal intercostal muscles. Internal intercostal muscle contraction reduces the anteroposterior diameter of the chest wall, lowering the ribs while the abdominal muscles, pushing the abdominal organs against the diaphragm, compress the lung.

### ALVEOLAR VENTILATION

Fresh gas enters the lung by cyclic breathing at a rate and depth (tidal volume, VT) determined by metabolic demand, usually 7 to 8 L/min.<sup>10</sup> While most inspired gas reaches the alveoli, some (100–150 mL) of each VT remains in the airways and cannot participate in gas exchange. Such dead space (VD) constitutes approximately one-third of each VT.<sup>11</sup> Anatomic VD is the fraction of the VT that remains in the “conducting” airways, and physiologic VD is any part of a VT that does not participate in gas exchange (Fig. 12.1). Alveolar ventilation (VA) is defined as the volume of air entering and leaving the alveoli per minute.

### DEAD SPACE VENTILATION

Maintenance of partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2) is a balance between CO2 production (V<sup></sup> CO2, reflecting metabolic activity) and alveolar ventilation (V<sup></sup> A). If V<sup></sup> E is constant, but VD is increased, V<sup></sup> A will naturally be reduced, and the PaCO2 will therefore rise. Therefore, if VD is increased, V  E must also increase to prevent a rise in PaCO2. Such elevations in VD occur when a mouthpiece or face mask is used, and in such cases, the additional VD is termed “apparatus deadspace” (which can be up to 300 mL; anatomic VD of the airways is 100–150 mL).<sup>12</sup>

Increases in the volume of the conducting airways (e.g., bronchiectasis) increase the overall VD only slightly. Far more significant increases in VD occur when perfusion to many ventilated alveoli is interrupted, as occurs in a pulmonary embolus (see Fig. 12.1). Indeed, with multiple pulmonary emboli, VD/VT can exceed 0.8 (2.7- fold normal). In such a case, to maintain a normal V<sup></sup> A (5 L/min), the V<sup></sup> E would have to increase (also 2.7- fold) to almost 20 L/min. This effort would cause considerable dyspnea, in addition to the dyspnea induced by the lowered partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2).

Obstructive lung disease can result in diversion of inspired air into (nonobstructed) ventilated, but poorly perfused, regions of the lung. This results in local excesses of ventilation versus perfusion (high ratio) in such regions,<sup>13</sup> which is equivalent to an increase in VD/VT (see Fig. 12.1). Patients with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) may have a VD/VT ratio of up to 0.9 and would have to hyperventilate massively (30–50 L/min) to maintain normal PaCO2, which is not possible where ventilator reserve is diminished. Such patients demonstrate reduced V  A but often have an elevated V  E. An important compensatory mechanism is that a lower level of V<sup></sup> A will maintain stable CO2 excretion where the PaCO2 is increased (Box 12.1).

## Diffusion of Gas

Gas moves in the large and medium- sized airways by bulk flow (i.e., convection), meaning that the gas molecules travel together at a given mean velocity according to a driving pressure gradient. Flow is through multiple generations of bronchi, and the net resistance falls with each division. After the 14th generation, airways merge with alveoli and participate in gas exchange (respiratory bronchioles). The cross- sectional area expands massively (trachea, 2.5 cm<sup>2</sup> ; 23rd-generation bronchi, 0.8 m<sup>2</sup> ; alveolar surface, 140 m<sup>2</sup> ),<sup>14</sup> resulting in a sharp drop in overall resistance. Because the number of gas molecules is constant, the velocity falls rapidly, reaching zero when at the alveolar membrane. The velocity of the gas entering the alveolus is slower than the diffusion rates of O2 and CO2; therefore, diffusion—not convection—is necessary for transport in the distal airways and alveoli. Indeed, CO2 is detectable at the mouth after just seconds of breath- holding, because of rapid diffusion and because of cardiac oscillations (i.e., mixing).

Diffusion of a gas is a process characterized by a net transfer of molecules from a zone in which the gas exerts a high partial pressure to a zone in which it exerts a lower partial pressure. The driving force of this process is the random movement of molecules excluding both active transport and movement caused by a total pressure difference such as

flow during a normal tidal ventilation. The diffusion of a gas across a membrane is described by Fick’s law:


![](assets/chapter-12/12._Respiratory_Physiology_and_Pathophysiology.pdf-0003-15.png)


D  A  P<br>Vx   x<br>

Vx is the volume of gas transferred per unit of time, D is the gas diffusion coefficient (mL/min), A is the membrane area, Δ _P_ is the partial pressure difference of gas across the membrane and Δ _x_ is the membrane thickness.

According to the described equation the driving force of gas diffusion is the difference of partial pressure across the membrane (Δ _P_ ) and the diffusion process continues until an equilibrium is reached (Δ _P_ = 0).

Oxygen diffusion across the alveolar membrane can reach an equilibrium (alveolar O2 partial pressure (PO2) − end capillary PO2 = 0) when the pulmonary capillary transit time is normal (i.e., during quiet breathing). Pulmonary capillary transit time, defined as the pulmonary capillary blood volume divided by pulmonary blood flow, is the main factor determining oxygen diffusing capacity (defined by net rate of gas transfer divided by Δ _P_ ) across the alveolar–capillary membrane.<sup>15</sup> During heavy exercise, the pulmonary blood flow increases, causing a reduction of pulmonary capillary transit time: the time available for oxygen diffusion. In healthy subjects breathing air, there is generally no measurable fall in end capillary PO2. However, if the blood–gas barrier is markedly thickened by disease so that oxygen diffusion is impeded, PO2 may not reach that of alveolar gas before the time available for oxygenation in the capillary has run out. In this case, a measurable difference between alveolar gas and end- capillary blood for PO2 may occur. This phenomenon is worsened if the subject is breathing a hypoxic gas mixture because Δ _P_ is reduced.

Carbon dioxide (CO2) diffusion coefficient (D) is 20 times higher than oxygen diffusion coefficient so, for a stable Δ _P_ , CO2 diffuses 20 times faster than oxygen. Consequently, the main factor determining CO2 diffusing capacity is alveolar ventilation not pulmonary capillary transit time.

## Lung Perfusion and V˙ /Q˙ Match

### PULMONARY CIRCULATION

The pulmonary circulation differs from the systemic circulation: it operates at a five- to tenfold lower pressure while receiving the whole cardiac output, and the vessels are shorter and wider. There are two important consequences of the particularly low vascular resistance. First, the downstream blood flow in the pulmonary capillaries is pulsatile, in contrast to the more constant systemic capillary flow.<sup>16</sup> Second, the capillary and alveolar walls are protected from exposure to high hydrostatic pressures; therefore, they can be sufficiently thin to optimize diffusion (i.e., exchange) of gas but not permit leakage of plasma or blood into the airspace. Whereas an abrupt increase in the pulmonary arterial (or venous) pressure can cause breaks in the capillaries,<sup>17</sup> slower increases (i.e., months to years) stimulate vascular remodeling.<sup>18</sup> This remodeling might protect against pulmonary edema<sup>19</sup> (and possibly against lung injury<sup>20</sup> ), but diffusion will be impaired (i.e., in patients with chronic pulmonary hypertension).

### DISTRIBUTION OF VENTILATION AND PERFUSION IN THE LUNG

Pulmonary blood flow depends on driving pressure (mean pulmonary pressure minus left atrial pressure) and vascular resistance (driving pressure divided by cardiac output). Since the pressure drop along the pulmonary arterioles is low, compared to the systemic circulation, driving pressure and resistance are not homogeneous throughout the lung but are affected by many external factors such as gravity. The traditional thinking about lung perfusion emphasized the importance of gravity<sup>16</sup> ; however, factors other than gravity are also important.

Blood has weight and therefore blood pressure is affected by gravity. The height (base to apex) of an adult lung is approximately 25 cm; therefore, when a person is standing, the hydrostatic pressure at the base is 25 cmH2O (i.e., approximately 18 mm Hg) higher than at the apex. The mean pulmonary arterial pressure is approximately 12 mm Hg at the level of the heart, and the pulmonary artery pressure at the lung apex can therefore approach zero. Thus, less blood flow will occur at the apex (versus the base), and in the setting of positive pressure ventilation, the apical alveoli can compress the surrounding capillaries and prevent any local blood flow.

Based on such gravitational distribution of pulmonary artery pressure, as well as the effect of alveolar expansion, West and colleagues<sup>21</sup> divided the lung into zones I to III (Fig. 12.2). This system is based on the principle that perfusion to an alveolus depends on the pressures in the pulmonary artery (PPA), pulmonary vein (PPV), and alveolus (PALV). In the apex (zone I), the key issue is that pulmonary arterial pressure is less than alveolar pressure; therefore, no perfusion occurs. Zone I conditions can exist during mechanical ventilation and be exacerbated by low PPA. Whenever zone I conditions exist, the nonperfused alveoli constitute additional dead space (VD). Below the apex in zone II, PPV is less than alveolar pressure, and the veins are collapsed except during flow, as in a “vascular waterfall.” Although PALV is always greater than PPV, perfusion occurs when PPA exceeds PALV (i.e., intermittently, during systole). Below this zone is zone III, in which there are two important differences: PPA and PPV both always exceed PALV. As a result, there is perfusion throughout systole and diastole (and inspiration and expiration). Gravity results in equal increases in both PPA and PPV toward the lung base; therefore, gravity cannot affect flow throughout zone III by increasing the PPA to PPV pressure gradient alone. Nonetheless, it is possible that the greater weight of the blood nearer the base results in vessel dilatation, thereby lowering vascular resistance and increasing flow.<sup>16</sup> It was subsequently recognized that there is also a decrease in perfusion in the lung base, or zone IV, that is thought to occur because of the effects of gravity compressing the lung at the bases—and the blood vessels therein—and thereby increasing vascular resistance.<sup>22</sup>

Finally, additional evidence for the effect of gravity comes from volunteer experiments in which gravity was increased or abolished by altering the flight pattern of a jet aircraft.<sup>23</sup> In these experiments, zero gravity decreased cardiac oscillations of O2 and CO2 during a breath- hold, indicating development of more homogeneous perfusion. ### HYPOXIC PULMONARY VASOCONSTRICTION

Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) is a compensatory mechanism that diverts blood flow away from hypoxic lung regions toward better oxygenated regions.<sup>33</sup> The major stimulus for HPV is low alveolar oxygen tension (PAO2), whether caused by hypoventilation or by breathing gas with a low PO2 and is more potent when affecting a smaller lung region. The stimulus of hypoxic mixed venous blood is weaker.<sup>34,35</sup> Whereas in humans halothane, isoflurane, fluroxene, and nitrous oxide were thought to inhibit HPV more than intravenously based anesthesia, modern volatile anesthetics, including sevoflurane<sup>36</sup> and desflurane,<sup>37</sup> have little effect.

During intravenously based anesthesia, exposure of one lung to a fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) of 1.0 and the contralateral lung to a hypoxic gas mixture (FiO2, 0.12 to 0.05) reduced perfusion to the hypoxic lung to 30% of the cardiac output.<sup>38</sup> Continuous or intermittent HPV, such as living at high altitude<sup>39</sup> or in presence of chronic hypoxemic lung disease, can lead to vascular remodeling and development of chronic pulmonary hypertension.

Pulmonary vascular resistance normal values are 0.25– 1.6 mm Hg·min/L or 37–250 dynes/sec/cm<sup>5</sup> .<sup>40</sup>

While the greatest part of systemic vascular resistance is in the arterioles, in the pulmonary circulation the pressure drop is equally distributed between arteries, capillaries, and veins. Extra- alveolar vessels, with muscular vessel walls, are mainly involved in active control of pulmonary vascular resistance while vascular resistance in intra- alveolar

vessels is mainly dependent on alveolar pressure and pulmonary blood flow.

The active control of pulmonary vascular resistance is maintained by a complex interplay between the endothelium and the vascular smooth muscle cell. The most important molecule involved in this interplay is nitric oxide (NO). NO is produced in the endothelial cell by the conversion of L- arginine to L- citrulline by an enzyme called nitric oxide synthase. NO is a gas able to rapidly diffuse across the cell membrane into the smooth muscular cell of the pulmonary vessel. NO stimulates soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) to synthesize cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) that activates cGMP- dependent protein kinase (PKG) leading to vascular relaxation. The physiologic actions of cGMP are limited by its hydrolysis to GMP by cyclic nucleotide phosphodiesterases (PDE), a family of proteins that hydrolyze cyclic nucleotides. It is important to understand this pathway since it is the target of drugs widely used for the treatment of pulmonary hypertension. Gaseous NO became in the past 20 years the cornerstone of pulmonary hypertension therapy. NO inhalation causes selective pulmonary vasodilation without systemic vasodilation since NO rapidly reacts with circulating hemoglobin exerting only a “local” effect on pulmonary vascular smooth muscle cell. Moreover, the intracellular NO concentration may be increased by PDE5 inhibitors such as sildenafil and vardenafil. They induce pulmonary vasodilation, inhibiting PDE5, the enzyme responsible for endogenous NO degradation in the smooth muscle cell.<sup>41</sup>

Other than the discussed active regulation of pulmonary vascular resistance, pulmonary circulation is highly affected by cardiac output and alveolar pressure/ volume.

The pulmonary circulation is highly compliant, able to adapt to large changes of cardiac output with minimum change in arterial pressure. When an acute increase in pulmonary blood flow occurs (i.e., in case of lung resection), the total cross- sectional area of the pulmonary vascular bed increases, reducing the overall pulmonary resistance. These adaptations to increased flow occur partly by passive distention of vessels and partly by recruitment of collapsed vessels such as capillaries in zone I.

<!-- PDF page 6 -->

Alveolar pressures and volumes greatly influence pulmonary vascular resistance. The effect of lung volume is different in extra- alveolar or alveolar vessels. Extra- alveolar vessels run through the lung parenchyma. As the lung expands, the diameter of these vessels increases via radial traction of the vessel walls, reducing vascular resistance as the lung volume increases. Conversely, during lung collapse, vascular resistance increases due to the unopposed action of vessel elasticity.

Alveolar capillaries include capillaries and vessels in the corner of the alveolar walls. The determinant of the amount of distention within these vessels is their transmural pressure (intravascular pressure minus alveolar pressure). During inspiration, alveolar pressure rises, compressing the surrounding alveolar capillaries. Stretching and thinning of the alveolar walls reduces capillary caliber and ultimately leads to an increase in pulmonary vascular resistance at large lung volumes.

Combining the behavior of extra- alveolar and alveolar vessels, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) at increasing lung volumes shows a characteristic U shape (Fig. 12.4), with PVR higher at residual volume and total lung capacity and lower at functional residual capacity.<sup>42</sup>

where CaO2 (O2 content) is milliliters of O2 per 100 mL of blood, SaO2 is the fraction of hemoglobin (Hb) that is saturated with O2, O2- combining capacity of Hb is 1.34 mL of O2 per gram of Hb, _Hb_ is grams of Hb per 100 mL of blood, PaO2 is the O2 tension (i.e., dissolved O2), and solubility of O2 in plasma is 0.003 mL of O2 per 100 mL plasma for each mm Hg PaO2.

Each hemoglobin molecule reacts with 4 molecules of oxygen. Their binding is reversible and depends on PO2: when PO2 is high, as in the pulmonary capillaries, O2 binds with the hemoglobin, but when PO2 is low, as in the tissue capillaries, O2 is released from the hemoglobin. This is the basis for almost all O2 transport from the lungs to the tissues.

The hemoglobin dissociation curve is not steady but can be displaced by CO2 partial pressure, pH, temperature, and 2,3- biphosphoglycerate (BPG) concentration. The increase of PCO2 (or a reduction of pH), temperature, and BPG concentration shift the Hb–O2 dissociation curve to the right.<sup>43</sup> A shift of the oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve to the right in response to increases in blood CO2 and hydrogen ions has a significant effect by enhancing the release of O2 from the blood in the tissues and enhancing oxygenation of the blood in the lungs. In the systemic capillaries, the PCO2 is higher than in the arterial blood (and the pH correspondingly lower) because of local CO2 production. These circumstances shift the Hb–O2 dissociation curve to the right, which increases the offloading of O2 to the tissues. The opposite occurs in the pulmonary capillaries; here, the PCO2 is lower (and the pH correspondingly higher) because of CO2 elimination, and the dissociation curve is shifted to the left to facilitate O2 binding to Hb. BPG is a byproduct of anaerobic glycolysis. In hypoxic conditions the quantity of BPG in the blood increases considerably, shifting the O2–hemoglobin dissociation to the right, improving oxygen release to tissues.

Hemoglobin’s crucial role in O2 delivery can be better understood by analyzing the effect of characteristic abnormalities of blood O2 carriage such as methemoglobinemia or carbon monoxide poisoning.

Methemoglobin (MetHb), formed by the oxidation to Fe<sup>3+</sup> (ferric) instead of the usual Fe<sup>2+</sup> (ferrous) iron, is less able to bind O2, resulting in diminished O2 content and less O2 delivery. Here, the PaO2 (in the absence of lung disease) will be normal: if the O2 content is calculated from the PaO2, it will appear normal, but if directly measured, it will be low. In contrast, MetHb level will be elevated. In severe cases, lactic acidosis develops because of impaired O2 delivery. In addition, because MetHb has a blue–brown color, the patient will appear blue, even if the fraction of MetHb is modest; specialized oximetry can separately measure MetHb levels.<sup>44,45</sup> The apparent cyanosis is not responsive to supplemental O2, and therapy involves converting (i.e., reducing) the MetHb to Hb (e.g., by using methylene blue). Important medical causes of MetHb include benzocaine, dapsone, and nitric oxide.

In CO poisoning, the CO binds to Hb, with far greater (over 200- fold) avidity than molecular O2, tightly forming CO–Hb and resulting in two main effects.<sup>46</sup> First, formation of CO–Hb results in fewer sites available for O2 binding, and this reduces the blood O2 content. Second, the formation of CO–Hb causes conformational changes in the Hb molecule such that the tendency to release bound O2 is reduced. This effect corresponds to a leftward shift of the Hb–O2 dissociation curve, and although this aspect of CO binding does

not reduce the O2 content or “global” delivery of O2, it does reduce the release of O2 and its local delivery to the cells. Because the color of CO–Hb closely resembles that of O2– Hb, the color of the blood (and the patient) is bright red; however, as with MetHb, the PaO2 will be normal (assuming no pulmonary disease) as will be the calculated CaO2; however, the measured CaO2 will be low and, if severe, a lactic acidosis will be present. Modern pulse oximeters can distinguish between Hb–O2 and CO–Hb.<sup>46</sup>

### OXYGENATION IN THE PULMONARY ARTERY

The central venous and pulmonary artery oxygen saturation are physiologic variables routinely measured in the intensive care unit setting since they can provide an insight on patient oxygen consumption and delivery.

Systemic venous blood (central venous blood) enters the right ventricle via the right atrium. The O2 saturation (SO2) differs among the major veins: higher venous SO2 reflects greater blood flow, less tissue oxygen uptake, or both.<sup>47</sup> SO2 is usually higher in the inferior vena cava (IVC) than in the superior vena cava (SVC), possibly because of the high renal and hepatic flow relative to O2 consumption. In the right ventricle, the central venous blood (ScvO2) from the SVC and IVC is joined by additional venous blood from the coronary circulation (via the coronary sinuses). In the right ventricle, an additional small amount of venous drainage from the myocardium enters through the thebesian veins, and as all this venous blood enters the pulmonary artery, it is well mixed and is termed _mixed venous blood_ (SvO2); thus SvO2 &lt; ScvO2, although the trends of each usually run in parallel.<sup>48</sup>

SvO2 and ScvO2 monitoring gained a key role within early goal- directed therapy protocols and has been shown to decrease mortality when taken and analyzed appropriately.<sup>49</sup> Indeed, ScvO2 >70% or mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2) >65% are usually recommended.

### RESPIRATION IN THE CELL

The ultimate goal of the respiratory and cardiovascular system is to deliver oxygen into the mitochondrion.

The partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) in normal arterial blood is approximately 100 mm Hg and decreases to 4 to 22 mm Hg in the mitochondrion, where it is consumed. Glucose (C6H12O6) is converted into pyruvate (CH3COCOO<sup>−</sup> ) and H<sup>+</sup> by glycolysis in the cytoplasm, and the pyruvate diffuses into the mitochondria and forms the initial substrate for Krebs cycle. At the end of the Krebs cycle are produced six nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), two flavin adenine dinucleotide (FADH2), two adenosine triphosphate (ATP), four CO2 for each glucose molecule. The NADH is a key electron (and H<sup>+</sup> ) donor in the process of oxidative phosphorylation, wherein O2 and adenosine diphosphate (ADP) are consumed, and ATP and H2O are produced. Thus the net effect is oxidation of glucose to produce energy (ultimately as ATP), H2O, and CO2.<sup>50</sup>

### TRANSPORT OF CO2 INTO THE BLOOD

CO2 is produced by metabolism in the mitochondria, where the CO2 levels are highest. The transport path (involving progressively decreasing pressure gradients) is from

mitochondria through cytoplasm, into venules, and finally, in mixed venous blood from where it is eliminated through the alveoli. In the blood, CO2 is transported in three main forms: dissolved (reflected as PaCO2, partial pressure; accounts for approximately 5% of transported CO2), bicarbonate ion (HCO3<sup>−</sup> ; almost 90%), and carbamino CO2 (CO2 bound to terminal amino groups in Hb molecules; approximately 5%).<sup>50</sup> The usual quantities of CO2 in the arterial and (mixed) venous blood are approximately 21.5 and 23.3 mmol of CO2 per liter of blood, respectively.

The hemoglobin affinity for CO2 is not stable but depends on hemoglobin oxygen saturation: the difference in the amount of CO2 carried by oxygenated versus deoxygenated hemoglobin is called the Haldane effect<sup>51</sup> (Fig 12.5). The Haldane effect can be explained by two mechanisms. First, increased PaO2 decreases the ability to form carbamino compounds—reducing the amount of CO2 bound to Hb—thereby raising the amount of dissolved CO2 (i.e., elevated PCO2). Second, the amino acid histidine, which has an imidazole group that is an effective H<sup>+</sup> buffer at physiologic pH, is an important linking molecule between heme groups and the Hb chains. Increasing the partial pressure of oxygen (PO2) increases the amount of O2 bound to Hb; this changes the conformation of the Hb molecule, which in turn alters the heme- linked histidine and reduces its H<sup>+</sup> buffering capacity. Therefore, more H<sup>+</sup> is free (not buffered) and binds to HCO3<sup>−</sup> , releasing stored CO2. This phenomenon has important clinical applications and explains why breathing O2 can induce hypercapnia in patients with severe chronic lung disease. ### COMPLIANCE OF THE RESPIRATORY SYSTEM

The lung is like a rubber balloon that can be distended by positive pressure (inside) or negative pressure (outside). Under normal circumstances, inflation of the lung is maintained because although the pressure inside (alveolar pressure) is zero, the outside pressure (i.e., the pleural pressure) is sufficiently negative. The net distending pressure, which is the difference of the (positive) airway pressure (PAW) and the (negative) pleural pressure (PPL) is termed the _transpulmonary pressure_ (PTP). Thus:

Clearly, increasing the PAW increases the PTP. In addition, lowering the PPL (which is usually negative and making it more negative) also increases the PTP.

_Compliance_ —the reciprocal of elastance—is the term that expresses how much distention (volume in liters) occurs for a given level of PTP (pressure, cmH2O); it is usually 0.2 to 0.3 L/cmH2O.<sup>24</sup> However, although higher values of PTP maintain greater levels of lung opening, the relationship— as with most elastic structures—between applied pressure and resultant volume is curvilinear (Fig. 12.6).<sup>16</sup> Lung compliance depends on the lung volume; it is lowest at an

Chest wall impedance is not noticed during spontaneous breathing because the respiratory “pump” includes the chest wall. Chest wall mechanics can be measured only if complete relaxation of the respiratory muscles can be achieved<sup>54</sup> ; however, during mechanical ventilation, the respiratory muscles can be completely relaxed. As the lung is inflated by PAW, the properties of the chest wall will determine the resulting change in PPL. Under these circumstances, the increase in lung volume per unit increase in PPL is the chest wall compliance. Values of chest wall compliance are about the same as that of the lung and are reduced with chest wall edema, pleural effusions, and diseases of the costovertebral joints.<sup>54</sup>

### Tissue

Although not intuitively obvious, resistance of the lung tissue is the applied pressure on tissue divided by the resulting velocity of tissue movement. There are various approaches to determining this in humans, including separately considering the PV characteristics using plethysmography (where the area of the PV curve corresponds to work against total pulmonary resistance) and esophageal pressure (where the area of the PV curve corresponds to work against “tissue”

resistance).<sup>62</sup> Alternative approaches mathematically model the lung responses to varying respiratory frequencies.<sup>63</sup> Lung tissue resistance amounts to 20% of the total resistance to breathing; it can be increased threefold or fourfold in chronic lung disease<sup>64</sup> and is reduced by panting respirations.<sup>65</sup> Finally, in ARDS the chest wall resistance is increased.<sup>66</sup>

### INERTIA OR ACCELERATION OF GAS AND TISSUE

A final component of the total impedance to breathing is inertance, or the pressure required to accelerate air and tissue during inspiration and expiration. This component is minor, however, and can hardly be measured under normal breathing, regardless of whether the lungs are healthy. Nonetheless, tissue inertia is large during rapid ventilation,<sup>67</sup> and it could be important during the rapid, shallow breathing characteristic of weaning failure or during highfrequency oscillation.

### FUNCTIONAL RESIDUAL CAPACITY

The amount of air in the lungs after an ordinary expiration is called _functional residual capacity_ (FRC); it is usually 3 to 4 L and occurs because of the balance of inward (lung) forces and outward (chest wall) forces. The inward force is the “elastic recoil” of the lung and emanates from the elastic lung tissue fibers, contractile airway smooth muscle, and alveolar surface tension. The outward force is developed by passive recoil from the ribs, joints, and muscles of the chest wall. FRC is greater with increased height and age (loss of elastic lung tissue), and smaller in women and in obesity.<sup>10,68</sup>

There are two reasons why maintenance of gas in the lung at end- expiration (i.e., FRC) is important. First, inflating an already opened (inflated) lung is easier than when the lung is deflated. This is because complete collapse results in liquid- only surfaces interfacing in alveoli (high surface tension), whereas alveoli in partially inflated lung have air–liquid interfaces (lower surface tension). Second, although perfusion in the lung is phasic, the frequency is rapid and the oscillations in flow are low, resulting in nearly continuous flow. Ventilation is different: the frequency is far slower and the size of the oscillations far larger. If the lung (or large parts of it) completely deflate between breaths, the blood flowing from closed alveoli (that contain zero O2) would have very low SO2 (the same as mixed venous blood); this would mix into the overall blood flow from the lungs and cause a major O2 desaturation after every exhalation.

### INPUTS TO THE RESPIRATORY CENTER

The main inputs to the respiratory centers affecting the breathing pattern arise from: the central and peripheral chemoreceptors, sensing changes in arterial blood gases; stretch and irritant receptors in the lungs, diaphragm, and the rib cage, regulating reflex responses; the central nervous system and muscle afferents regulating the ventilatory response to exercise, and finally from several cortical

afferents, modulating the breathing pattern through projections to the respiratory centers and the pons.<sup>78</sup>

### Effect of pH and CO2

Changes in PCO2 and pH are sensed by both peripheral and central chemoreceptors and induce changes in the respiratory centers’ output. Central chemoreceptors consist of glutaminergic neurons, which are mainly located at the retrotrapezoid nucleus on the ventrolateral surface of the medulla, and respond to changes in cerebrospinal fluid proton concentrations, likely via pH- sensitive potassium channels. Because the cerebrospinal fluid contains few buffering proteins, abrupt changes in CO2 levels cause greater alterations in H<sup>+</sup> levels than in the blood, while in metabolic acid–base derangements equilibrium between plasma and cerebrospinal fluid is reached with some delay. Peripheral chemoreceptors are located in the aortic and carotid bodies and respond rapidly to changes in arterial pH.

### Effect of Oxygen

The main chemoreceptors mediating the ventilatory response to hypoxemia are the carotid bodies, small clusters of oxygensensitive cells located at the carotid bifurcation. Acute hypoxemia results in a rapid increase in ventilation, peaking within a few minutes, but soon declining, to a level above resting ventilation. The magnitude of this response varies greatly not only among healthy individuals but also among the same individual at different time points. With gradually developing hypoxemia, ventilation changes minimally with mild hypoxemia (PaO2, 60–70 mm Hg), but at lower PaO2, it increases progressively with hypoxemia (see Fig. 12.12). Hypercapnia enhances the acute hypoxic response, as hypoxemia enhances hypercapnic responses, so that the ventilatory response to an acute interruption of breathing (asphyxia) is greater than the sum of the expected response to either change alone. Sustained hypoxemia (such as in high altitude) also leads to increased ventilation. Increase in tidal volume is largely responsible for the initial ventilatory rise with hypoxemia, while breathing frequency is increased with prolonged hypoxic exposures. Hypoperfusion of the carotid bodies produces an effect similar to hypoxemia. Nitric oxide has an inhibitory effect on carotid body hypoxemic chemoreception. Finally, very severe hypoxia of the medulla may abolish respiratory neuronal activity.<sup>78,79,82</sup>

### Other Inputs: Reflex Responses and Cortical Inputs

The most well- described reflex response is the Hering– Breuer reflex, which is important in balancing the rate and depth of ventilation. The afferent pathway includes slowly adapting mechanoreceptors associated with airway smooth muscle of the tracheobronchial tree, reaching the respiratory centers through the vagus. The Hering–Breuer reflex decreases inspiratory time in response to lung inflation. Other reflex responses include deep breaths and cough, mediated via rapidly adapting pulmonary receptors in response to decreased lung compliance, rapid shallow breathing in response to stimulation of pulmonary J receptors, and coughing and sneezing in response to stimulation of irritant receptors in lungs, airways, larynx, and nose.

The diving reflex is activated by strong activation of receptors in the face, nose, and pharynx via the trigeminal nerve, and receptors in the larynx via the superior laryngeal nerve, and causes apnea, also accompanied by a vagally mediated bradycardia and peripheral vasoconstriction.

Finally, cortical inputs can modulate the breathing pattern, either by (temporarily) overriding the automatic control of breathing (i.e., voluntary apnea), or as part of responses to sensory or emotional stimuli.<sup>83–85</sup>

### OUTPUT OF THE RESPIRATORY CENTER

The respiratory centers’ output travels from the medulla and pons to the upper motor neurons, the junction with the respiratory motor neurons in the anterior horn cells in the cervical spine, finally leading to activation and contraction of the respiratory muscles, and generation of inspiratory (and expiratory) flow.

Normally the changes in ventilation are primarily achieved through changes in effort and thus tidal volume, and to a lesser extent by changes in breathing frequency as shown in Fig. 12.12. Yet, when further increase in tidal volume is limited, such as when approaching total lung capacity, or in the presence of diaphragm weakness, breathing frequency may increase disproportionately to tidal volume.

In clinical practice the output of the respiratory centers (respiratory drive) cannot be directly quantified, but can be estimated using the rate of increase of the respiratory muscles motor output, such as the electrical activity of the diaphragm (dEAdi/dt), or the pressure generated by the diaphragm (dPdi/dt) or all the respiratory muscles, as indicated by the esophageal pressure (dPes/dt) or airway pressure (if the airway is occluded and there is no change in lung volume, dPaw/dt). A commonly used index of respiratory drive is the pressure drop generated against an occluded airway during the first 100 ms of inspiration (P0.1), as this can be easily measured in many ventilators. Clearly, any condition affecting the efferent respiratory centers’ pathway, globally (such as neuromuscular blockade) or locally but closer to the respiratory centers than the point of measurement (e.g., in the anterior horn cells), will lead to underestimation of the respiratory drive.<sup>79,80</sup>

## Mechanisms of Hypoxemia and Hypercapnia

In the previous sections we have discussed ventilation, diffusion, perfusion, and gas transport, all of which affect oxygenation and CO2 elimination. This section details the mechanisms of hypoxemia and hypercapnia, focusing on conditions relevant to anesthesia practice. The mechanisms . . of hypoxemia include right to left shunt, V/Q mismatch, impaired diffusion, alveolar hypoventilation, and low FiO2. The mechanisms of hypercapnia include alveolar hypoven-. . tilation (absolute or relative) and V/Q mismatch.

An increase in metabolic rate triggers an increase in ventilation from the control of breathing system. Yet, in subjects unable to increase ventilation, for example sedated patients under controlled ventilation, an increase in VCO2, for example due to fever, will result in increase in PaCO2.

## Lung Function Tests

Postoperative pulmonary complications after major surgery are common and associated with significant morbidity and health care costs. Therefore, it is important to assess pulmonary risk in all preoperative patients. A thorough history and physical examination for lung disease remains the cornerstone of preoperative pulmonary assessment. In patients with known risk factors, preexisting lung diseases, and thoracic surgery candidates, pulmonary function tests complement preoperative evaluation and risk stratification. Additionally, pulmonary function test results are used in the staging and prognosis of chronic lung diseases such as COPD and interstitial lung diseases and can provide helpful information in the decision- making process of critically ill patients.

### SPIROMETRY

The spirometry test consists of an inhalation to total lung capacity (TLC) followed by a forceful exhalation to measure the forced vital capacity (FVC), the forced expired volume in 1 second (FEV1), and the forced expiratory flow at 25%– 75% of FVC (Fig. 12.21). A reduction in the FEV1/FVC ratio below 70% indicates an obstructive airway disease, and the reduction of FEV1 from its predicted value is used for staging of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. An FEV1 &lt;60% of predicted is associated with increased risk of serious postoperative pulmonary complications. Different thresholds of FEV1 are also used to guide lung cancer resection surgery decisions. Spirometry can also help identify variable intrathoracic or fixed (intra- or extrathoracic) airway obstruction from the shape of the forced expired flow–volume curve

(Fig. 12.22). A decreased FVC with preserved FEV1/FVC ratio indicates a restrictive lung disease.<sup>97,98</sup>

### MEASUREMENT OF LUNG VOLUMES

Measurement of lung volumes (Fig. 12.23) requires a method to estimate the gas that remains in the lungs at end  expiration, which cannot be calculated from spirometry. The methods used to measure lung volumes include nitrogen washout and body plethysmography, and less commonly helium dilution. Body plethysmography method applies Boyle’s law, which states that, under constant temperature, the product of volume and pressure of a constant mass of gas at any given moment remains the same, to measure lung volumes. The patient sits in an airtight, constant- volume chamber called the body box, and makes inspiratory efforts against a closed shutter equipped with a pressure transducer, and, as the diaphragm contracts causing the pressure inside the thorax to decrease and the volume of air to increase, the pressure in the box increases proportionately, allowing FRC or RV to be calculated. The nitrogen washout technique is based on the principle of conservation of mass of the inert gas, nitrogen. As the subject inhales 100% oxygen, nitrogen concentration in the exhaled gas is measured by dedicated microprocessors. When nitrogen disappears from the exhaled air, the test is terminated, the volume of nitrogen computed, and FRC calculated, on the basis that this nitrogen volume represents 80% of the lung gas contained at the beginning of the test. Commercially available ventilators allow estimation of FRC using modification of this technique.

## Effect of Anesthesia on Respiratory Physiology

### LUNG VOLUME AND RESPIRATORY MECHANICS DURING ANESTHESIA

Anesthesia impairs pulmonary function, whether the patient is breathing spontaneously or is receiving mechanical ventilation. Impaired oxygenation of blood occurs in most subjects who are anesthetized,<sup>101</sup> and this is why supplemental O2 (FiO2 usually 0.3–0.5) is almost invariably used. Mild to moderate hypoxemia (SaO2, 85% to 90%) is common and lasts from seconds to minutes; sometimes it is severe, and approximately 20% of patients may suffer from SaO2 less than 81% for up to 5 minutes.<sup>102</sup> Indeed, greater than 50% of claims in anesthesia- related deaths relate to hypoxemia during anesthesia.<sup>2</sup> Beyond the operating room, the alterations in lung function acquired during anesthesia persist: clinically significant pulmonary complications can be seen in 1% to 2% of patients after minor surgery, and in up to 20% of patients after more major upper abdominal or thoracic surgery.<sup>103</sup> Such consequences of anesthesia place prime importance on ascertaining the causes of perioperative respiratory dysfunction and the clinical approaches to treatment.

In this section, we describe the effects of anesthesia and mechanical ventilation on lung function. The arrangement of this section parallels the sequence of events involved in oxygenating the blood and removing CO2. Thus, the first phenomenon that might be seen with anesthesia is loss of muscle tone with a subsequent change in the balance between outward forces (i.e., respiratory muscles) and inward forces (i.e., elastic tissue in the lung) leading to a fall in FRC. This causes or is paralleled by an increase in the elastic behavior of the lung (reduced compliance) and an increase in respiratory resistance. The decrease in FRC affects the patency of lung tissue with the formation of atelectasis (made worse with the use of high concentrations of inspired oxygen) and airway closure. This alters the distribution of ventilation and matching of ventilation and blood flow and impedes oxygenation of blood and removal of CO2.

### Lung Volume

Resting lung volume (i.e., FRC) is reduced by almost 1 L by moving from upright to supine position; induction of anesthesia further decreases the FRC by approximately 0.5 L.<sup>104</sup> This reduces the FRC from approximately 3.5 to 2 L, a value close to RV. General anesthesia causes a fall in

FRC (approximately 20%), whether breathing is controlled or spontaneous,<sup>70,73</sup> and whether the anesthetic is inhalational or intravenous,<sup>105</sup> this is a major contributor to lowered oxygenation (discussed later). Muscle paralysis in the context of general anesthesia does not cause additional reduction in FRC.

The anatomic basis of the FRC reduction is not well understood. A landmark experiment on three volunteers using two- dimensional tomography suggested that a cephalad shift of the diaphragm, induced by anesthesia and paralysis, was responsible.<sup>106</sup> Recent studies using CT scanning also suggest cephalad diaphragm shift, as well as a decrease in the transverse chest area.<sup>105,107</sup> However, other data suggest little role for the diaphragm, with possible caudal (not cephalad) shift of its anterior aspect.<sup>108</sup> Simple CT suggests a cranial displacement, except in severe obstructive lung disease. Although the anatomic components of reduced FRC are debatable, the mechanism appears to be related to loss of respiratory muscle tone. FRC is maintained by a balance of the forces inward (lung recoil) versus forces outward (chest wall recoil, chest wall muscles, diaphragm). For example, maintenance of muscle tone using ketamine as the anesthetic does not reduce FRC.<sup>105</sup> Because patients are usually supine, the FRC will already have been reduced, and in elderly patients, this is particularly the case; in this context, the effects of anesthesia are more marked (see Fig. 12.10). As can be seen in the figure, FRC decreases with age if weight does not change.

### Compliance and Airway Resistance During Anesthesia

Static compliance of the total respiratory system (lungs and chest wall) is reduced on average from 95 to 60 mL/cmH2O during anesthesia.<sup>109</sup> Most studies of lung compliance during anesthesia indicate a decrease compared with the awake state, and pooled data from several studies suggest that anesthesia is associated with a reduction in mean static compliance from almost 190 to approximately 150 mL/ cmH2O.<sup>109</sup> Data on changes in respiratory resistance are less clear. Although most studies suggest that anesthesia increases respiratory resistance, especially during mechanical ventilation,<sup>109</sup> no studies have corrected for lung volume and flow rates (both affect resistance considerably), and it is possible that changes in resistance occur merely because of volume (i.e., FRC) loss (Fig. 12.24).

### EFFECTS OF ANESTHESIA ON VENTILATION– PERFUSION AND GAS EXCHANGE

### Distribution of Ventilation

Redistribution of inspired gas away from dependent to nondependent lung regions has been demonstrated, using isotope techniques in anesthetized supine humans. Radiolabeled aerosol and SPECT demonstrate that ventilation is distributed mainly to the upper lung regions, with a successive decrease toward the lower lung regions, and an absence of ventilation in the lowermost regions, a finding consistent with the atelectasis demonstrable using CT (Fig. 12.25).<sup>113</sup>

### Distribution of Lung Blood Flow

The distribution of lung blood flow has been studied by injection of radioactively labeled macroaggregated albumin and SPECT.<sup>114</sup> During anesthesia, a successive increase in perfusion occurs from upper toward lower regions, with a slight drop in perfusion in the lowermost portion of the lung, which is atelectatic on simultaneous CT (see Fig. 12.25). ### ONE-LUNG VENTILATION

Oxygenation can be a challenge during one- lung surgery. One lung is not ventilated but is still perfused, and in the postoperative period, restoration of lung integrity and ventilation–perfusion matching can take time.<sup>130</sup>

The technique of one- lung anesthesia and ventilation means that only one lung is ventilated and that the lung provides oxygenation of—and elimination of CO2 from— the blood. Persisting perfusion through the nonventilated lung causes a shunt and decreases PaO2 (Fig. 12.27); measures can be taken to reduce this blood flow.<sup>131,132</sup>

During one- lung anesthesia, there are two main contributors to impaired oxygenation: (1) the persisting blood flow through nonventilated lung and (2) development of atelectasis in the dependent lung, resulting in local shunt . . and low VA/Q.<sup>133</sup> A recruitment maneuver can identify the influence of the dependent atelectasis<sup>134</sup> ; serial increases in peak airway pressure and PEEP directed to the dependent, ventilated lung increased significantly the PaO2, indicating that dependent atelectasis was an important cause of hypoxemia. In this situation, diversion of perfusion from the dependent (ventilated) to the nondependent (i.e., nonventilated) lung would have worsened oxygenation.

Recruitment can also affect VD. Recruitment during onelung anesthesia improved oxygenation, but also decreased VD.<sup>135</sup> The slope of the CO2 curve during a tidal expiration (phase III) was flatter, indicating a more even distribution of inspired gas throughout the lung and more synchronous alveolar emptying. Thus, a secondary effect of recruiting collapsed lung tissue can be (presumably not when recruitment causes overinflation) more even distribution of ventilation and a decrease in the dead space fraction. This

effect should facilitate the use of a smaller VT. In contrast to an individual recruitment, the application of continuous elevated PAW (PEEP titrated to optimal compliance in the ventilated lung) increased compliance by 10% but slightly worsened oxygenation, probably because of redistribution of blood from the ventilated to the nonventilated (nondependent) lung.<sup>136</sup> The rationale for identifying and using optimal PEEP has also been reviewed.<sup>137</sup>

Maneuvers can also be applied to the nondependent lung. The effects of compressing the nondependent lung on oxygenation were examined using an intra- arterial O2 sensor, which provides instantaneous and continuous PaO2.<sup>138</sup> Compression resulted in increased PaO2, suggesting a shift of blood flow from the nondependent (nonventilated) to the dependent (ventilated) lung; development of complete absorption atelectasis in the nondependent lung may have similar effects.<sup>139</sup>

NO alone has little effect,<sup>140</sup> but oxygenation is improved when NO is combined with almitrine.<sup>141,142</sup> Almitrine alone also improves oxygenation<sup>143</sup> at a dose that does not alter PPA or cardiac output. ## Postoperative Respiratory Complications

### MECHANISMS OF POSTOPERATIVE ATELECTASIS

The development of intraoperative atelectasis is as a cause of impaired oxygenation and reduced respiratory compliance during anesthesia.<sup>158</sup> This phenomenon causes a progressive decrease in compliance that paralleled decreases in oxygenation in both anesthetized humans and experimental animals, which was interpreted as progressive of atelectasis. However, others noticed an abrupt decrease in compliance and PaO2 during induction of anesthesia, and yet atelectasis could not be shown on conventional chest radiography.

Since then, CT scanning has improved our knowledge of the nature of anesthesia- induced atelectasis, and the technique reveals prompt development of densities in the dependent regions of both lungs during anesthesia (data up to 1990 reviewed by Moller and associates).<sup>102,159</sup> Morphologic studies of these densities in various animals supported the diagnosis of atelectasis. An example of atelectasis as seen on a CT scan is shown in Fig. 12.28.

Atelectasis develops in approximately 90% of patients who are anesthetized, but it is unrelated to the choice of anesthesia.<sup>160</sup> It is seen during spontaneous breathing and after muscle paralysis, and with either intravenous or inhaled anesthetics.<sup>105</sup> The atelectatic area near the diaphragm is usually 5% to 6% of the total lung area but can easily exceed 20%. The amount of lung tissue that is collapsed is larger, because the atelectatic area consists mostly of lung tissue, whereas normal aerated lung consists of 20% to 40% tissue (the rest being air). Thus, 15% to 20% of the lung is atelectatic during uneventful anesthesia, before surgery has commenced; it decreases toward the apex, which usually remains aerated (Fig. 12.29). However, this degree of atelectasis is larger (upward of 50% of lung volume) after thoracic surgery or cardiopulmonary bypass and can last for several hours.<sup>161</sup> Abdominal surgery adds little to the atelectasis, but after such surgery, it can persist for several days.<sup>129</sup>

### Recruitment Maneuvers

A sigh maneuver, or a large VT, has been suggested for reversing atelectasis<sup>50</sup> ; however, atelectasis is not uniformly reduced by a VT increase or sigh up to PAW of 20 cmH2O.<sup>70</sup> Instead, a PAW of 30 cmH2O is required for initial opening, and 40 cmH2O for more complete reversal (Fig. 12.32). In the presence of normal lungs, such inflation is equivalent to a VC and can therefore be called a _VC maneuver_ (albeit achieved with positive PAW). In addition, a significant hemodynamic effect is likely if the VC maneuver is sustained; in fact, inflation with a PAW of 40 cmH2O for 7 to 8 seconds appears to successfully open almost all anesthesia- induced atelectasis.<sup>70</sup>

### Minimizing Gas Resorption

Although recruitment of anesthesia- induced atelectasis is completely possible with either PEEP or a VC maneuver, continuous application of some level of PEEP is required to prevent rapid recurrence of the atelectasis.<sup>164</sup> However, nitrogen (N2)—an insoluble gas that is not absorbed into the blood—can “splint” the alveolus if the alveolus is already opened. As a result, in anesthetized patients, a VC maneuver followed by ventilation with a gas mixture containing 60% N2 (40% O2) reduced the propensity for reaccumulation of atelectasis, with only 20% reappearing 40 minutes after recruitment.<sup>165</sup>

Here, the aim is to prevent O2 desaturation (i.e., gain an O2 safety margin) during induction before the airway has been secured when the anesthesiologist can better manage ventilation and oxygenation. Traditionally, the application of FiO2 1.0 has been used. Although the SaO2 is usually well maintained with this approach, atelectasis inevitably forms. ### Maintenance of Muscle Tone

Because loss of muscle tone in the diaphragm or chest wall appears to increase the risk of atelectasis, techniques that preserve muscle tone may have advantages. Intravenous ketamine does not impair muscle tone and is the only individual anesthetic that does not cause atelectasis. If neuromuscular blockade is added, atelectasis occurs as with other anesthetics.<sup>106</sup> Ketamine is an extremely useful anesthetic in special circumstances but has challenges with widespread use.

An experimental approach is restoration of respiratory muscle tone by diaphragm pacing. This approach is achieved with phrenic nerve stimulation, and it can modestly reduce the degree of atelectasis; however, the effect is minor, and the approach is complicated.<sup>168</sup>
