Intraoperative neurophysiologic monitoring (IONM) is used to assess and monitor the integrity of critical neural structures that are placed at risk during the perioperative period. Neurophysiologic monitoring encompasses several distinct techniques or modalities that are used during diverse surgical procedures and even postoperative settings.

Techniques for monitoring fall into two broad categories: techniques that assess metabolic integrity of the nervous system and those that assess functional integrity. Techniques that monitor metabolic integrity assess global or regional blood flow/oxygen delivery to the nervous system while techniques that monitor functional integrity assess the actual function of a limited number of defined anatomic structures and pathways in the nervous system. The term neurophysiologic monitoring implies that the data for the assessment of the integrity of the nervous system are acquired on a continuous or frequent intermittent basis rather than just at the beginning and end of a procedure.

The procedures and settings in which neurophysiologic monitoring is typically applied share the characteristic that changes detected in the monitored signals can be corrected or minimized by either modifying the surgical approach or manipulating physiologic and pharmacological parameters under the control of the anesthesiologist. During certain procedures, the monitoring may help direct the surgical approach, such as localization of the motor cortex during tumor resection surgery or the neurologic examination during “awake” craniotomy. In other procedures, the monitoring is used more passively to detect unwanted insults to the nervous system that is placed at risk.

In many procedures that require neurophysiologic monitoring, the monitored target is also susceptible to drugs administered during the course of an anesthetic. The anesthesiologist and the surgeon should be aware of the limitations inherent in individual monitoring techniques and of nonsurgical factors that influence the monitoring results.

The monitoring approach ideally should anticipate nonsurgical factors and provide a degree of redundancy using multiple techniques that can help distinguish a localized surgical trespass from a systemic event. Likewise, the anesthetic approach should facilitate the neurophysiologic monitoring techniques in a particular case and anticipate periods of particular risk by providing a stable level of anesthesia and physiologic support for the patient.

The application of neurophysiologic monitoring to a surgical procedure may be organized in different ways. In some instances, that may be as simple as setting up a piece of monitoring equipment, such as a facial nerve electromyography (EMG) monitor that provides acoustic feedback to surgeons when they get close to a nerve that innervates muscles. At the other end of the spectrum, it may require the dedicated services of a technologist and a neurophysiologist during the operation, as would be the case during the mapping phase of a brain tumor resection. Between those extremes are a variety of delivery models for IONM that may entail components of telemedicine. Regardless of the organizational structure, the utility of IONM will depend on a shared understanding of surgical objectives, anesthetic constraints, and limitations of monitoring. This shared understanding will then need to be supported by open lines of communications among the entire perioperative team, particularly during critical phases of an operation.<sup>1,2</sup>

For some surgical procedures, neurophysiologic monitoring is routinely employed because outcome data clearly support its use. Examples include correction of scoliosis and resection of vestibular schwannoma. In other cases, the approach to monitoring is based on local conventions and surgical expectations, rather than being supported by robust scientific evidence. In the latter case, monitoring utility depends even more on a good understanding of the technique’s capabilities and limitations by the perioperative team members. Good communication and mutual collaboration between anesthesiologists, surgeons, and the intraoperative monitoring team are paramount to allow timely corrective action in the face of signal changes and minimize false alarms that unnecessarily disrupt surgery. This chapter first discusses individual monitoring modalities so that the clinician can appreciate the inherent strengths and weaknesses of each technique. Subsequent sections apply this information by describing suitable approaches to various clinical settings that combine and integrate individual techniques to optimize neurologic outcome for patients, followed by a discussion of how to tailor the anesthetic approach to optimize the utility of neurophysiologic monitoring. The chapter ends with a brief discussion of how neurophysiologic monitoring is believed to be useful today, and where more work is needed to determine whether monitoring has a role for surgical patients in the future.

## Monitoring Modalities

### MONITORS OF ADEQUACY OF NERVOUS SYSTEM BLOOD FLOW

Adequacy of cerebral blood flow (CBF) can be monitored by two principal methods. The first method assesses blood flow

itself with the implicit assumption that “normal” flow provides adequately for the metabolic needs of the brain. The second approach assesses local or global oxygen delivery with the assumption that normal oxygen delivery at the site of measurement implies adequate blood supply throughout the central nervous system (CNS). To illustrate the limitations imposed by such implicit assumptions, let us examine global or hemispheric CBF in the context of a patient’s disease process.

In the normal brain, values of hemispheric CBF of approximately 50 mL/100 g/min reflect adequate oxygen delivery for maintaining structural integrity and function. Values of less than 20 to 25 mL/100 g/min are first associated with failure of function and on further decrease with structural damage.<sup>3</sup> It is important to note that in neurosurgical patients, CNS structural integrity and function may be altered by disease processes and anesthetics, which have an impact on the interpretation of measured CBF. For example, a CBF of 40 mL/100 g/min in a patient in a barbiturate coma after resection of an arteriovenous malformation may represent hyperemia (because metabolic demand is very low), whereas the same CBF in a patient with a mass lesion may reflect a modest decrease in cerebral perfusion pressure secondary to increasing intracranial pressure (ICP). Thus, the clinical response to an abnormal CBF value requires context and clinical judgment.

### Global Blood Flow Monitoring Techniques (Noninvasive)

**Intravascular Tracer Compounds.** Direct measurement of CBF is possible by determining the kinetics of wash- in and/ or wash- out of an inert tracer compound, a method originally described by Kety and Schmidt.<sup>4</sup> A widely used modern variation of this concept is the imaging of the first passage of intravascular contrast agents during computer tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for determining locoregional blood flow (Fig. 35.1). A less quantitative use of the same principles occurs during neurointerventional procedures, when the progression of a bolus dose of contrast agent is observed as it distributes through the arteries to a tissue phase followed by a venous phase, all imaged via X- ray at a fixed frame rate. While useful, these techniques share the limitation of providing a snapshot of CBF in time instead of continuous assessment of flow over time.

**Transcranial Doppler Ultrasound.** Transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasound is a technique that infers CBF from measurements of the blood flow velocity in the large conducting arteries of the brain. The TCD probe transmits pulses of sound waves through the thin temporal bone in a variation of the pulsed wave Doppler technique. When these sound waves are reflected off the red blood cells back toward the TCD probe, the velocity of the reflected sound waves is changed because the blood cells themselves are in motion toward or away from the probe. This phenomenon is known as the “Doppler shift,” and is directly related to flow velocity and flow direction of the blood cells. Blood flow is faster during systole and in the center of a vessel, whereas it is slower in diastole and near the vessel wall. TCD records a spectrum of flow velocities whose outline resembles an arterial waveform tracing. These concepts are illustrated in Fig. 35.2.

Intraoperatively, TCD measurements are most commonly made by continuous monitoring of the middle cerebral artery for the purpose of detecting either significant changes in flow velocity or the presence of particulate emboli.<sup>5</sup> As a diagnostic study, segments of all proximal intracranial arteries and the internal carotid artery of the neck can be insonated. An important limitation of TCD results from the fact that most of the examination is done through the temporal bone, which may be thick enough to preclude an adequate examination in 10% to 20% of patients.<sup>6,7</sup>

Two assumptions that are intuitive and plausible, but ultimately unproven, must be made for TCD- measured blood flow velocity to have a direct relationship to CBF. First, blood flow velocity is directly related to blood flow only if the diameter of the artery where the flow velocity is measured and the measurement angle of the Doppler probe remain constant. In practical terms, the difficulty with this assumption lies in finding a method to affix the TCD probe in a way that prevents dislodgement or movement during

monitoring. The second assumption requires that CBF in the basal arteries of the brain is directly related to cortical CBF. It is easy to understand how these two assumptions can constrain the utility of TCD as a stand- alone monitor of CBF. However, the changes in flow velocity seen in typical applications (discussed subsequently) are large enough to provide useful clinical information.

Some important advantages of TCD monitoring are that it is the only continuous technique that can provide early warning for hyperperfusion and that it detects the number of emboli delivered to the brain during various phases of an operation. Because of their high echogenicity, emboli show up in the TCD spectrum as high- intensity transient signals (HITS, see Fig. 35.2) and are easily identified as brief beeps or chirps within the background of the Doppler sounds.

The dominant jugular vein (i.e., the right for most patients) drains predominantly cortical venous blood, whereas the contralateral jugular vein drains more of the subcortical regions.<sup>8</sup> Despite such regional differences, Sjvo2 must be considered a monitor of global cerebral oxygenation because inadequate perfusion

**Cerebral Oximetry.** Cerebral oximetry is a noninvasive technique that uses reflectance oximetry to measure the oxygen saturation of the tissues underneath the sensor. Typically, two sensors are applied to both sides of the forehead. The light passes from a fiberoptic light source through the skull, scalp, and parts of the frontal brain and arrives back at the sensors. Contamination of the oximetry signal by extracranial blood sources is a concern, although the use of two sensing diodes with different distances from the light source within one sensor patch and adjustments of the algorithm of the oximeter help minimize this problem.<sup>9,10</sup>

Because 66% to 80% of the cerebral blood volume is venous blood, cerebral oximetry determines predominantly “local venous oxygen saturation.” The simplicity of its use,

and the familiarity with the principles of treating decreases in systemic mixed venous oxygen saturation, have made cerebral oximetry a popular trend monitor in operations that potentially cause decreases in blood flow to the vessels of the head.<sup>11,12</sup> There are some significant limitations to consider during its use. First, adequacy of global cerebral perfusion is inferred from measurements over the frontopolar brain. This assumption may not hold if, for example, the territory of the middle cerebral artery is at the center of an ischemic core as would be the case during clamping the carotid artery. Second, data on normal values or allowable changes for cerebral oximetry are largely absent. Therefore, rather than relying on absolute numbers, preoperative application of the sensors and obtaining initial measurements in conjunction with a neurologic baseline examination are important, so monitoring the trend during the procedure can be more meaningful.<sup>13</sup>

An example of how these limitations can be demonstrated is provided by a study of the use of cerebral oximetry during 100 carotid endarterectomies in awake patients.<sup>14</sup> Cerebral oximetry was able to identify 97.4% of patients with adequate CBF as indicated by the absence of clinical symptoms. Conversely, however, the monitor frequently indicated inadequate CBF, defined as a 20% decrease in cerebral oxygen saturation from the pre- clamp baseline, in patients without clinical symptoms of inadequate CBF. This false- positive rate of 66.7% may simply illustrate the fact that cerebral oxygen extraction increases before function fails. The real problem is that the lower limit for acceptable regional oxygen saturation is unknown in a large population of patients.<sup>15</sup> Acceptable values may be different from patient to patient, and addition of anesthetic drugs that influence cerebral oxygen metabolism may further complicate accurate interpretation.

### Tissue- Level Blood Flow Monitoring Techniques (Invasive)

Tissue- level monitoring for the brain is, by definition, invasive. All monitors in current clinical or research use are implanted through a burr hole, extend either into the white matter or ventricular system, and typically use a bolt for stabilization. They all share a 1% to 2% risk of bleeding, infection, or ischemia owing to the implantation procedure.<sup>15</sup> A second shared feature is their limited spatial resolution (i.e., each monitoring probe monitors only a limited area of brain surrounding the probe). When these monitors were first developed, there was considerable debate regarding the optimal placement of the device given such limited spatial resolution. Based on today’s appreciation for the impact of secondary neurologic insults on the ultimate outcome, there is growing agreement that tissue- level monitoring is best performed in morphologically and functionally normal tissue that is part of the penumbra or vulnerable zone of interest.<sup>16–18</sup>

Of the tissue- level monitors, tissue partial pressure of oxygen (Po2) monitoring has undergone sufficient refinement to be in wider clinical use. Thermal diffusion blood flow measurement,<sup>19</sup> laser Doppler flow measurement, and microdialysis are experimental and not in widespread clinical use.

### Tissue Partial Pressure of Oxygen Monitoring

Localized monitoring of tissue Po2 is based on an oxygensensitive electrode originally described by Clark.<sup>20</sup> The

diffusion of oxygen molecules through an oxygenpermeable membrane into an electrolyte solution causes an electric current that is proportional to Po2. Currently available catheter- based electrodes placed into the subcortical white matter provide stable recording conditions over long periods.

Most of the data on brain tissue oxygen levels (PBro2) come from studies in patients with head trauma.<sup>21</sup> Comparison with stable xenon CT for assessment of CBF and studies during temporal clipping in aneurysm surgery show good correlation between PBro2 and CBF.<sup>22,23</sup> Similarly, the time course of changes in PBro2 after traumatic brain injury resembles that of CBF.<sup>24,25</sup> Critics of the technique argue that PBro2 values are highly influenced by the partial pressure of arterial oxygen (Pao2) and are merely an elaborate indicator of the quality of patient ventilation. This view is supported by the observation that increasing the fraction of inspired oxygen (Fio2) increases PBro2, but likely represents an oversimplification.<sup>26</sup> Concurrent microdialysis studies have shown that increasing Fio2 not only increases PBro2, but also decreases tissue lactate levels, suggesting a true improvement in the metabolic milieu of the brain tissue itself.<sup>27,28</sup> While decreases in PBro2 are associated with worse outcomes in traumatic brain injury patients and the role of PBro2 -directed therapy is still investigational, there is evidence that ICP and PBro2- guided management of severe TBI may lower mortality and improve neurologic outcomes compared to ICP-guided management.<sup>29</sup>

### MONITORS OF NERVOUS SYSTEM FUNCTION

The most commonly used monitors of CNS function are the electroencephalogram (EEG), sensory evoked responses (SERs), motor evoked responses, and the electromyogram (EMG). The EEG is a surface recording of the summation of excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials spontaneously generated by the pyramidal cells in the cerebral cortex. The signals are very small, and each recording electrode records information both directly beneath the electrode and information volume conducted from deeper tissue.<sup>30</sup> Monitoring the EEG is usually directed toward one or more of four perioperative uses. First, the EEG is used to help identify inadequate blood flow to the cerebral cortex caused by either a surgically induced (e.g., retraction on cerebral tissue) or anesthetic- induced reduction in blood flow. Second, it may be used to guide an anesthetic- induced reduction of cerebral metabolism either in anticipation of a loss of CBF or in the treatment of high ICP. Third, the EEG may be used to predict the neurologic outcome after a brain insult. Finally, the EEG, particularly in the form of processed EEG, may be used to optimize anesthetic delivery (see Chapter 36).

Evoked responses or potentials are electrical activity generated in response to either a sensory or a motor stimulus along a neural pathway that goes beyond a single nerve and incorporates part(s) of the CNS. Measurements of evoked responses may be made at multiple points along the pathway. Sensory evoked responses are generally smaller than other electric activity generated in nearby tissue (muscle or brain) and are readily obscured by these other biologic signals. In the case of SERs, repeated sampling and sophisticated electronic summation and averaging techniques are

needed to extract the desired evoked potential signal from background biologic signals. There are three basic types of SERs: somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs), brainstem auditory evoked potentials (BAEPs), and visual evoked potentials (VEPs). Motor evoked potentials (MEPs) are larger and commonly do not require averaging.

### Electroencephalogram

**Basic Unprocessed Electroencephalogram Concepts.** The EEG is produced by a summation of excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials produced in cortical gray matter. Because the EEG signal is much smaller than action potentials recorded over nerves or from heart muscle, excellent electrical contact is necessary to avoid significant signal loss. Excellent contact may be achieved for surface electrodes by prepping the skin and using conductive gel, by placement of subdermal needles, or by saturating electrodes placed on the brain with an electrolyte solution.

EEG electrodes generally are placed according to a mapping system that relates surface head anatomy to underlying brain cortical regions. The placement pattern of recording electrodes is called a montage. Use of a standard recording montage permits anatomic localization of signals produced by the brain and allows development of normative EEG patterns and comparison of EEG recordings made at different times. The standard EEG “map” is called the 10 to 20 system for EEG electrode placement (Fig. 35.3). Amplitude is the size, or voltage, of the recorded signal and ranges commonly from 5 to 500 μV (vs. 1–2 mV for the electrocardiogram signal). Because neurons are irreversibly lost during normal brain development and aging, EEG amplitude decreases with age, starting in infancy. Frequency can be thought of simply as the number of times per second the signal oscillates or crosses the zerovoltage line. Time is the duration of the sampling of the signal. Time is continuous and without delay during standard digital EEGs but may be broken into sampling “epochs” during processed EEGs (see later).

**Normal Electroencephalogram.** Normal patterns seen on the EEG vary among normal individuals but are consistent enough to allow for accurate recognition of normal and pathologic patterns. The usual base frequency in an awake patient is the beta range (>13 Hz). This highfrequency and usually low- amplitude signal is common from an alert attentive brain and may be recorded from all regions. With eye closure, higher amplitude signals in the alpha frequency range (8–13 Hz), seen best in the occipital region, appear (Fig. 35.4). This “eyes closed” resting pattern is considered the baseline awake pattern when anesthetic effects on the EEG are described. When events that lead the brain to produce higher frequencies and larger amplitudes occur, the EEG is described as “activated,” and when slower frequencies are produced (theta = 4 to &lt;8 Hz, and delta &lt;4 Hz), the EEG is said to be “depressed.” The EEG during natural sleep may contain all these frequencies at various times. The slower frequencies occur during deep natural sleep with “sleep spindles” (Fig. 35.5), but during light sleep or rapid eye movement sleep, the EEG becomes activated, and the eye muscle EMG appears on the EEG.

In the normal EEG in awake and asleep patients, patterns recorded from corresponding electrodes on each hemisphere are symmetric in terms of frequency and amplitude, the patterns are predictable if clinical states are known, and spike (epileptic) waveforms are absent. In most cases, normal EEG patterns are associated with normal underlying brain function in awake and asleep patients.

**Processed Electroencephalogram Concepts.** Numerous limitations are introduced when moving from interpreting raw EEG data to understanding processed EEG. First, during processed EEG, artifacts are processed in many cases along with the desired signal, leading to a perfectly believable processed EEG result that is materially incorrect. Second, most available processed EEG devices use four or fewer channels of information – translating to at most two channels per hemisphere. Therefore, processed EEG devices generally monitor less cerebral territory than a standard 16- channel EEG. Third, some intraoperative changes are unilateral (e.g., regional ischemia owing to carotid artery clamping), and some are bilateral (e.g., EEG depression by bolus administration of an anesthetic). This means that processed EEG should have an adequate number of leads over both hemispheres to delineate unilateral from bilateral changes.

Most early studies validating intraoperative EEG monitoring used continuous visual inspection of a 16- to 32- channel analogue EEG by an experienced electroencephalographer – such monitoring was considered the gold standard.<sup>34,35</sup> Adequate studies comparing the processed EEG with fewer channels with this gold standard across multiple uses and operations have not been done. Limited data using processed EEG monitoring during carotid surgery suggest that two- or four- channel instruments typically detect significant hemispheric ischemia,<sup>36,37</sup> provided that the electrodes are appropriately placed over watershed areas of blood supply.

**Fourier Transformation of EEG.** EEG processing for intraoperative monitoring is typically based on power analysis of a segment of raw EEG over a specific period of time, referred to as an epoch. Power analysis uses Fourier

<!-- PDF page 8 -->

transformation to convert the digitized raw EEG signal into component sine waves of identifiable frequency and amplitude. The raw EEG data, which is a plot of voltage versus time, is converted to a plot of frequency and amplitude versus time. The data are then displayed either as a compressed spectral array or a density spectral array. In the compressed spectral array, frequency is displayed along the x- axis, and power is displayed along the y- axis with the height of the waveform equal to the power at that frequency. Time is displayed along the z- axis. Tracings overlap each other, with the most recent information in front (Fig. 35.6). Density spectral array also displays frequency along the x- axis, time is displayed along the y- axis, and power is reflected either by the density of the dots at each frequency or by a spectrum of colors. There is a relationship between epoch length and spectral resolution. A longer epoch may produce less epoch- to- epoch variability and allow more precise description of frequency and power. However, the longer epoch increases the delay before new information is processed and displayed, reducing the timeliness of information available for clinical decision- making. Many commercially available devices have used two- second epoch lengths, updated at varying user- selected intervals.

In combination with the loss of the occipital alpha activity, this phenomenon produces the appearance of a “shift” of the alpha activity from the posterior cortex to the anterior cortex. Further increases in the dose of inhaled or intravenous anesthetic drugs produce further slowing of the EEG. Some anesthetic drugs suppress EEG activity totally at sufficient doses (see Table 35.1). Other anesthetic drugs never produce burst suppression or an isoelectric EEG, despite increasing doses, either because they are incapable of completely suppressing the EEG (e.g., opioids, benzodiazepines) or because cardiovascular toxicity of the drug (e.g., halothane) prevents administration of a large enough dose.

A critical concept for the use of EEG in neurophysiologic monitoring is that the progression of EEG changes caused by increasing doses of volatile and most intravenous anesthetics closely parallels EEG changes caused by progressive cerebral ischemia. Therefore, using the EEG to monitor for ischemia not only requires an anesthetic plan that retains an active EEG, but also a stable level of anesthesia during phases of the operation where cerebral ischemia is a risk. Because of age- related changes in number and connectivity of neurons, retaining an active EEG is more difficult at the extremes of age.<sup>39</sup> Conversely, metabolic suppression of neuronal activity is more easily achieved. If EEG suppression is targeted during a surgical procedure to extend ischemia time, suppression of EEG activity for 50%–80% of an EEG epoch is typically targeted.

### Evoked Potentials

**Sensory Evoked Responses.** SERs are electric CNS responses to electric, auditory, or visual stimuli. SERs are produced by stimulating a sensory system and recording the resulting electric responses at various sites along the sensory pathway up to and including the cerebral cortex. Because of the very low amplitude of SERs (0.1–10 μV), it is often impossible to distinguish SERs from other background biologic signals, such as the EEG or EMG, which in this case may be considered undesirable “noise.” To extract the SER from the electrical background, the recorded signal is digitized, and signal averaging is applied. To accomplish this, signal recording is time- locked to the application of the sensory stimulus. For example, during intraoperative posterior tibial nerve SER monitoring, signal information occurring within 90 ms after nerve stimulation at the ankle is recorded (Fig. 35.7). Whereas the SER occurs at a constant time after stimulus application, other electric activity, such as spontaneous EEG, occurs at random intervals after the sensory stimulus. Repeated recordings – typically 50 to 500 – allow the electrical background noise to be averaged out. This signal averaging technique eliminates random elements and enhances the SER by improving the signalto- noise ratio.<sup>40</sup>

SER recordings are of two general types determined by the distance of the recording electrode from the neural generator of the evoked response. SERs recorded from electrodes close to the neural generators (within approximately 3–4 cm in the average adult) are termed “near- field potentials.”<sup>41</sup> Near- field potentials are recorded from electrodes placed very close to the actual signal generator site,<sup>42</sup> and the morphology is directly affected by electrode location.<sup>41</sup> Far- field potentials are recorded from electrodes located a greater distance from the neural generator and are conducted to the recording electrode through a volume conductor (brain, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and membranes). Because the current spreads diffusely throughout the conducting medium, it is more difficult to locate the source

of the recorded signal and the electrode position has less effect on the morphology of the recorded evoked potential (see Fig. 35.7).<sup>41,42</sup> As the distance between the recording electrode and the neural generator increases, the recorded SER becomes smaller. More responses must be averaged to record far- field potentials (e.g., several thousand) than near- field potentials (50–100).<sup>41,42</sup>

**Somatosensory Evoked Potentials.** SSEPs are recorded after electric stimulation of a peripheral mixed nerve using needles or surface gel electrodes and recording the evoked responses along various points along the sensory pathway. Cortical short- latency SSEPs are most commonly recorded intraoperatively because they are less influenced by changes in anesthetic drug levels. The pathway involved in the generation of upper extremity short-latency SSEPs begin with large-fiber sensory nerves that have their cell bodies in the dorsal root ganglia and central processes that travel rostrally in the ipsilateral posterior column of the spinal cord. These first-order neurons synapse in the dorsal

column nuclei at the cervicomedullary junction. From there, second-order fibers decussate and travel to the contralateral thalamus via the medial lemniscus and third-order fibers project from the thalamus to the frontoparietal sensorimotor cortex. These primary cortical evoked responses, which are recordable with most anesthetic techniques, result from the earliest electric activity generated by the cortical neurons and are thought to arise from the postcentral sulcus parietal neurons. While there are longer latency secondary cortical waves that are thought to arise in the association cortex, these are not usually monitored intraoperatively because they are severely suppressed by general anesthesia.<sup>41</sup>

Although most evidence indicates that upper extremity evoked potentials are conducted rostrally in the spinal cord through dorsal column pathways, some data suggest that lower extremity SSEPs are conducted at least partially by the lateral funiculus.<sup>43</sup> Stimulation of the posterior tibial nerve or common peroneal nerve at or above motor threshold activates group I fibers that synapse and travel rostrally through the dorsal spinocerebellar tract. After synapsing in nucleus Z at the spinomedullary junction, the pathway decussates and projects onto the ventral posterolateral thalamic nucleus.<sup>44</sup> This pathway difference is important because the dorsal lateral funiculus is supplied primarily by the anterior spinal artery, the artery that also supplies the descending motor pathway as well as neurons in gray matter of the spinal cord. Manipulations, such as distraction of the spinal column to correct scoliosis, which may secondarily compress or distort radicular blood supply to the anterior spinal cord, could cause changes in the SSEP in the event blood supply is reduced to critical levels. This hypothesis is verified by the very low, but not zero, incidence of postoperative paraplegia on awakening without any intraoperative changes in SSEPs.

**Transcranial Motor Evoked Potentials.** Although SSEP monitoring became widely adopted during scoliosis surgery and was credited with improving surgical outcomes, reports of postoperative paraplegia despite normal intraoperative SSEPs demonstrated the need for directly monitoring the integrity of the corticospinal motor tracts. MEPs are generated by applying a train of electric stimuli to the scalp and recording evoked responses along the spinal cord (D- waves) and most typically in peripheral muscles. The recording from the spinal cord requires placement of an epidural electrode, which is typically done by the surgeon after a laminectomy to access a spinal tumor, with one electrode cephalad and one caudad to the operative site. An advantage of such an epidural recording is that it only requires activation of the corticospinal tract, unlike muscle recordings, which require integration of diverse motor inputs at the level of the spinal alpha motor neuron as well as transmission at the neuromuscular junction. Since muscle activation involves passing the stimuli across the neuromuscular junction, neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) may obliterate MEPs entirely or significantly reduce their reliability. MEPs are especially useful during spinal surgery which risks directly injuring the nervous system because nerve transmission across the operative field can be assessed. MEPs may also be useful during aortic surgery because they can monitor for potential compromise to the blood supply to the anterior spinal cord.

Relative to SER monitoring, transcranial MEP monitoring requires much higher stimulus intensity (usually ≥400 V). This is because the applied electrical stimulus from the scalp electrodes must travel through scalp, skull, and CSF before reaching the pyramidal cells in the cortex to produce action

potentials that activate muscles. Very young children and adults with some degree of neurologic compromise at baseline are more challenging to monitor with MEPs and special adjustments to the stimulation parameters may be necessary.

Several variants of MEP monitoring exist. The most common method involves transcranial electrical stimulation.<sup>59</sup> During transcranial MEP (tcMEP) monitoring, stimulating electrodes (usually small, metallic screw- type electrodes like those used in fetal monitoring) are placed into the scalp overlying the motor cortex, and a train of electric stimuli (usually around 400–500 V) is applied to the scalp. This stimulation strongly activates muscles of mastication, and bilateral bite- blocks must be placed to prevent serious damage to the tongue during the recording of MEPs. If the precentral gyrus or motor strip is exposed during surgery, stimulating electrodes may be placed directly onto the cortex. Because approximately 80%–90% of the transcranial stimulus dissipates across the scalp and skull, stimulus intensities for direct cortical stimulation are significantly reduced to approximately 40 to 50 V.

Even though stimulation activates pyramidal cell axons, where they originate from the cell body, it is important to note that both stimulating methods also activate surrounding cortical structures and deeper subcortical white matter pathways (sensory and motor). In fact, tcMEPs can frequently be recorded from patients with cerebral palsy, despite the disruption of their cortical neuronal architecture, suggesting that the stimulation may directly activate structures deeper to the cortex. Distal antidromic propagation of the transcranially applied stimulus is blocked by synapses in all the ascending sensory pathways. The stimulus is propagated easily orthodromically through descending motor pathways. It is important to note that only the largestdiameter fibers, which comprise approximately 2% to 3% of all fibers in the corticospinal tract, propagate the impulses associated with tcMEPs. To enhance the waveforms, MEPs may be averaged in the same manner as SERs, but averaging

is almost always unnecessary. Activation of the corticospinal tract well below the motor cortex may limit the utility of tcMEP to assess the adequacy of CBF to the motor cortex, because activation may be distal to the site of ischemia in a part of the CNS perfused by the posterior cerebral circulation.

Transcranial electrical stimulation can also activate the corticobulbar tract, yielding compound muscle action potential (CMAP) responses from muscles innervated by motor branches of cranial nerves. These recordings present several challenges that limit their widespread adoption: (1) the muscle response is recorded in close proximity to the site of the stimulating electrodes and follows the stimulus with a short latency, making it difficult to separate stimulus artifact from response; (2) a large portion of the stimulating current dissipates around the scalp and may activate a cranial nerve after its exit from the cranial vault, thus providing no information about the corticobulbar tract or the intracranial portion of the monitored cranial nerve. Nonetheless, several authors have reported success and clinical utility of such recordings, especially for the facial nerve.<sup>60–62</sup>

**Anesthesia and Evoked Responses.** Many drugs used in the perioperative period can influence the ability to monitor evoked responses accurately (Table 35.3). SSEPs are the most completely studied with respect to the effects of anesthetic drugs. The effects of the currently used volatile anesthetics on cortical SSEPs are dose- dependent increases in latency, conduction times and a decrease in

**Electromyography.** Intraoperative monitoring of electromyography (EMG) responses generated by cranial and peripheral motor nerves allows early detection of surgically induced nerve damage and assessment of nerve function intraoperatively. During EMG, the ability of a nerve to produce a response in the innervated muscle is used to assess

the health of a cranial or peripheral nerve at risk during surgery. Recordings are typically made from needle electrodes placed directly in the innervated muscle of interest. Surface electrodes and subdermal needle electrodes may completely miss neurotonic discharges that indicate damage to a nerve.<sup>100</sup> The most experience with this monitoring modality has been obtained during its use in facial nerve monitoring.<sup>101</sup>

### Awake Craniotomies

Perhaps the most comprehensive approach to localize and preserve critical brain structures and thus allow a good balance between aggressive resection and neurologic morbidity is to perform supratentorial craniotomies in an awake patient. During “awake” craniotomies, the patient undergoes repeated neurologic examinations targeted to assess

the eloquent area at risk. Such procedures are typically divided into exposure, mapping, and resection phases, and can be done with the patient entirely awake or awake only during periods when the neurologic examination must be assessed.<sup>107</sup> Common to all these approaches is the need for meticulous locoregional anesthesia of the scalp at the craniotomy site and the pin sites of the head holder. A second requirement is a patient who is well informed about the awake parts of the procedure and is willing and able to cooperate. Dexmedetomidine, propofol, and remifentanil are the agents most frequently incorporated into the anesthetic regimens for awake craniotomy.<sup>108</sup> Complications of awake craniotomy include nausea, vomiting, respiratory problems, and “tight” brain, but are typically mild and occur in less than 10% of cases in experienced centers.

Mapping is done by systematic electrical stimulation of the cortical surface to localize cortical areas that subserve specific neurologic functions such as movement or elements of speech. The plan for mapping is frequently aided by functional MRI images that localize eloquent cortex based on the increase in blood flow required by functional tasks executed during scanning. Cortical stimulation either triggers motor output or disrupts task performance. Cortical stimulation is combined with electrocorticography, i.e., the recording of EEG from an electrode array placed on an adjacent area of exposed cortex, to look for afterdischarges that may progress to seizure activity (Fig. 35.16). Seizures triggered by cortical stimulation can be stopped by the application of iced saline to the exposed cortex or a small amount of barbiturate or propofol.

### INTRACRANIAL NEUROSURGERY – POSTERIOR FOSSA

The posterior fossa is a narrow space around the brainstem that contains the cerebellum, ascending and descending sensorimotor pathways, cranial nerve nuclei, cardiorespiratory centers, reticular activating system, and the neural networks that underlie crucial protective reflexes such as eyeblink, swallowing, gag, and cough. Surgery within the posterior fossa is therefore considered highly risky, and even small injuries can leave significant neurologic deficits. Although some of these neural structures, such as the sensory, voluntary motor, or auditory pathway, can be monitored consistently, many elements within the posterior fossa cannot be directly monitored during surgery. Instead, the integrity of these other neural structures is frequently only inferred from the well- being of neighboring structures amenable to monitoring.

### Microvascular Decompression of Cranial Nerves V, VII, and IX

Microvascular decompression is done most frequently for trigeminal neuralgia (cranial nerve V) in patients who present acceptable medical risks for a posterior fossa craniotomy. More rarely, the same approach is used to treat hemifacial spasm or neurovascular compromise of lower cranial nerves. The surgery entails dissecting along the intracranial portion of the nerve, identifying offending blood vessels that encroach on the nerve, and placing an insulating Teflon pad between vessel and nerve. The surgery risks ischemic damage to perforating vessels arising from the offending arteries and cerebellar retraction–related damage

to cranial nerves. The facial and vestibulocochlear nerves are at particular risk for stretch- induced injury caused by medial retraction of the cerebellum. Retraction- induced stretch produces a prolongation of the interpeak latency between peaks I and V of the BAEP waveform, which may ultimately lead to a complete loss of all waves beyond wave I (Fig. 35.18). Failure to release retraction in a timely manner results in postoperative hearing loss. BAEP monitoring therefore increases the chances for preserved hearing after microvascular decompression.<sup>109–113</sup> During microvascular decompression of the facial nerve for hemifacial spasm, recent developments using stimulated EMG monitoring have enabled better documentation of the adequacy of nerve decompression and lessened the likelihood of persistence/recurrence of hemifacial spasm postoperatively. This new technique monitors the so- called lateral spread response (LSR) of the facial nerve. First, a peripheral branch of the facial nerve is stimulated. In the normal patient, this stimulation would not result in a recordable EMG response in a muscle innervated by a different branch of the facial nerve. In patients with hemifacial spasm, stimulated EMG responses can be recorded in muscles innervated by a different branch of the facial nerve (an LSR) indicating abnormal crossover of electrical activity. Decompression of the facial nerve has been shown in multiple studies to result in a great decrease or elimination of this LSR, and studies have indicated that elimination of the LSR is highly predictive of immediate postoperative relief of hemifacial spasm.<sup>114</sup>

### SPINE SURGERY

The risk of injury to neural structures varies widely between different types of spine surgery. Neurophysiologic techniques can provide early warning of such injury, but their full potential is only realized in procedures characterized by modifiable or reversible steps.<sup>134</sup> For example, a laminar hook used as part of spinal instrumentation will narrow the spinal canal at the level it is placed. If the placement of the hook compromises local spinal cord perfusion resulting in a change in SSEPs or tcMEPs, the surgeon can alter the construct and prevent potential injury to the patient. Note that for some of these modifiable steps, alternatives to neurophysiologic techniques exist that may minimize the risk of neurologic injury. For example, safely positioning a patient with an unstable cervical spine in the prone position, can be done while monitoring SSEPs and tcMEPs. Alternatively, positioning the patient awake after airway anesthesia or under fluoroscopic

<!-- PDF page 23 -->

evaluation of spine alignment may similarly serve to minimize neurologic injury of this step in the procedure.

### Scoliosis, Adult Correction, and Spinal Cord Surgery

Intraoperative monitoring of SSEPs has been used most extensively in patients undergoing surgical procedures involving the spinal column, spinal cord, or both. Extensive experience has been gained in patients who have decompressive laminectomies or who have undergone corrective procedures for scoliosis. Intraoperative changes in SSEPs have been noted in 2.5% to 65% of patients undergoing surgical procedures on the spine or spinal cord.<sup>135–138</sup> When these changes are promptly reversed either spontaneously or with interventions by the surgeon or anesthesiologist (e.g., lessening the degree of spine straightening in scoliosis surgery or increasing arterial blood pressure), the patients most often have preserved neurologic function postoperatively. However, when these changes persisted without resolution intraoperatively, the patients most often awakened with worsened neurologic function.

False- negative (rare) and false- positive (common) results have been reported with SSEP monitoring during spine surgery. There have been reports of patients with intact SSEPs throughout the procedure that have awakened with a new significant neurologic deficit, but the total reported incidence of this finding is less than 1% of all cases monitored. Overall, the reliability of properly performed SSEP monitoring to predict the postoperative sensory and motor function has been reported to be excellent.<sup>41,138,139</sup> However, as previously discussed, it is important to remember that motor tracts are not directly monitored by SSEPs. The sensory and motor tracts are in different areas of the spinal cord and have distinct anatomic blood supply. The blood supply to the dorsal columns of the spinal cord, which carries all the upper extremity SSEPs and at least a portion of the lower extremity SSEPs, is derived primarily from the posterior spinal arteries. The blood supply to motor tracts is derived primarily from the anterior spinal artery. Therefore, it is possible for a significant motor deficit to develop postoperatively in patients with intact SSEPs throughout the surgery and such events have been reported.<sup>140,141</sup> The reason why local injury typically affects both sensory and motor tracts is because of collateral circulation across the surface of the spinal cord and along ventral and dorsal spinal nerve roots.

Many case series confirm that simultaneous recording of sensory and motor evoked potentials increases sensitivity for detecting intraoperative changes and reduces false negatives, i.e., patients without significant monitoring changes who nonetheless awaken with a postoperative deficit.<sup>142–147</sup> In a consensus statement, the American Society of Neurophysiologic Monitoring concluded that use of MEP monitoring in combination with SSEP monitoring is well established to prevent injury to sensory and motor tracts during spinal column surgery.<sup>148</sup>

The most frequent component of spinal instrumentation is a pedicle screw. Ideally it is placed into the vertebral body while making good contact with the cortical bone of the pedicle, thus permitting the application of significant corrective force at each instrumented level. A risk of pedicle screw placement is the breach of the pedicle resulting in a weakened construct or postoperative radicular pain.

Pedicle screw testing may help prevent malpositioned screws.<sup>149,150</sup> Typically, the pilot hole or, less desirable, the shank of an implanted pedicle screw is repeatedly stimulated with a ball- tip probe or an electrified pedicle probe with increasing current to determine the threshold for eliciting a myotomal CMAP. The interpretation of responses is complicated by the fact that the anatomic relationship between pedicles and nerve roots depends on the level of the spinal cord because the spinal cord is shorter than the bony spinal column. Thus, a medially misplaced screw in the lumbar region will come to lie next to a nerve root, whereas in the thoracic spine, a medial misplacement puts the screw next to the corticospinal tract, which cannot be activated by single stimuli. Because thresholds vary among cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine, as well as between healthy and diseased nerve roots or bone, this technique has limitations but is widely thought to be useful.<sup>151</sup>

Surgery on the spinal cord may also benefit from neurophysiologic monitoring.<sup>152,153</sup> Sensory evoked potentials may be elicited after opening the dura by stimulating the dorsal column and identifying the anatomic midline.<sup>154</sup> During resection of tumor, motor evoked potential monitoring can limit trauma to the unaffected cord even without sacrificing oncologic results. It is important to note that the improvement in neurologic outcome with the use of neuromonitoring is incremental, e.g., one grade in muscle strength on the five-grade McCormick scale.<sup>153</sup> For the patient’s quality of life, such improvements may nonetheless be clinically meaningful.

### Lumbar Spine Surgery

The bulk of lumbar spine procedures occur below the level of the spinal cord, which terminates at L1 in normal adults. Therefore, the cauda equina and nerve roots are the neural structures most at risk for injury. Dermatomal and myotomal overlap reduces the sensitivity for detecting nerve root injury through SSEPs and tcMEPs, respectively.<sup>155</sup> Sensory fibers from the posterior tibial nerve, for example, enter the spinal canal through the L5–S2 nerve roots, while the gastrocnemius muscle derives innervation from L5–S2 nerve roots. Likewise, spontaneous EMG is less sensitive to mechanical nerve irritation or injury, because, unlike cranial nerves, nerve roots are well protected by myelin. Thus, anterior and posterior approaches to the lumbar spine are frequently performed without neurophysiologic monitoring. The lateral approach to the lumbar spine is minimally invasive and may offer biomechanical advantages, explaining its increasing popularity. This approach puts the lumbar plexus in the path of the surgical approach. Therefore, many surgeons use triggered EMG to map proximity to the lumbar plexus as they establish the working corridor to the disc space.<sup>156</sup> Despite such monitoring, nerve injuries – typically of a neurapraxic type – are not uncommon. Motor evoked potential monitoring and saphenous SSEP monitoring have both shown promise to reduce such injuries and are incorporated into the monitoring approach in some centers.<sup>157,158</sup>

### Peripheral Nerve Surgery

Neurologic monitoring for surgeries involving peripheral nerves can be done in two different settings. In the first setting, the peripheral nerve is intact, but threatened by the surgery. Examples would be an intrinsic nerve tumor,

<!-- PDF page 24 -->

such as a schwannoma or an extensive soft tissue tumor, particularly if it displaces the normal anatomic course of a nerve. Monitoring of spontaneous and stimulated muscle responses from muscle groups innervated by the nerve in question can be used to guide the resection. Spontaneous EMG discharges can be generated by stretch or compression of the nerve, by local heating from electrocautery, or from ischemia. Two caveats apply to the monitoring of spontaneous EMG. First, the neuromuscular junction is part of the monitored pathway, and muscle relaxation decreases/abolishes the sensitivity of monitoring in a dose- dependent manner. Second, sharp section of the nerve may not result in a noticeable discharge. To search out the course of the nerve intraoperatively, the surgeon may stimulate the wound area with a handheld probe and listen for stimulated EMG or palpate for muscle contraction. The underlying concept should be familiar to anesthesiologists from the use of nerve stimulators in regional anesthesia.

A second setting where monitoring of peripheral nerves is used is in patients with prolonged weakness and sensory loss after nerve injury undergoing nerve exploration.<sup>159</sup> The aim is to determine whether nerve reconstruction may improve outcome. The area of the lesion is determined by preoperative nerve conduction studies. Intraoperatively, the nerve is first stimulated proximal to the lesion, and a recording of the nerve action potential is made directly from the nerve distal to the lesion, as illustrated in Fig. 35.21. If there is nerve conduction across the lesion, lysis of scar is performed, and the incision is closed. Natural recovery by means of axonal regrowth produces the best outcome. If conduction does not occur across the lesion,

resection of the damaged nerve and nerve cable grafting is performed.<sup>103,160,161</sup>

### NEUROPHYSIOLOGIC MONITORING DURING VASCULAR AND CARDIOVASCULAR SURGERY

### Carotid Vascular Surgery

Carotid vascular surgery (see also Chapter 52) typically requires transient interruption of blood flow to the brain through the affected carotid artery. Although the risk of stroke associated with carotid vascular surgery has been declining,<sup>162</sup> the residual risk varies greatly based on the indication for the procedure. It is lowest for asymptomatic patients and highest after a recent revascularization for ischemic stroke.<sup>163</sup>

The use of the EEG as a monitor of the adequacy of hemispheric blood flow during carotid endarterectomy has been established for many years. In a large series of patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy at the Mayo Clinic,<sup>35</sup> the EEG was compared with regional CBF using the Xe washout method. This study validated the EEG as an indicator of the adequacy of regional CBF. Normal CBF in gray and white matter averages 50 mL/100 g/min. With most anesthetic techniques, the EEG begins to become abnormal when CBF decreases to 20 mL/100 g/min. Cellular survival is not threatened until CBF decreases to 12 mL/100 g/min. The difference in blood flow between when the EEG becomes abnormal and the blood flow at which cellular damage begins to occur provides a rational basis for monitoring the EEG during carotid surgery. In many cases, prompt detection of EEG changes may allow intervention (e.g., shunting, increasing cerebral perfusion pressure) to restore CBF

Should the patient undergoing carotid endarterectomy have EEG monitoring? That question cannot be answered based on available data. EEG monitoring provides information about CBF that would not otherwise be available. Based on EEG changes, the clinician has an opportunity to recognize and intervene to increase inadequate blood flow when it occurs. Anecdotally, many clinicians have found such monitoring useful and use it routinely. Population studies do not support routine use, reflecting the fact that strokes during carotid vascular surgery are infrequent and the majority are caused by embolism, which may not be adequately treated by simply increasing CBF.

In a large series of patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy with selective shunting who were monitored with 16- channel unprocessed EEG, no patient awakened with a new neurologic deficit that was not predicted by EEG.<sup>164</sup> While transient, correctable EEG changes were not associated with stroke, persistent changes were associated with stroke. This study had no comparison group for analyzing stroke rates when EEG monitoring was not used during surgery. Conversely, in the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial and the European Carotid Surgery Trial, retrospective comparisons of patients receiving EEG monitoring and patients not receiving EEG monitoring failed to show a significant difference in outcome.<sup>165,166</sup>

Even more difficult to prove is that EEG monitoring is useful when all patients are shunted during carotid clamping. Such monitoring has detected correctable shunt malfunction, and investigators have described hypotension- related EEG changes in patients with critical stenoses and poor collateral circulation.<sup>167</sup> Advocates of selective shunting based on EEG (or other monitoring) criteria claim that inserting a shunt unnecessarily through a region of diseased vessel would surely increase embolization. A multicenter study of 1495 carotid endarterectomies provides some evidence that shunting of patients without evidence of decreased cerebral perfusion increases the incidence of stroke more than sixfold.<sup>168</sup> Although this study and other more recent studies<sup>169–171</sup> advocate that selective shunting using some form of monitoring of the adequacy of CBF should improve perioperative stroke rate, an analysis by the Cochrane Stroke Group<sup>172</sup> failed to show sufficient evidence to advocate for routine shunting, selective shunting, or even no shunting at all. In addition, this review failed to demonstrate that any type of monitoring for cerebral ischemia was superior to another.

Processed EEG also has been used during carotid vascular surgery. Two issues affect the efficacy and reliability of processed EEG as a monitor for cerebral ischemia. First, it is unclear what is the minimum number of channels (or areas of the brain) that should be monitored. Clinical experience and clinical investigations suggest that four channels (two per side) are the minimum number of channels for adequate sensitivity and specificity.<sup>36</sup> Interestingly, when a limited number of channels were compared with 16- channel EEG monitoring, 100% sensitivity and specificity were obtained using two channels per hemisphere, provided that those channels monitored the middle cerebral artery territory. These results were obtained with a frontoparietal channel combined with a frontotemporal channel.<sup>36</sup>

The second issue is the experience level of the observer monitoring the processed EEG. Is a dedicated, experienced technician or electroencephalographer needed? In a study addressing this question, the 16- channel unprocessed EEG monitored by a dedicated technician was compared with a processed EEG reviewed by three anesthesiologists of differing levels of experience with processed EEG.<sup>37</sup> The three anesthesiologists interpreted the tracings without knowledge of the case. They were presented only with the written trace with an indication of the point at which the carotid artery was clamped. In these cases, the most important interpretation pitfall to avoid is the “false- negative” pattern. If the clinician interprets the EEG as showing adequate CBF when in fact it does not, the surgeon may fail to shunt an ischemic patient. A false- positive result may be less of a problem because that patient is not ischemic but is given a shunt anyway. In this case, only the risk of emboli from the “unnecessary” shunt is incurred. The positive predictive value of the anesthesiologist correctly interpreting the trace as unchanged after clamping was 91% to 98%, which indicates that the device can be used by novice interpreters with fair accuracy to determine the presence of cerebral ischemia at the time of carotid occlusion (Fig. 35.22).

SSEP monitoring also has been used to gauge adequacy of CBF to the cerebral cortex and subcortical pathways during carotid vascular surgery.<sup>49,173,174</sup> SSEPs have been found in the laboratory to have a similar but slightly lower threshold for electrical failure compared with the EEG when CBF is reduced. SSEPs are generally intact until the cortical blood flow decreases to less than 15 mL/100 g/min.<sup>47</sup> A recent review (meta- analysis) of shunting during carotid vascular surgery suggests that selective shunting based on SSEP monitoring results in a perioperative stroke rate similar to when EEG monitoring is used as the basis for shunting.<sup>175</sup> Combining SSEP and EEG increases the sensitivity for detecting ischemia.<sup>176,177</sup>

TCD monitoring during carotid vascular surgery is based on measurement of two primary parameters: blood flow velocity and the number of emboli detected in the middle cerebral artery. The hypothesis justifying the use of this monitoring during carotid vascular surgery has two components: blood flow velocity correlates with CBF and increasing numbers of emboli increase the likelihood of embolic stroke. Intraoperative use of TCD has not been widely adopted for many reasons. As previously noted, good TCD signals cannot be obtained in up to 20% of individuals who could benefit from monitoring. Also, TCD probe motion during surgery causes major problems with loss of signal or with angle of insonation- induced changes in the relationship between blood flow velocity and blood flow. Nonetheless, multiple series of carotid endarterectomies with TCD monitoring have been reported with good success, quoting a critical blood flow velocity reduction around 50% as indicative of inadequate CBF requiring intervention (shunt or increased blood pressure or both).<sup>178–184</sup> The relationship between emboli count and stroke is better established. Multiple studies conducted in the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative periods indicate that higher emboli counts are associated with higher stroke risk and warrant intervention.<sup>178–184</sup> It is worth noting that TCD is the only monitor that can detect dangerous hyperemia, also known as normal perfusion pressure breakthrough, after repair of

Near- infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) is an attractive monitor during carotid endarterectomy because of its ease of application and lack of training required for interpretation. The hypothesis governing its use is very simple: as oxygen delivery to the brain decreases, oxygen extraction from arterial blood increases, and the oxygen saturation in cerebral venous blood will decrease. Multiple case reports and series document the use of cerebral oximetry during neurovascular surgery, but several major questions surrounding use of NIRS during carotid surgery remain unanswered.

The first and most important question is what degree of decrease in oxygen saturation can be tolerated before intervention is necessary. Because most interventions involve some risk (e.g., shunting leads to higher risk of emboli and increasing blood pressure may lead to myocardial ischemia), the answer to this question is important. It is likely that this tolerance may vary from patient to patient. Two studies in awake patients showed that the saturation value at which any patient would develop symptoms varied in

individual patients,<sup>14,15</sup> and an absolute value that required shunting could not be determined. Another study showed that cerebral oxygen saturation decreased before the EEG developed changes, and the authors used this observation to claim superiority for NIRS monitoring of the brain during carotid surgery.<sup>185</sup> This finding should not be surprising, however, because function of the brain (in this case, electric function) does not fail until increased extraction of oxygen no longer meets metabolic demands for the tissue. If metabolic demands are being met by increased extraction, it is unclear that any intervention is needed.

Finally, a study by Friedell and colleagues<sup>186</sup> compared NIRS with EEG and SSEP monitoring during carotid surgery. In 24 of 323 patients, significant differences were observed between NIRS and the monitors of electric function. Seventeen patients showed no changes in electric function with significant decreases in cerebral oxygen saturation. In seven patients, no change in cerebral oxygen saturation occurred despite significant change in the EEG and SSEP. The latter finding may be because NIRS and EEG/ SSEP monitor different vascular territories. These data in combination with the data from studies in awake patients suggest that use of NIRS alone during carotid vascular surgery may be inappropriate. In addition, an aggregate of studies and case reports available in the literature suggest that there is no clear cutoff value of regional oxygen saturation which would mandate shunt placement or increasing the cerebral perfusion pressure.

### Aortic Surgery

One of the feared complications of aortic surgery is spinal cord ischemia (see also Chapter 52) resulting in immediate or delayed postoperative paraplegia. Aortic disease and previous grafts reduce collateral blood supply to the spinal cord. Reduced collateral flow risks ischemia particularly in the territory of the anterior spinal artery, which supplies the bulk of the spinal cord gray matter as well as the motor tracts. Therefore, patients with neurologic dysfunction after thoracic aortic vascular surgery frequently show intact posterior spinal cord function (proprioception, vibration, light touch) when motor and other sensory functions (pain, temperature) are impaired. This result occurred in 32% of patients with neurologic injury after aortic aneurysm repair in one series,<sup>187</sup> with similar results in several other series. Intraoperative SSEP monitoring in these patients carries a significant risk for false- negative results, and as a result, such monitoring is not widely used. Conversely, the literature shows mixed but improving support for the use of MEP monitoring. Recent clinical series have shown much greater success in correctly detecting inadequate spinal cord blood flow with the use of MEPs and in improving operative outcome. The technique has proven useful, particularly when operative strategies such as reimplantation of crucial intercostal vessels based on results of MEP monitoring, alteration of spinal cord perfusion pressure (blood pressure increase or CSF drainage or both), spinal cord cooling, and other methods are used.<sup>188</sup> Additional studies, particularly during endovascular stent repair of thoracoabdominal aneurysms, have found MEPs very useful in guiding therapy and improving outcome.<sup>189,190</sup> One caveat is that failure to elicit compound muscle action potentials in the lower extremities may be due either to spinal cord ischemia or ischemia of the leg muscle itself. Placement of stimulating electrodes over a peripheral nerve of the lower extremity allows to quickly distinguish between those possibilities.<sup>191</sup>

### Cardiopulmonary Bypass

Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB; see also Chapter 50) requires cannulation of large proximal arteries. Cannulation creates a risk of embolic stroke and cannula- associated complications can jeopardize cerebral perfusion. Because of these risks, there is an interest in monitoring the brain during these cases. In humans, changes that occur with the institution of CPB may alter the EEG by multiple different mechanisms. Plasma and brain concentrations of anesthetic drugs may be altered by CPB or by anesthetic drugs commonly given during CPB. Alterations in arterial carbon dioxide tension and arterial blood pressure may occur during CPB, and hemodilution with hypothermic perfusate nearly always occurs. These effects, all of which may produce EEG changes similar to pathologic changes seen with ischemia, make it difficult to interpret EEG changes occurring during CPB. Levy and others<sup>192,193</sup> tried to distinguish the normal effects of hypothermia from other events occurring at the institution and conclusion of CPB. Initially, they concluded that only a qualitative relationship could be determined, but later, with the use of a much more sophisticated EEG analysis technique (approximate entropy), EEG changes associated with changes in temperature could be quantified.

Chabot and colleagues<sup>194</sup> and Edmonds and colleagues<sup>195</sup> have attempted to use quantitative (processed, multiplechannel) EEG during CPB to detect cerebral hypoperfusion and relate these changes to postoperative neurologic function. Some minimal work has been done with intervention after detection of cerebral hypoperfusion using quantitative EEG but only a few patients have been studied with very few corroborating studies. In addition, this type of monitoring is extremely costly in time, personnel, and equipment. Given the lack of convincing outcome data, the cost- to- benefit ratio is unclear at best.

TCD ultrasound can monitor the cerebral circulation during CPB. Anecdotal reports and case series document the use of TCD for determining adequacy of CBF, detection of emboli, and detection of improper cannula placement.<sup>196</sup> As previously mentioned, probe placement instability and inability to obtain signals in some patients limit its use. Only very limited outcome data exist, and use of TCD ultrasound during CPB does not stand up to evidence- based examination, primarily because of lack of information.

As is the case with EEG monitoring during CPB, there are multiple case reports and several series that advocate the use of NIRS or jugular venous oxygen saturation (Sjvo2) as indicators of adequate perfusion of the brain during CPB.<sup>197,198</sup> Incorrect placement of CPB cannulae have been detected clinically. One series of patients undergoing CPB for coronary bypass surgery showed a higher incidence of major organ system dysfunction and longer hospital stays in patients with lower baseline and intraoperative cerebral oxygen saturation values.<sup>199</sup> The same questions exist for this application of NIRS as for the use of NIRS during carotid vascular surgical procedures. Although a recent systematic review of the use of NIRS during CPB showed that data are insufficient to conclude that interventions based on low cerebral oxygen saturation values prevent either stroke or postoperative cognitive dysfunction,<sup>200</sup> several more recent studies suggest significant promise for this modality when used in combination with TCD ultrasound. Use of NIRS and TCD ultrasound together identified a significant percentage of patients undergoing CPB with impaired autoregulation who were at higher risk for postoperative cognitive dysfunction and/or stroke.<sup>201–203</sup> This patient population may benefit from higher mean arterial pressure during CPB. Use of NIRS during CPB clearly provides information that would not otherwise be available, but more work is needed before we fully understand the role of this monitor during CPB.

Based on current information, no neurologic monitoring techniques, either alone or in combination, are clearly useful in improving outcome during surgical procedures requiring CPB. That said, processed EEG and NIRS are both noninvasive and comparatively simple to interpret, while providing information not otherwise obtainable during CPB. They can be usefully integrated into the armamentarium of the cardiac anesthesiologist. Whether dedicated neuromonitoring personnel and further techniques add meaningfully beyond that is at present unclear.

## Nonsurgical Factors Influencing Monitoring Results

### PATHOPHYSIOLOGIC EFFECTS ON THE ELECTROENCEPHALOGRAM

### Hypoxia

Hypoxia in the cerebral cortex may lead to EEG changes that are similar to those produced by ischemia. Initially, hypoxemia may not result in any EEG changes because the brain can increase cerebral blood flow to compensate. When the hypoxemia becomes severe enough, and further increases in blood flow are impossible, EEG changes will occur. “Slowing” of the EEG during hypoxia is a nonspecific global effect. Fast frequencies are lost, and low frequencies dominate. Eventually, the EEG is abolished as the brain shuts down electric activity and diverts all oxygen delivered to maintenance of cellular integrity.

### Hypotension

In a normal, awake patient, significant levels of hypotension are needed to cause the earliest signs of CNS compromise, as measured by discrimination tests such as the flicker- fusion test. The EEG changes associated with even this level of hypotension are not dramatic, although they are clear by comparison with a previously active recording. Herein lies the problem with using intraoperative EEG to determine whether a given level of hypotension has resulted in brain ischemia. EEG changes are not very pronounced and are bilateral. These changes also are nearly identical to the changes caused by increasing doses of many anesthetic drugs. EEG changes associated with hypotension can be detected, but when the hypotension is induced slowly and associated with changes in anesthetic drugs (e.g., use of isoflurane to reduce blood pressure), the changes are very difficult to interpret. EEG changes associated with acute and severe hypotension such as those during sudden arrhythmias, are easier to read. It is also important to consider the fact that many patients undergoing surgery do not have normal cerebral circulation. In these individuals, even mild hypotension may result in significant cerebral ischemia. Therefore, monitoring the EEG during planned hypotension for those patients may be helpful, provided that other causes of similar EEG changes are carefully controlled. There remains little literature to support the use of EEG monitoring during hypotension, but in our opinion, when the EEG is being monitored (e.g., during carotid surgery), EEG changes secondary to hypotension really do represent cerebral ischemia of a significant degree and should be considered an important finding.

### Hypothermia

During cooling on CPB, the total power and peak power frequency of the high- frequency band derived from Fourier analysis and spectral edge data were highly correlated with temperature. However, significant variability was noted among subjects, especially during cooling.<sup>207</sup> Complete EEG suppression usually develops at 15°C to 18°C. Levy and colleagues<sup>193</sup> showed an improved ability to quantify the effects of hypothermia on the EEG using an EEG processing technique known as “approximate entropy.”

### Hypercarbia and Hypocarbia

Hypocapnia is known to activate excitable seizure foci and in rare cases may produce EEG evidence of cerebral ischemia even in awake subjects.<sup>208</sup> Hypercapnia, unless severe and associated with hypoxemia, has only indirect effects secondary to increased CBF. In an anesthetized patient, hypercarbia- associated increases in CBF may have similar effects to the effects seen with increasing end- tidal tension of volatile anesthetics.<sup>209</sup>

### Untoward Events

One of the suggested reasons for monitoring the brain of an anesthetized patient is to enable detection of injuries to the nervous system that would not be otherwise apparent. Although hundreds of such case reports are in the literature, as well as many in our experience, cost- effectiveness of such monitoring is unclear. In a case example at our institution,

severe EEG changes occurred at the beginning of a carotid endarterectomy, before surgical incision, that were unassociated with any other vital sign changes or hypotension. Immediate angiography revealed acute carotid occlusion, completely changed the operation performed with this patient, and facilitated a complete recovery of the patient. There are intraoperative events that could lead to a CNS insult, which, if detected early, could be rapidly reversed or treated to prevent permanent injury. Given the rarity of such events, however, it is extremely unlikely that such monitoring would be shown to be beneficial in any foreseeable randomized trial. Also, routine implementation of EEG monitoring for all patients to prevent such rare events may not be cost- effective. If the “at- risk” patient could be identified preoperatively, perhaps the EEG or other types of neuromonitoring could be useful in detecting untoward CNS events during anesthesia, such as a new stroke after elective general surgery.

### PHYSIOLOGIC FACTORS INFLUENCING SENSORY EVOKED RESPONSES

Numerous physiologic variables, including systemic arterial blood pressure, temperature (local and systemic), and blood gas tensions, can influence SEP recordings. Decreases in mean arterial blood pressure to below levels of cerebral autoregulation lead to progressive changes in SERs. SSEP changes observed in this scenario are progressive decreases in amplitude until loss of the waveform with no changes in latency.<sup>210,211</sup> BAEPs are resistant to even profound levels of hypotension (mean arterial pressure of 20 mm Hg in dogs).<sup>210</sup> Cortical SERs, which are produced as a result of multiple synaptic transmissions, seem to be more sensitive to hypoperfusion than SERs produced by the spinal cord, brainstem, or non- synaptic transmission.<sup>211</sup> Rapid decreases in arterial blood pressure to levels above the lower limit of autoregulation have also been associated with transient SSEP changes of decreased amplitude that resolve after several minutes of continued hypotension at the same level.<sup>212</sup> Reversible SSEP changes at systemic pressures within the normal range have been observed in patients undergoing spinal distraction during scoliosis surgery. These changes resolved with increases of systemic arterial blood pressure to slightly higher than the patient’s normal pressure, suggesting that the combination of surgical manipulation with levels of hypotension generally considered “safe” could result in spinal cord ischemia.<sup>213</sup>

Changes in temperature also affect SERs. Hypothermia causes increases in latency and decreases in amplitude of cortical and subcortical SERs after all types of stimulation.<sup>214–216</sup> Hyperthermia also alters SERs, with increases in temperature leading to decreases in amplitude in SSEPs and loss of SSEPs at 42°C during induced hyperthermia.<sup>217</sup>

Changes in arterial blood gas tensions can alter SERs, probably in relation to changes in blood flow or oxygen delivery to neural structures.<sup>218,219</sup> Hypoxia produces SSEP changes (decreased amplitude) similar to the changes seen with ischemia.<sup>219</sup> Decreased oxygen delivery associated with anemia during isovolemic hemodilution results in progressive increases in latency of SSEPs and VEPs that become significant at hematocrit values lower than 15%. ## Anesthetic Optimization During Multimodality Neurophysiologic Monitoring

Anesthetic agent choice, patient- specific comorbidities, and management of physiologic variables affect both the quality and interpretability of IONM signals. To plan for an optimal anesthetic approach for neurophysiologic monitoring, a good understanding of the neural pathways that are at risk during a given procedure is essential. Once atrisk pathways are determined, multiple IONM modalities are usually selected to provide some degree of redundancy for assessing those pathways throughout the procedure. The patient’s allergies and medical history are taken into consideration when selecting anesthetic agents that are compatible with those modalities. The level of anesthesia can affect the function of the nervous system and systemic physiologic variables, which in turn impact neurophysiologic monitoring signals. Therefore, it is recommended to maintain a consistent steady state of anesthesia as much as possible throughout the procedure to minimize falsepositive IONM alerts. When significant adjustments to the anesthetic agents must be made, clear communication with the IONM team is important to avoid unnecessary interruptions to the procedure. When signal changes do occur, timely open communication between all members of the perioperative surgical team allows for early detection and intervention to prevent or minimize permanent deficits to the nervous system.

From an anesthetic perspective, IONM modalities can be divided into four groups depending on whether they are significantly affected by neuromuscular blocking agents or volatile anesthetic agents. When motor evoked potentials or electromyography are utilized, neuromuscular blocking agents should be minimized or avoided altogether. Volatile anesthetics significantly impede the ability to obtain motor evoked potentials and in higher doses can degrade SSEPs. It is for this reason that when motor evoked potentials are utilized, total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) without the use of NMBA is commonly implemented. TIVA is usually composed of an opioid infusion (e.g., remifentanil or sufentanil) and a sedative infusion (most commonly propofol). These infusions are titratable and, more importantly, readily permit a steady- state anesthetic concentration throughout the procedure. Opioid infusions block noxious stimuli and are thought to provide immobility during the procedure by interrupting the motor reflex pathway to those stimuli at the level of the spinal cord. Sedative infusions such as propofol can be titrated according to EEG activity and should be periodically adjusted during the procedure to avoid over- anesthetizing the patient, which may lead to degradation in signals and false-positive alerts. Ketamine and etomidate use may improve the quality of evoked potentials and are often used as adjuncts to propofol infusions. Dexmedetomidine use has been described in addition to propofol infusions without significantly affecting SSEPs, but its effects on motor evoked potentials

have been mixed. Once a steady state is reached, avoiding sudden large boluses of anesthetics as much as possible is recommended.

Although volatile anesthetics can be used with many modalities, it is important to note that the amount of volatile anesthetic that can be tolerated to produce adequate evoked potentials varies from patient to patient. The amount of volatile anesthetic that can be used is affected by several variables such as extremes of age, other medications, and pathologic conditions that affect the nervous system. For instance, in very young children with incomplete myelination, it can be more challenging to generate adequate somatosensory evoked potentials without special adjustments to stimulating parameters. In patients with peripheral neuropathies, such as those which result from diabetes, higher stimulation parameters may be needed to generate motor evoked potentials. In addition, the monitorability of evoked potentials with volatile anesthetics degrades in a nonlinear fashion over a narrow dose range. Therefore, the admonition that 0.5 MAC of a volatile anesthetic agent should be compatible with neurophysiologic monitoring is too simplistic and may not be true for a given patient.

EEG monitoring during IONM is useful for maintaining unconsciousness and titrating hypnotic agents such as propofol while avoiding excessive accumulation in lipidrich tissues. Accumulation of anesthetic agents during prolonged infusions will lead to a progressive degradation in evoked potentials over time.<sup>221</sup> Clinicians can utilize EEG monitoring to gradually reduce the rate of infusions during the procedure to avoid excessively deep anesthesia.

Whether NMBAs can be used during the procedure should be determined prior to induction because it may have implications for the medications used during intubation. For example, if a procedure will not utilize motor evoked potentials or electromyography, NMBAs may be used during the case and other anesthetics can be titrated to optimize for EEG monitoring and generating somatosensory potentials. When NMBAs will not be utilized, it is common to utilize opioid infusions to help ensure immobility.

Optimization of anesthesia and physiologic homeostasis is most important when the plan is to use neuromonitoring for surgical decision- making. For instance, when the anatomy of the brain is distorted due to a tumor, the surgeon may utilize brain mapping to identify the borders of the motor cortex and the underlying corticospinal tract. Anesthetic optimization is important for correctly identifying critical motor pathway structures before tumor resection and continuously assessing their integrity throughout the operation. Anesthetic optimization will minimize falsepositive IONM changes and thus potentially reduce incomplete resections. Another example is the correction phase of a spinal deformity. This correction, particularly in severe deformities, typically occurs after spinal osteotomies, which may cause significant blood loss. This blood loss may simultaneously cause hypotension and increase the concentration of anesthetic by decreasing spinal cord perfusion and reducing the volume of distribution of an anesthetic infusion, respectively. In this setting careful management of both anesthesia administration and hemodynamics may affect the degree of correction achievable and thus the functional outcome of the surgery.

### TROUBLESHOOTING IONM ALERTS

For many monitoring modalities, thresholds of “tolerable” changes have been described beyond which the operative team must be alerted. Examples include a 0.5 ms increase in latency of wave V of a BAER,<sup>222</sup> a 50% reduction in amplitude of a cortical SSEP waveform,<sup>51</sup> or an 80% reduction in the CMAP response to tcMEP stimulation.<sup>223</sup> Clearly there are clinical scenarios where waveform changes cross such thresholds quickly and prompt corrective action is required. Examples may be the placement of a cerebellar retractor followed by a latency increase in the BAER to ipsilateral stimulation or placement of a laminar hook followed by a loss of cortical SSEP responses to posterior tibial nerve stimulation. On the other hand, simply assessing changes in signals in comparison to a baseline based on rigid definition of thresholds for “IONM alerts” risks both creating unnecessary alerts as well as missing subtle changes that are clinically significant. Cortical SSEP amplitudes, for example, may diminish over the course of a long spine surgery by 50% from a baseline acquired after induction, but before incision, without raising concern for a new neurologic deficit.<sup>51</sup> Likewise, the threshold for tcMEPs may gradually increase during a surgical procedure, a phenomenon so prevalent that it is referred to as “anesthetic fade”.<sup>224</sup> A 0.5-ms increase in latency of wave V of a BAER has different implications for the welfare of a healthy auditory nerve during a microvascular decompression compared to an auditory nerve displaced by a vestibular schwannoma.<sup>225</sup> In order to best capture such clinical nuances and optimize the sensitivity of IONM, it is important to strive for pharmacologic and physiologic stability during phases of the operation when neural structures are at risk. In this case any change in IONM signals that exceeds baseline variability of responses recorded prior to this phase can serve as a starting point to investigate potential causes.

When changes in IONM signals occur during a procedure, a systematic approach to investigate the cause is needed. Early detection and identification of the etiology will lead to timely intervention, which may minimize or prevent permanent deficits in the patient. For this to happen, it is essential that open communication occurs between all members of the surgical, anesthesia, and IONM teams.<sup>226</sup> Most often, the investigation and intervention occur simultaneously, with each member of the team performing their respective roles. Changes in IONM signals may not always represent a true neurologic insult to the patient, but it is important to respond with vigilance until the cause is determined.

Causes of IONM changes can be divided into five categories: surgical insult, anesthetic effect, positioning injury, physiologic disturbance, and technical problem. Since there are many factors that must be investigated, having a predetermined plan may improve efficiency and decrease the likelihood of omitting important interventions. Positioning injuries during procedures are another potential source of IONM signal changes. Positioning injuries can be due to excessive stretch or compression on neural structures. Changes in signals due to positioning problems tend to be limited to a particular region of the body. Although signal changes due to technical problems related to IONM equipment are most often addressed by the neuromonitoring team, anesthesiologists should be aware of the location of electrodes and the interference that other electrical equipment can cause. Any electrical equipment that is placed near recording electrodes or recording amplifiers can introduce electrical noise, which will degrade IONM signals. A fluid warmer placed near the patient’s electrodes is a common offending piece of equipment. Lifting other electrical cables away from the electrodes or amplifiers or moving or rotating an offending piece of equipment further away may help minimize electrical noise. Excessive noise can also be a sign that some of the electrodes are partially or completely dislodged.

## Summary

Neurophysiologic monitoring utilizes several modalities, with the goal of assessing the metabolic and functional

integrity of the various components of the nervous system. When monitoring detects changes, the aim is to lead the perioperative team to early intervention and prevent or minimize the loss of neurologic function in the patient. Regardless of the type of intraoperative neurologic monitor, several principles must be observed for neurologic monitoring to provide potential benefit to the patient. First, the pathway at risk during the surgical procedure must be amenable to monitoring. Second, if evidence of injury to the pathway is detected, some intervention must be possible. If changes in the neurologic monitor are detected, and no intervention is possible, although the monitor may be of prognostic value, it does not have the potential to provide direct benefit to the patient from early detection of impending neurologic injury. Third, the monitor must provide reliable and reproducible data. If the data have a high degree of variability in the absence of clinical interventions, their utility for detecting clinically significant events is limited.

Ideally, clinical studies would provide outcome data on the efficacy of a neurologic monitor in a given procedure to improve neurologic outcome. Although there is a wealth of clinical experience with many of these monitoring modalities, there is little in the way of randomized prospective studies evaluating the efficacy of neurologic monitoring. Based on clinical experience with neurologic monitoring and nonrandomized clinical studies in which neurologic monitoring is used and generally compared with historical controls, practice patterns for use of neurologic monitoring have developed. In certain procedures, neurologic monitoring is recommended and used by most centers; in other procedures, monitoring is used almost routinely in some centers, but not in others; and in some procedures, no clear clinical experience or evidence indicates that monitoring is useful at all (experimental use). Finally, there are procedures in which monitoring is used selectively for patients believed to be at higher- than- usual risk for intraoperative neurologic injury.
