# Perioperative Acid–Base Balance

<!-- PDF page 1 -->


PATRICK J. NELIGAN

- **KEY POINTS** The presence of a significant acid–base abnormality often signals a sinister underlying problem. ᑏ All acid–base abnormalities result from alterations in the dissociation of water. ᑏ Only three factors independently affect acid–base balance: the PaCO2, the strong ion difference (SID), and the total concentration of weak acids (ATOT).

- Respiratory acidosis and alkalosis are caused by hypercarbia and hypocarbia, respectively. Metabolic acidosis is caused by decreased SID or increased ATOT. Decreased SID results from accumulation of metabolic anions (shock, ketoacidosis, and renal failure), hyperchloremia, and free water excess. Increased ATOT results from hyperphosphatemia.

- Metabolic alkalosis is caused by increased SID or decreased ATOT. SID increases due to sodium gain, chloride loss, or free water deficit. ATOT decreases in hypoalbuminemia and hypophosphatemia. This is particularly common in critical illness.

- Most acid–base disorders are treated by reversal of the cause.

## Introduction – Why Is Acid–Base Balance Important?

Alterations in the hydrogen ion (H<sup>+</sup> ) activity, pH, and bicarbonate concentration [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] in blood have been robust biomarkers of acute illness for more than a century. The observation of these data is known as acid–base balance. Today point- of- care arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis provides myriad information that can be used to diagnose, treat, and prognosticate physiologic disruption. There is good reason to believe that understanding of “acid–base” will continue to be important for physicians for decades to come.<sup>1</sup>

The human body is composed principally of water, partitioned into intracellular and extracellular compartments. The electrolyte composition of each of these spaces is tightly controlled to maintain homeostasis. Alterations in the relative concentrations of electrolytes and of carbon dioxide (CO2) impact the tendency of water to ionize into its component parts: H<sup>+</sup> and hydroxyl ions (OH<sup>−</sup> ).<sup>2</sup> This manifests as changes in acid–base balance.

The H<sup>+</sup> activity is conventionally measured as pH (often interpreted as the “power of hydrogen”), the negative logarithmic value (base 10) of its concentration, or more accurately, its activity. There are vanishingly few “free” H<sup>+</sup> in the human body: 1 liter of extracellular fluid contains 55 moles of water but only 40 nmol (40 × 1 billionth of a mole) of active H<sup>+</sup> , and far fewer free protons. Extracellular pH is maintained in and around 7.4; intracellular pH is maintained at 6.8, approximately the pH of H2O at 37°C. Hence, the extracellular space is normally alkaline with respect to both water and the intracellular space. When clinicians

discuss “acid–base balance” they are exclusively talking about extracellular pH and [H<sup>+</sup> ] activity, as measured from sampling arterial blood. Deviations in arterial pH away from the resting value of 7.4 have long been associated with acute and critical illness. Such deviations are known as “acid–base” abnormalities.”<sup>3</sup> All acid–base abnormalities result from changes in the local concentration of strong ions, weak acids, and CO2.<sup>2,4,5</sup>

This chapter first looks at the basic science behind acid– base abnormalities. Subsequently we will explore the detection and treatment of acid–base conundrums, with specific reference to perioperative medicine and critical care.

## What Are Acids and Bases?

The concept of acids and bases is relatively new in medicine,<sup>6</sup> and arose with the development of laboratory science in the early part of the 20th century. However, as early as 1831 O’Shaughnessy had identified loss of “carbonate of soda” from the blood as a fundamental disturbance in patients dying of cholera.<sup>7</sup> This led to the early development of crystalloid replacement therapy for hypovolemic shock. In the late 19th century Arrhenius, having previously described ionization, defined acids as moieties that deliver H<sup>+</sup> into aqueous solutions, and bases as moieties that deliver OH<sup>−</sup> . The acidity or alkalinity of the solution was determined by the relative ratio of H<sup>+</sup> to OH<sup>−</sup> . In a neutral solution, such as water, they are equal. The pH scale was introduced in Copenhagen by Sorenson in 1909, to simplify the expression of [H<sup>+</sup> ] into a negative logarithmic scale. Around the same time, L.J. Henderson coined the term “acid–base” balance.<sup>8</sup> At the time measurement of pH and

It only became possible to introduce this concept into clinical practice with the development of volumetric CO2 analysis by Van Slyke and others in 1919.<sup>11</sup> Actual bedside measurement of [H<sup>+</sup> ] and pH was limited during the next 30 years and did not become common clinical practice until well into the 1950s. Consequently, Van Slyke, Henderson, Gamble, Singer, Hastings, and others developed a traditional model of acid– base chemistry based on CO2 equilibrium and electrolyte balance: anions were acids, cations were bases.<sup>12</sup> In the 1950s work by Astrup and colleagues led to the focus on pH and the H<sup>+</sup> rather than electrochemistry, characterized by hydrogen ion “counting.” From that time on, the dominant approach to acid–base has focused on the Henderson–Hasselbalch equilibrium and the H<sup>+</sup> activity/pH. In the 1980s, once accurate measurement of electrolytes and blood gases was universally available, Peter Stewart, a Canadian physiologist, reclaimed the electrophysiologic approach, based on a series of calculations derived from physical chemistry. Due to the Stewart approach’s superior ability to diagnose multiple simultaneous acid–base anomalies, it has gained considerable attention in the perioperative and critical care community and is the major focus of this chapter. It is important to state that all the differing approaches to acid– base balance are fully compatible with one another and, regardless of viewpoint, the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation remains the cornerstone of how clinicians interpret acid–base anomalies.

### THE AUTO- IONIZATION OF WATER

An understanding of acid–base chemistry in the human body requires familiarity with physical chemistry. Because the body contains a large quantity of water, the physical properties of water have enormous implications for maintenance of homeostasis. Water is a simple triatomic molecule. Its chemical formula is H2O, and its structural formula is H- O- H. The charge distribution of each covalent bond is unequal and therefore the molecule has a polar conformation and an H- O- H bond angle of 105 degrees. Water molecules attract and form hydrogen bonds with one another. Consequently, water has a high surface tension, a low

vapor pressure, a high specific heat capacity, a high heat of vaporization, and a high boiling point.

Water molecules are in continual motion. Occasionally a collision generates sufficient energy to transfer a proton from one water molecule to another. Thus, water is always slightly dissociated into a negatively charged OH<sup>−</sup> and a positively charged hydronium ion (H3O<sup>+</sup> ). Conventionally, this self- ionization of water is written as follows:


![](assets/chapter-44/44._Perioperative_Acid-Base_Balance.pdf-0002-13.png)


H2O  H   OH <br>

The symbol H<sup>+</sup> is convenient because, while protons dissociating from water have many aliases (such as H3O<sup>+</sup> and H9O4<sup>+</sup> ), most physician and chemists refer to them as hydrogen ions. This has led to the erroneous belief that [H<sup>+</sup> ] represents proton (i.e., individual hydrogen atoms) concentration. Single protons are extremely rare; rather the reader should consider [H<sup>+</sup> ] to be the H<sup>+</sup> activity of large chains of water molecules that express a positive charge.

The self- ionization of water is miniscule. In pure water at 25°C, the [H<sup>+</sup> ] and [OH<sup>−</sup> ] are 1.0 × 10<sup>−7</sup> mmol/L. The tendency for water to dissociate into its component parts is represented by the expression


![](assets/chapter-44/44._Perioperative_Acid-Base_Balance.pdf-0002-16.png)


Keq H2O = [H + ] [OH – ]<br>

The molarity of water is extremely high: 55.5M (“there is a lot of water in water”). As the concentration of water and the Keq are constants, the ion- product dissociation constant for water can be expressed as


![](assets/chapter-44/44._Perioperative_Acid-Base_Balance.pdf-0002-18.png)


Keq H2O = Keq (55.5) = K ' w = [H + ] [OH – ]<br>

The implication is that the product of [H<sup>+</sup> ] and [OH<sup>−</sup> ] is constant. Pure water is considered neutral, because [H<sup>+</sup> ] and [OH<sup>−</sup> ] are equal at 1.0 × 10<sup>−7</sup> mmol/L. The degree of dissociation may be determined by temperature. For example, as temperature falls, water dissociates less: at 0°C the pH is 7.47. As temperature rises, water dissociation increases: at 100°C the pH is 6.13. Importantly, the pOH, the [OH<sup>−</sup> ] is also 7.47 and 6.13 at these temperatures. The solution is neutral. Thus, pH in and of itself does not identify acidity or alkalinity.

A solution is considered acidic if the [H<sup>+</sup> ] exceeds that of [OH<sup>−</sup> ] ([H<sup>+</sup> ] >1.0 × 10<sup>−7</sup> mmol/L, [OH<sup>−</sup> ] &lt;1.0 × 10<sup>−7</sup> mmol/L). A solution is considered alkaline if the [OH<sup>−</sup> ] exceeds the [H<sup>+</sup> ]. Importantly, mammals maintain their body temperature within very strict limits; for humans that is around 37°C. Hence, if body temperature is within the physiologic range, a fall in the pH of blood represents acidosis and a rise in alkalosis.

### ACID–BASE DEFINITIONS

In the 1890s and 1900s, Svante Arrhenius (1859–1927) established the foundations of ionization theory and acid– base chemistry. In an aqueous solution, an Arrhenius acid is any substance that delivers H<sup>+</sup> into the solution.<sup>5</sup> A base is any substance that delivers OH<sup>−</sup> into the solution. Because of its high dielectric constant, water is a highly ionizing solution. Therefore substances with polar bonds will dissociate into their component parts in water. While this theory was ideal for biological systems, for general chemistry it was limited: not all reactions occur in water. Brønsted and Lowry independently advanced this concept using slightly

<!-- PDF page 3 -->

different terminology: an acid is a substance that releases H<sup>+</sup> and a base is a substance that receives H<sup>+</sup> . (Simplified, an acid is a proton donor; a base a proton acceptor.) For each acid in this definition, there is a conjugate base. Water itself is amphoteric: it acts either as an acid or as a base. Thus, when hydrochloric acid (HCl) is dissolved in water, chloride (the acid) donates a proton to water (the base). Similarly, when potassium hydroxide (KOH) is dissolved in water, potassium, the base, receives H<sup>+</sup> from water, the acid or proton donor. Some confusion often arises in medicine when these definitions are applied because biological buffers are weak acids, but when they buffer stronger acids, they are Brønsted and Lowry bases. Hence, depending on whether one uses the Arrhenius or the Brønsted and Lowry definitions, HCO3<sup>−</sup> may be considered a “weak acid buffer” (it is incompletely dissociated – see below) or the conjugate base of carbonic acid.

The degree of dissociation of substances in water determines whether they are strong acids or strong bases. The p _K_ a is the dissociation constant for an ionizable compound: it represents the pH at which the moiety is 50% dissociated. Lactic acid has a p _K_ a of 3.8. That means that, at pH 3.8, lactic acid is 50% dissociated; the compound becomes less dissociated at lower pH and more dissociated at higher pH. Within the physiologic range (pH 7.7 to 8.0), lactic acid is always completely dissociated. Hence there is no lactic acid in the body, only lactate, and it is considered a physiologically strong acid; it is not a chemically strong acid. Conversely, carbonic acid, which has a p _K_ a of 6.4, is incompletely dissociated at all physiologic pH levels, and is a weak acid.

Rather than using the confusing terms “strong acids” and “strong bases,” the term “strong ion” is frequently used to describe ions that are usually found in dissociated state at physiologic pH. These “strong ions” include sodium, potassium, and chloride. They do not easily bind other molecules and exist free in solution. Traditionally, using the Arrhenius and electrochemical approach, each sodium ion delivers a hydroxyl moiety into extracellular fluid, it is functionally a base, as are all cations. As each chloride ion delivers a hydrogen moiety into extracellular fluid, it is functionally an acid, as are all anions. H<sup>+</sup> and OH<sup>−</sup> delivered in this way bind to one another, forming water molecules, and relatively few free H<sup>+</sup> or OH<sup>−</sup> remain free in solution.

HCl + H2O → H3O<sup>+</sup> + Cl<sup>–</sup>

In this reaction, hydrogen chloride acts as a Brønsted and Lowry acid and water as a Brønsted and Lowry base.

In summary, all acid–base reactions in the human body relate to the presence of charged particles within an aqueous environment. In the section below, I discuss how the components of extracellular fluid influence the acid–base status of the body, as measured by clinicians. I subsequently explain different acid–base abnormalities, and tools that are

used to identify them. These approaches are neither distinct from one another, nor scientifically incompatible.

## What Determines the Acidity or Alkalinity of a Solution?

All acid–base reactions are based on the principles of physical chemistry and must follow three simple rules<sup>2</sup> :

1. _Electrical neutrality_ : in aqueous solutions, in any compartment, the sum of all positive charged ions must equal the sum of all negative charged ions.

2. _Dissociation equilibria_ : the dissociation equilibria of all incompletely dissociated substances, as derived from the law of mass action, must be always satisfied.

3. _Mass conservation_ : the amount of a substance in each compartment remains constant unless it is added, removed, generated, or destroyed. Therefore the total concentration of an incompletely dissociated substance is the sum of concentrations of its dissociated and nondissociated forms.

Thus, to determine the acid–base status of a fluid, all substances to which these rules might be applied must be accounted for. Fundamentally this involves counting up all of the charges (and hence H<sup>+</sup> and OH<sup>−</sup> ), delivered by strong cations (representing bases) and strong anions (representing acids), weak acid buffers, and CO2.<sup>13</sup> A discussion of key groups follows.

### STRONG IONS

The first group of ions, the strong ions, dissociate completely. The most abundant strong ions in the extracellular space are Na<sup>+</sup> and Cl<sup>−</sup> . Other important strong ions include K<sup>+</sup> , SO4<sup>2−</sup> , Mg<sup>2+</sup> , and Ca<sup>2+</sup> . These are occasionally referred to as “mineral” acids or bases, as they cannot be metabolized.<sup>6</sup> Organic acids are generated from metabolism, and accumulate when there is metabolic dysfunction – such as kidney failure, splanchnic hypoperfusion, or hormonal deficiency. Strong organic anions include lactate, beta- hydroxy butyrate (βOHB), acetoacetate (ketones), components of the “citric acid” cycle (e.g., oxaloacetate), and by-products of protein metabolism (renal acids).

## Acid–Base Abnormalities

The terms “acidosis” and “alkalosis” are physiologically difficult to define. From a laboratory perspective, an acidosis may represent a pH value of, for example, pH &lt;7.3 and an alkalosis a pH value of >7.5. However, in physiology, changes in respiration and buffer activity may result in normal range pH, despite concurrent acidifying and alkalinizing processes. Consequently, we are left with a series of terms that were agreed by consensus in 1965, and have remained in place since.<sup>15</sup> In that text “acidosis” was described as “an abnormal condition that tends to produce a downward deviation of blood pH or that would produce a (significant) downward deviation of pH if there were no secondary changes.” Using these conventions, acid–base disturbances have been classified as being either due to alterations in PaCO2 (respiratory acidosis or alkalosis), or alterations in blood chemistry (metabolic acidosis or alkalosis).<sup>16</sup> The disorders may be primary or secondary or mixed. The term “compensated” refers to whether the pH has been partially (“partially compensated”) or fully (“fully compensated”) restored to normal range; if the pH is entirely correct for the respiratory or metabolic condition, then it is “uncompensated.”

In general, primary respiratory abnormalities were described by increases or decreases in PaCO2, with concomitant alterations in HCO3<sup>−</sup> . Conversely, metabolic abnormalities were viewed as alterations in HCO3<sup>−</sup> with concomitant changes in PaCO2. For example, a primary respiratory acidosis was associated with a rise in the PaCO2 and minimal change in the HCO3<sup>−</sup> or surrogate (e.g., base excess). A primary metabolic acidosis was associated with a fall in the HCO3<sup>−</sup> with a compensatory fall in the PaCO2.

### RESPIRATORY ACID–BASE ABNORMALITIES

### Respiratory Alkalosis

The normal PaCO2 is 40 mm Hg (5.3 kPa). Respiratory alkalosis occurs when there is an acute decrease in PaCO2,

due to hyperventilation. Acute respiratory alkalosis is characterized by a pH >7.45, a low PaCO2, and a low HCO3<sup>−</sup> . A simple rule of thumb for this reaction is:


![](assets/chapter-44/44._Perioperative_Acid-Base_Balance.pdf-0005-14.png)


 [HCO3  ]  0.2  PaCO2<br>

For example: if the PaCO2 falls to 30 mm Hg, the ΔHCO3<sup>−</sup> should be: 0.2 × (40 − 30) = 0.2 × 10 = 2 mEq/L. Therefore, the estimated [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] would be 24 − 2 = 22 mEq/L.

The hyperventilating patient will present with symptoms and signs of vasoconstriction: light- headedness, visual disturbances, dizziness, and hypocalcemia, due to increased binding of calcium to albumin. The latter is caused by an increase in the available negative charge on albumin in alkaline states. Acute hypocalcemia is associated with paresthesia and tetany. In anesthesia practice patients may hyperventilate preoperatively consequent of anxiety, and postoperatively secondary to pain, agitation, or a full bladder. More commonly, hyperventilation results from poor mechanical ventilation strategy and may result in significant systemic and, in particular, cerebral vasoconstriction.

### Respiratory Acidosis

Respiratory acidosis occurs when there is an acute rise in PaCO2 usually associated with respiratory failure. This may result from problems with:

- Central ventilator control such as toxicity from anesthetic agents, benzodiazepines or opioids, stroke, spinal cord injury

- Peripheral ventilator control such as myasthenia gravis, poliomyelitis, polymyopathy, or neuromuscular blockade

- Ventilation perfusion mismatch associated with pneumothorax, pleural effusion, atelectasis, pneumonia, or pulmonary edema

Clinically, there are signs of CO2 retention: cyanosis, vasodilatation, and narcosis.

Respiratory acidosis causes a rapid increase in [H<sup>+</sup> ]. Although the acidosis is buffered by intracellular proteins, particularly hemoglobin, these are rapidly overwhelmed. Metabolic compensation for hypercarbia is slow, requiring increased urinary excretion of chloride,<sup>8</sup> and pH falls rapidly. There is a concomitant increase in the plasma [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ], reflecting higher total CO2 load. Brackett, Cohen, and Schwartz in the mid- 1960s elegantly described the relative changes in [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] in response to acute and chronic elevations in PaCO2, providing us with extremely useful “rules of thumb.”<sup>17</sup>

In acute hypercarbia, there is an increase in [H<sup>+</sup> ] and plasma [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] rises slowly (Table 44.1):

- An increase in PaCO2 by 10 mm Hg (1.3 kPa) results in an increase in [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] by 1 mEq/L

- Δ[H<sup>+</sup> ] (nEq/L) = 0.8 (ΔPaCO2)

- 1 nEq/L increase in [H<sup>+</sup> ] results in a fall in pH by 0.001

A patient returning to the intensive care unit (ICU) from the operating room who has been underventilated for several hours may be hypercapnic – for example, with a PaCO2 of 80 mm Hg (10.5 kPa). Using this rule the expected [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] will be 28 mEq/L (see Table 44.1). Also, the [H<sup>+</sup> ] to 72 nEq/L and pH will be reduced to 7.08 (assuming the starting pH was 7.4).

### METABOLIC ACID–BASE DISTURBANCES

Metabolic acid–base disturbances are caused by abnormalities of extracellular water, electrolyte composition, and serum protein levels. Using the terminology described above, metabolic acid–base abnormalities are caused by alterations in the SID or ATOT, or both (Table 44.2). An increase in the SID causes alkalemia; a decrease in the SID causes acidemia. If this results in a significant change in pH, the disorder manifests as acidosis or alkalosis. The alternation may be caused by a change in the total or relative concentration of strong ions. For example, a decrease in the SID (i.e., more anions relative to cations) causes acidosis. This may occur because of a net increase in anions: mineral acids such as chloride or organic acids lactate or ketones (remember, organic acids can be metabolized). In addition, SID can fall due to an increase in the volume of distribution of the same quantity of ions (see Table 44.2). Traditionally, metabolic acid–base disorders have been characterized by changes in HCO3<sup>−</sup> . A useful “rule of thumb” is that for every 1 mEq/L fall in the SID, there is a 1 mEq/L fall in the [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] from baseline (although the “normal” level varies with respiratory function – see Table 44.1) and this approach is widely used to characterize metabolic acidosis. Likewise, for every 1 mEq/L increase in the SID, there is a 1 mEq/L increase in the [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] from baseline – and this approach is widely used to characterize metabolic alkalosis. Note that, in all scenarios, [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] is a _dependent_ variable; dependent on changes in SID or ATOT.

Metabolic acidosis is of clinical significance for two reasons: pathologies arising from the acidosis itself and pathologies arising from the cause of the acidosis. Acidosis is associated with alterations in transcellular ion pumps and increased ionized calcium. The result is vasodilation, diminished myocardial performance, and arrhythmias. The oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve shifts rightward to increase oxygen offload into the tissues. Rapid- onset metabolic acidosis may be associated with profound hypotension, cardiac arrhythmias, and death. The malignancy of the acidosis is strongly related to the underlying disease process; lactic acidosis caused by circulatory shock is more malevolent than hyperchloremic acidosis.<sup>13</sup> The body is hyperresponsive to acidosis. Increasing hydrogen ion activity in cerebrospinal fluid activates the respiratory center to stimulate respiration. Alveolar ventilation increases, reducing arterial CO2 content, hence reducing the total body [H<sup>+</sup> ]. Metabolic alkalosis rarely occurs because of acute illness. Symptoms and signs of metabolic alkalosis include widespread vasoconstriction, lightheadedness, tetany, and paresthesia. The main compensatory mechanism is hypoventilation.

In normal extracellular fluid, the SID is 40–44 mEq/L, this positive charge being balanced principally by weak acids (without which, the pH of blood would be 11.9). Anything that increases the SID will increase the relative concentration of cations to anions and alkalinize the solution. Anything that decreases the SID will decrease the relative concentration of cations to anions and will acidify the solution. Hence, if the extracellular compartment volume is expanded with free water (no electrolytes), the components of the system are diluted, with relatively more dilution of more abundant moieties (sodium rather than chloride); the result is a reduction in SID and “dilutional acidosis.”<sup>21</sup> This is rarely seen in clinical practice, due to the renal water clearance. Conversely, if free water is removed from the extracellular fluid, for example from increased evaporative losses, there is an increase in the relative concentration of charged moieties, and this concentration effects more abundant ions and compounds (sodium rather than chloride). The SID increases and the patient develops a “contraction alkalosis” (see Table 44.2).<sup>22</sup> This is commonly seen in clinical practice, in particular following the administration of loop diuretics that have a greater impact on water removal over salt.

A liter of “normal saline solution” (NaCl 0.9%) contains 154 mEq (3.5 g) of sodium and 154 mEq (5.5 g) of chloride and is commonly used for fluid resuscitation. The SID of this solution is 0. As each liter of fluid delivers relatively more chloride than sodium (as the baseline proportions in extracellular fluid are 1.4:1 – SID 35 to 40), progressive hyperchloremia results. This reduces SID and results in “hyperchloremic acidosis.”<sup>23</sup> Any process that removes chloride without sodium, such as vomiting or aggressive nasogastric suctioning with a loss of HCl, causes metabolic alkalosis (hypochloremic alkalosis) due to an increase in SID. The alkalosis is caused by chloride loss, which obeys the law of conservation of mass (i.e., there is a finite quantity available in the extracellular fluid), not hydrogen ions, whose source (water) is unlimited. Severe diarrhea, which is associated with loss of both potassium and sodium, reduces the SID, and is associated with metabolic acidosis. Aggressive use of diuretics causes a net loss of free water over sodium and chloride and causes contraction alkalosis.

The most significant form of metabolic acidosis is associated with a net gain of organic anions (that may or may not be measured) and, consequently, a decreased SID.

1.  In dysoxia, liver dysfunction, and states of severe stress, lactate production exceeds metabolism, reducing the SID and resulting in acidosis (lactic acidosis).

2.  In diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) or during starvation or liver disease, βOHB and acetoacetate are produced, reducing SID, resulting in acidosis (ketoacidosis).

3.  In severe renal failure, Cl<sup>−</sup> , SO4<sup>2−</sup> , PO4<sup>3−</sup> (“fixed renal acids”), and various other metabolic intermediaries are not excreted, resulting in acidosis (renal acidosis).

Measurement of plasma lactate levels is performed by all blood gas analyzers, and ketones can be easily measured with point- of- care devices. Only “renal” acids are “unmeasured.”

The total weak acid pool (ATOT), principally serum albumin and phosphate, is also an important determinant of acid–base status. Hyperphosphatemia has long been associated with the acidosis of acute and chronic kidney injury. Hypoalbuminemia is common in critical care. Hypoalbuminemia decreases ATOT and is associated with metabolic alkalosis.<sup>24,25</sup> There is a strong association between hypoalbuminemia and severity of critical illness. Albumin deficits result from four different homeostatic changes: reprioritization of hepatic protein production favoring production of acute phase reactants and limiting albumin synthesis, capillary leak with loss of albumin into the interstitium, breakdown of preexisting albumin so that its constituent amino acids can be used for protein synthesis, and replacement of plasma with protein- free fluids.

## Regulation of Acid–Base Balance

Extracellular hydrogen ion activity appears to be tightly controlled by the body as a component of homeostasis. Probably, this regulation reflects a need to prevent rapid changes in extracellular electrochemical balance from interfering with the function of transcellular ion pumps. To prevent fluctuation a variety of intracellular and extracellular buffering systems have evolved.<sup>29</sup> A _buffer_ is a solution of two or more chemicals that minimizes changes in pH in response to the addition of an acid or base. Ideally, a buffer has a p _K_ a that is equal to the pH, and an ideal body buffer has a p _K_ a between 6.8 and 7.2. Most biologically salient buffers are weak acids.

It is valuable to view control of H<sup>+</sup> activity in terms of volatile and metabolic acids (mineral and organic). The major source of acid in the body is the volatile acid carbon dioxide, which produces 12,500 mEq of H<sup>+</sup> a day, mostly excreted by the lungs. An equivalent quantity of lactate is manufactured and processed by the body each day. In contrast, only 20 to 70 mEq of H<sup>+</sup> - promoting anions are excreted daily through the kidney.

Volatile acid is principally buffered by hemoglobin within erythrocytes. Carbon dioxide easily passes through cell membranes. Within the erythrocyte, CO2 combines with H2O, under the influence of the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, to form H2CO3, which immediately ionizes to H<sup>+</sup> and HCO3<sup>−</sup> . H<sup>+</sup> binds to histidine residues on deoxyhemoglobin (the “Haldane” effect), and HCO3<sup>−</sup> is actively pumped out of the cell. Chloride moves inward to maintain electroneutrality (the chloride shift) and to ensure the continued production of carbonic acid. CO2 is also buffered directly by hemoglobin (carbaminohemoglobin) and by plasma proteins (carbamino proteins). Venous blood contains 1.68 mEq/L extra CO2 over arterial blood: 65% as HCO3<sup>−</sup> and H<sup>+</sup> bound to hemoglobin, 27% as carbaminohemoglobin (CO2 bound to hemoglobin), and 8% dissolved.

When respiratory failure occurs, the principal CO2 buffering system, hemoglobin, becomes overwhelmed, leading to the rapid development of acidosis. In response, the kidney excretes an increased chloride load, using NH4<sup>+</sup> , a weak cation, for electrochemical balance. Thus extracellular fluid osmolality is maintained. This process is conventionally referred to as “metabolic compensation.” Chronic respiratory acidosis is associated with increase in total body CO2 content, reflected principally by an increase in serum HCO3<sup>−</sup> (see Table 44.1). Hypercapnia is associated with a progressive increase in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) HCO3<sup>−</sup> . Compensation for this hypercarbia is a reduction in CSF chloride<sup>30</sup> and an increase in CSF SID.<sup>31–33</sup> This is probably controlled by active transport mechanisms across the blood–brain barrier or at the level of the choroid plexus and can be blocked by furosemide and acetazolamide.<sup>34–37</sup> The result is a rightward shift in the PaCO2 response curve; the respiratory center responds to hypercarbia by increasing respiratory drive at a higher PaCO2 level than under normal conditions.

HCO3<sup>−</sup> is a dependent variable, that increases or decreases with PaCO2.<sup>38</sup> The rate of conversion of CO2 to HCO3<sup>−</sup> is dependent on carbonic anhydrase activity and occurs slowly. Thus it is possible to mathematically determine whether a rise in PaCO2 is acute or long- standing (see Table 44.1).

Metabolic acid is buffered principally by increased alveolar ventilation, producing respiratory alkalosis, and extracellular weak acids. These weak acids include plasma proteins, phosphate, and HCO3<sup>−</sup> . The HCO3<sup>−</sup> buffering system (92% of plasma buffering and 13% overall) is probably the most important extracellular buffer. The p _K_ a of bicarbonate is relatively low (6.1), but the system derives its importance from the enormous quantity of CO2 present in the body. The coupling of HCO3<sup>−</sup> and H2O produces CO2, which is then excreted through the lungs, increasing alveolar ventilation. Physicians must be aware of the importance of this compensatory mechanism. For example, anesthetized or critically ill patients on controlled mechanical ventilation lose the capacity to regulate their own PaCO2. Consequently, the combination of acute metabolic and respiratory acidosis can cause a devastating reduction in pH.

The major effect of the kidney on acid–base balance relates to renal handling of sodium and chloride ions. As dietary intake of sodium and chloride is roughly equal, the kidney excretes a net chloride load, using NH4<sup>+</sup> , to electrochemically neutralize urinary chloride.<sup>39</sup>

In metabolic acidosis, the kidneys preferentially excrete chloride. In metabolic alkalosis, chloride is retained, and sodium and potassium are excreted. The presence of bicarbonate in the urine reflects the need to maintain electrical neutrality. Abnormalities in the renal handling of chloride may be responsible for several inherited acid–base disturbances. In renal tubular acidosis, there is inability to excrete Cl<sup>−</sup> in proportion to Na<sup>+</sup> .<sup>40</sup> The diagnosis can be made by observing a hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, with inappropriately low levels of Cl<sup>−</sup> in the urine: the urinary SID is positive. If the urinary SID is negative, the process is not of renal origin. Similarly, pseudohypoaldosteronism appears to be due to high reabsorption of chloride.<sup>41</sup> Bartter syndrome is caused by a mutation in the gene encoding the chloride channel (CLCNKB) that regulates the Na- K- 2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2).<sup>42</sup>

The other causes of hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis are gastrointestinal losses (diarrhea, small bowel or pancreatic drainage), parenteral nutrition, excessive administration of saline, and the use of carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.

### THE CO2–BICARBONATE DESCRIPTIVE APPROACH (BOSTON)

Schwartz, Brackett, Relman, and colleagues at Tufts University in Boston developed the most popular descriptive approach to acid–base chemistry in the 1960s. Their formulation uses acid–base maps and the mathematical relationship between PaCO2 and plasma HCO3<sup>−</sup> (or total CO2), derived from the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, to classify acid–base disturbances in terms of two independent variables: PaCO2 and [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ].<sup>4,17,44</sup> To validate this approach, a number of patients with known acid–base disturbances, at steady states of compensation, were evaluated. The degree of compensation, relative to what was considered normal,

was measured for each disease state. The investigators were able to describe six primary states of acid–base imbalance, using linear equations or maps, relating hydrogen ion concentration to PaCO2 for respiratory disturbances, and PaCO2 to HCO3<sup>−</sup> concentration for metabolic disturbances (Fig. 44.2). For any given acid–base disturbance, an expected HCO3<sup>−</sup> concentration was determined. These were then compiled into a series of mathematical rules (see Table 44.1 and Box 44.2). For most simple disturbances, this is a reasonable approach. As discussed above, in acute respiratory acidosis, the [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] will increase by 1 mEq/l for every 10 mm Hg (1.3 kPa) elevation in PaCO2 above 40 mm Hg (5.3 kPa). In chronic respiratory acidosis, the [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] will increase by 3 mEq/l for every 10 mm Hg (1.3 kPa) elevation in PaCO2 above 40 mm Hg (5.3 kPa).

In acute metabolic acidosis, the [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] falls by 1 mEq/L for every 1 mEq/L in strong anions. The respiratory center is activated, resulting in a predictable fall in the PaCO2. This was neatly characterized by Winters in a pediatric population in 1967 and remains robust.<sup>45</sup> In acute metabolic acidosis, the PaCO2 (in mm Hg) falls predictably, using the 1.5 × [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] + 8 rule. For example, if the [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] is 12 mEq/L, then the expected PaCO2 is 1.5 × 12 + 8 = 26 mm Hg. If the PaCO2 is higher than this, then compensation is inadequate and there is a concomitant respiratory problem (for example ketoacidosis in the presence of a respiratory tract infection). Winters also described expected compensation using the base excess approach (see next section): for every 1 mEq/L reduction in the base excess the PaCO2 is expected to fall by 1 mm Hg, otherwise “compensation” is inadequate.

In metabolic alkalosis, in order to restore pH to homeostatic levels it is necessary to retain carbonic acid, and hypoventilation occurs, ultimately resulting in increased [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ]. The expected PaCO2 equals 0.7 × [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] + 20 (in mm Hg). So if a patient has a [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] of 34 mEq/L, then the PaCO2 should be 44 mm Hg.

### THE BASE EXCESS (COPENHAGEN) APPROACH

In metabolic acidosis, the addition of routinely unmeasured anions to the extracellular fluid results in a net gain of one H<sup>+</sup> for each anion. This is “buffered,” principally by bicarbonate, such that each anion gained results in an equivalent fall in [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ]. Thus the change in the [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] from baseline should reflect the total quantity of net anions gained. Adherents to the descriptive approach to acid–base, refer to this as the “delta” HCO3<sup>−</sup> . However, this is problematic, as it does not separate out the effect of CO2 metabolism on the [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ].

In 1948, Singer and Hastings<sup>50</sup> proposed that changes in the whole blood buffer base could be used to quantify the metabolic component independent of Henderson–Hasselbalch model. The buffer base represents the sum of the [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] and the non- volatile buffer ions (essentially the serum albumin, phosphate, and hemoglobin). Applying the law of electrical neutrality, the buffer base was observed to equal the electrical charge difference between strong ions. Thus, normal buffer base = [Na<sup>+</sup> ] + [K<sup>+</sup> ] − [Cl<sup>−</sup> ]. Alterations in buffer base represent changes in strong ion concentrations (which could not be easily measured in 1948). Buffer base increases in the presence of a metabolic alkalosis and decreases in metabolic acidosis. The major drawback of buffer base measurement is the potential for changes in buffering capacity associated with alterations in hemoglobin concentration and pH.

Following the development of sophisticated electrodes for measuring PaCO2 in Copenhagen in the early 1950s,<sup>51</sup> Astrup and colleagues developed the standard bicarbonate – the [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] at 37°C and at a PaCO2 of 40 mm Hg (5.3 kPa).<sup>52</sup> Building upon this work, Siggard- Andersen and Astrup<sup>53</sup> recognized that PaCO2 and [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] were not independent variables. As a result, they derived the base

excess (BE) as a measure that could differentiate respiratory from metabolic acid–base disturbances. As defined, the BE is the amount of strong acid (HCl, in mmol/L) or base (NaOH) required to return the pH to 7.4, assuming that PaCO2 is constant at 40 mm Hg (5.3 kPa) and that the temperature is 37°C.<sup>54</sup> Like the Boston group, the Siggard- Andersen data were derived from observations on a large population of patients. The investigators carefully titrated known amounts of acid or base to blood maintained by tonometry at various PaCO2 values and a wide range of haemoglobin concentration at 37°C. These studies led to the development of an alignment nomogram (Fig. 44.5, Table 44.3) that allowed for the determination of BE from a single measurement of pH, PaCO2, and hemoglobin concentration at 37°C. There have been a variety of calculations used to determine the BE since the 1960s.<sup>55</sup> That is because of several erroneous assumptions that Siggaard- Andersen made about extracellular (albumin, phosphate, proteins) and intracellular (hemoglobin) buffers, and the role of transcellular chloride movement.<sup>56</sup> The following formula is now used to calculate the standard base excess (SBE) or the base excess of extracellular fluid in blood gas machines:


![](assets/chapter-44/44._Perioperative_Acid-Base_Balance.pdf-0012-09.png)


SBE = [HCO – 3 ] actual  in mEq/L – 24.8<br>+ (16.2 × (pH – 7.40))<br>

Although various descriptions of this approach to acid–base chemistry refer to BE, and that term is reported on blood gas forms, when a negative base excess is reported, the correct terminology is _base deficit_ (= 1 − BE).<sup>57</sup> For simplicity, in the following discussion, I will refer to BE, keeping in mind that it may be positive (metabolic alkalosis) or negative (metabolic acidosis).

Application of simple mathematical rules allows for use of the BE in each of the common acid–base disturbances (Boxes 44.4 and 44.5; Fig. 44.6). For example, in acute respiratory acidosis or alkalosis, BE does not change. Conversely, in acute metabolic acidosis, the magnitude of change of the PaCO2 (in mm Hg) is the same as that of the BE (in mEq/L).

There has been considerable discussion over the past 60 years about the merits and demerits of the BE, as compared to the CO2–HCO3<sup>−</sup> system. However, there is little difference between the two: both equations and nomograms were derived from patient data and abstracted backward. Calculations use HCO3<sup>−</sup> as derived on a blood gas analyzer. Consequently, for most patients, either approach is relatively accurate but may be misleading because they do not allow the clinician to distinguish between, for example, acidosis due to lactate or chloride, or alkalosis due to dehydration or hypoalbuminemia. These measures may miss the presence of an acid–base disturbance entirely: for example, a hypoalbuminemic (metabolic alkalosis) critically ill patient with a lactic acidosis (metabolic acidosis) may have a normal pH, [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ], and BE values. This lack of precision may lead to inappropriate or inadequate therapy.

## Acid–Base Problems in Perioperative and Critical Care Medicine

### RESPIRATORY ACIDOSIS AND ALKALOSIS

In perioperative medicine, respiratory acid–base abnormalities are an uncommon complication of prolonged spontaneous breathing under anesthesia, inadequate mechanical ventilation (both acute respiratory acidosis), or excessive mechanical ventilation (metabolic alkalosis) (Table 44.4). Acute respiratory acidosis results from hypoventilation or increased dead space ventilation. Patients may manifest respiratory distress characterized by respiratory acidosis in the recovery room or surgical ICU. Assessment begins with an examination of the patient’s breathing pattern (Fig. 44.8): slow shallow breathing indicates impaired respiratory drive, rapid shallow breathing suggests chest wall or lung pathology, and obstructed breathing signifies airway obstruction. Blood gas analysis of acute respiratory acidosis will reflect a dramatic fall in pH, an elevated PaCO2, a slight rise in HCO3<sup>−</sup> (by 1 mEq/L for every 10 mm Hg [or 1.2 kPa] rise in PaCO2). The base excess should be zero. ### METABOLIC ACIDOSIS AND ALKALOSIS

### Metabolic Acidosis

Acute metabolic acidosis is caused by an alteration in SID or ATOT. SID is changed by an alteration in the relative quantity of strong anions to strong cations. Mineral anions and cations are gained due to exogenous intake or administration. Organic anions (lactate, ketones, nitrogen waste products) are generated via metabolic pathways and metabolized in the liver. Mineral anions and cations may be lost due to vomiting, diarrhea, intra- abdominal collections, diuresis, or renal tubular abnormalities, including acute kidney injury.

Acute metabolic acidosis is characterized by a pH of &lt;7.35, and a fall in both PaCO2 and [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] below the baseline. For patients without COPD or chronic CO2 retention, this represents a PaCO2 &lt;40 mm Hg (5.3 kPa) and a [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] &lt;24 mEq/L. There is a negative base excess (base deficit) whose magnitude represents the net strong anion gain. This simple approach indicates that an acidosis is present, and that it is metabolic in origin. To further investigate the acidosis, one or more of the analytical tools described above may be employed. The most widely used tool is the anion gap, which should be adjusted for albumin and include the plasma lactate. This differentiates hyperchloremic acidosis from acidosis associated with other measured and unmeasured anions. In the setting of metabolic acidosis, where possible, lactate, ketones, phosphate, and albumin should be measured as should surrogate markers of anion accumulation (serum creatinine and the osmolar gap). Causes of metabolic acidosis that are commonly encountered by anesthesiologists are discussed below.

### Lactic Acidosis

Lactate is one of the major sources of energy of the body; it is a circulating carbohydrate fuel.<sup>61</sup> Lactic acid is produced intracellularly from glucose, via lactate dehydrogenase, both in the presence and absence of oxygen. Lactic acid immediately dissociates due to its p _K_ a of 3.8, and H<sup>+</sup> is buffered intracellularly.<sup>62</sup> Anionic lactate is transported into the extracellular space. Between 12,500 to 1500 mEq of lactate enters the circulation each day from muscle (25%), brain (25%), skin (25%), erythrocytes (20%), and intestine (10%).<sup>63</sup> Lactate is widely used as an energy source, particularly by the brain, kidney, and heart, where it undergoes oxidative phosphorylation.<sup>61</sup> It is also transported to the liver where it undergoes gluconeogenesis and subsequent metabolism to CO2 and water (the Cori cycle). Hepatic processing can increase 10- fold during exercise.<sup>63</sup>

Accumulation of lactate in extracellular fluid is associated with metabolic acidosis: for each mEq/L rise in the lactate concentration, [HCO3<sup>−</sup> ] decreases in mEq/L by the same magnitude and pH falls correspondingly. Although controversy exists regarding the source of “acid” (H<sup>+</sup> ),<sup>64</sup> the generation of metabolic acidosis associated with hyperlactatemia (lactic acidosis) is entirely consistent with alterations in water dissociation and electrochemical balance.<sup>65</sup> Regardless of mechanism, the presence of hyperlactatemia and metabolic acidosis is universally known as “lactic acidosis,” in analogous to ketoacidosis or hyperchloremic acidosis. The presence of lactic acidosis is an excellent marker of acute critical illness, the magnitude of which often reflects the degree of hyperlactatemia. Lactic acidosis occurs when the production of lactate in the body is greater than the liver’s capacity to clear it, i.e., there is a problem of overproduction or inadequate clearance or both.

In nature lactate exists as two isoforms: L- Lactate, which is produced by the human body, and is measured by blood gas analyzers, and D- Lactate, which can only be produced by fermentation by bacteria. The formation of L- Lactate (lactate) from pyruvate is catalyzed by lactate dehydrogenase. Lactate is used as a “buffer” in isotonic fluids, principally lactated Ringer’s solution and Hartmann’s solution; these contain a racemic mixture of both D and L lactate at a concentration of 14 mEq/L each. In this setting, lactate is rapidly metabolized to CO2 and water, and hence HCO3<sup>−</sup> .

Under normal conditions the ratio of lactate to pyruvate is less than 20:1. In anaerobic conditions, for example following vigorous exercise, lactate levels increase dramatically, and high levels of circulating lactate are frequently interpreted as evidence of increased glycolytic activity. However, lactate is often produced under aerobic conditions. Activation of beta-adrenergic receptors in skeletal muscle is by stress (increased circulating catecholamines) or exogenous infusion (epinephrine/norepinephrine infusions), or even nebulized bronchodilators; these increase [lactate], resulting in “aerobic glycolysis.”

Plasma lactate and arterial pH should be measured early in any critically ill patient; it is now a diagnostic component of the definition of septic shock.<sup>66</sup> [lactate] >2 mEq/L is clinically significant and a level of 5 mEq/L in the presence of metabolic acidosis is considered severe.<sup>67</sup> Isolated hyperlactatemia in the absence of acidosis is of unclear clinical significance.<sup>68</sup>

Traditionally, lactic acidosis has been described as taking one of two forms: type A (also termed as type 1), where global inadequate oxygen delivery is seen in hypovolemic/ haemorrhagic shock while type B (or type 2) occurs despite normal global oxygen delivery and tissue perfusion. Lactic acidosis may also develop in situations where there is significant regional hypoperfusion. Examples include bowel ischemia, where lactate is produced in large quantity due to glycolysis despite global oxygen delivery that is normal. Type B lactic acidosis is associated with any state where circulating catecholamines (endogenous or exogenous) are in excess. Examples include simple exercise and the hyperinflammatory state of trauma or sepsis. Type B lactic acidosis may also be seen in cyanide poisoning (associated with sodium nitroprusside), with biguanides (metformin, which blocks hepatic gluconeogenesis), and in hypercatabolic diseases such as lymphoma, leukemia, AIDS, or DKA. In

critical illness, type A and type B lactic acidosis frequently coexist.

It is universally accepted that lactic acidosis is a sensitive marker of disease severity.<sup>69</sup> Persistence of lactic acidosis strongly predicts poor outcomes in acute illness.<sup>70–72</sup> Rapid clearance (i.e., reduced plasma concentration presumably due to reduced production and increased metabolism) of lactate has been associated with improved outcomes.<sup>73,74</sup> What is controversial is whether it is possible to institute therapy that improves outcomes and simultaneously increases lactate clearance. Simplistically, improved overall perfusion consequent of blood or isotonic fluid administration should reduce glycolysis and reduce lactate production, increasing hepatic blood flow and increasing metabolism. However, not all patients who are under- resuscitated are hyperlactatemic, and probably the minority of patients who are hyperlactatemic are under- resuscitated. Although fluid resuscitation with the goal of normalizing plasma lactate may be associated with improved outcomes, excessive or late fluid resuscitation increases mortality.<sup>75</sup> It is important to note that, following fluid bolus, plasma lactate rapidly falls, perhaps due to hemodilution, and then falls slowly, at 10%–20% per hour, due to rate-limited hepatic clearance. If plasma lactate does not fall, fluid resuscitation should be discontinued.<sup>76</sup>

The presence of evidence of good overall oxygen delivery and normal consumption as measured by cardiac output monitors and mixed venous oxygen saturation in lactic acidosis is not reassuring. Indeed, bowel ischemia may be indicated by otherwise unexplained lactic acidosis, and the availability heuristic associated with sepsis may result in delayed diagnosis and inappropriate fluid resuscitation (Fig. 44.9).

Metformin is associated with severe lactic acidosis, a phenomenon that appears more likely when the patient has liver dysfunction, is dehydrated, in heart failure or acute kidney injury, or septic.<sup>77</sup> The mechanism of harm is unknown, although metformin is thought to impair oxidative metabolism in hepatocyte mitochondria, and blocks gluconeogenesis. Renal impairment appears to be a significant risk factor. The patient presenting with metforminassociated lactic acidosis frequently looks reasonably well, despite plasma lactate that may exceed 10 mmol/L. There is no specific treatment except for withdrawal of the drug, gentle rehydration, and patience.

D- lactate-induced acidosis can occur, typically in patients with short bowel syndrome and bacterial overgrowth. It manifests as a widened anion gap (or SIG) acidosis where no other potential source of metabolic acid is identified. Crucially, point of care blood gas analyzers do not measure D- lactate. However, many labs can measure the molecule, and this test should be considered in a high- risk patient (post major abdominal surgery) with unexplained acidosis.

### KETOACIDOSIS

Ketone bodies – acetone (&lt;2%), acetoacetate (20%), and 3- βOHB (78%) – are normal by- products of fat metabolism. They are produced when fatty acids are metabolized by the liver and glucose is unavailable as an energy source. During ketogenesis, acetoacetate is generated from acetyl coenzyme A. This either enters mitochondria to be converted

to βOHB or it spontaneously decarboxylates to acetone. Ketones are transported by blood to the tissues, in particular the brain, where they are used as energy sources. Under normal dietary conditions, ketones are undetectable in blood and urine. However, in situations where fat becomes the primary source of energy (for example in starvation or low carbohydrate diets), ketones can be measured in blood (principally βOHB) and urine (principally acetoacetate). In a variety of clinical situations, such as prolonged starvation, alcoholism, alcoholic or obesity- related steatohepatitis, and most commonly, insulin deficiency (diabetes mellitus), a dramatic increase in circulating ketones occurs. As these are strong anionic compounds, ketones reduce strong ion difference, resulting in metabolic acidosis. When this occurs in type 1 diabetes mellitus, it is known as diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

Ketoacidosis may be the first manifestation of type 1 diabetes mellitus or may result from poor glycemic control or specific stress triggers such as infection, trauma, or surgery. Typically, there is an imbalance between the relative quantity of insulin and glucose-promoting hormones (cortisol, epinephrine, and glucagon). Blood glucose increases exceeding the renal reabsorption threshold result in glycosuria, osmotic diuresis, dehydration, and the vicious cycle of activation of stress hormones. In the absence of insulin, or consequent of severe insulin resistance, there is unrestrained oxidation of fatty acids to ketones in the liver. Regardless of the cause, there is usually elevated blood glucose, significant dehydration, and depletion of potassium, phosphorous, and magnesium.

The diagnosis of DKA is relatively simple: usually there is a clear history of either diabetes mellitus or polyuria and polydipsia – the patient presents to the emergency room with hyperglycemia and glycosuria, and, usually, positive urinary ketones. Plasma ketones can be measured using handheld devices and testing strips. βOHB levels are reported in mmol/L. The quantitative impact of this result on base excess is difficult to estimate: the minimal impact is equivalent to 1 × the βOHB level in mmol/L, the maximal impact is 2 × the βOHB level. Consequently, if the βOHB level is 4 mmol/L, the impact on base excess is between −4 and −8 mEq/L, accounting for unmeasured acetoacetate.

Ketoacidosis is confirmed by performing arterial blood gas analysis. Every hospital has a protocol for the management of diabetic ketoacidosis. Insulin is administered by an infusion (with or without an initial bolus); this may be a weight-based fixed rate insulin infusion (0.1 units/kg/hour) or more traditional infusion based on blood glucose.<sup>78</sup> The patient is resuscitated with several liters of isotonic crystalloid (usually isotonic saline [0.9%] solution), and when blood glucose falls within a “controlled” range, intravenous dextrose is administered. Insulin suppresses ketone production. Glucose is required to assist ketone metabolism, which can take some time. Two major mistakes are made in the management of DKA. Administration of 0.9% NaCl results in an entirely predictable hyperchloremic acidosis; this may or may not be harmful but may be perceived incorrectly as persistence of ketoacidosis. ### RENAL ACIDOSIS

The kidney excretes water and a variety of metabolic byproducts, principally derived from protein. The kidney also excretes surplus electrolytes, some of which are strong ions, including chloride, sulfate, formate, urate, citric acid cycle metabolites (fumarate, citrate), and phosphate. These accumulate in acute kidney injury (AKI) and cause “renal acidosis.” Early in AKI, hyperchloremia is the principal source of acidosis; subsequently, 50%–60% of acidosis is caused by unmeasured anions and up to 30% is associated with hyperphosphatemia.<sup>88</sup> Fifty percent of patients with AKI in critical illness have a normal anion gap.<sup>88</sup>

In perioperative medicine, acute kidney injury (AKI) may accompany hypotension, hypovolemia, renal hypoperfusion (from aortic cross- clamping or intra- abdominal hypertension), rhabdomyolysis, sepsis, or urinary obstruction. Irrespective of the cause, patients develop oliguria, volume overload, and hyperkalemia secondary to metabolic acidosis.

The identification of metabolic acidosis in acute kidney injury is key to the diagnosis, severity, and therapeutic strategy in AKI. Although serum creatinine is the most widely used marker of renal function, isolated readings are unhelpful, and fluid resuscitation can artificially lower the creatinine concentration by dilution; diuresis can artificially elevate it. Metabolic acidosis, particularly in the presence of hyperkalemia, with an elevated creatinine should always prompt the clinician to determine the extent of “renal acidosis.” This is not easy – hyperchloremia typically accompanies renal acidosis, but isotonic saline solution is frequently administered to patients with elevated creatinine under the mistaken belief that patients are less likely to develop hyperkalemia compared with balanced salt solutions.<sup>89</sup> A study of anephric patients undergoing renal transplantation demonstrated that patients treated with normal saline

were more acidotic and hyperkalemic than patients treated with lactated Ringer’s solution.<sup>89</sup> Hyperphosphatemia is a minor contributor to renal acidosis, and currently no tests are available to clinically identify the unmeasured anions, except by a process of exclusion. Hence, renal acidosis is usually diagnosed by identifying a widened anion gap, base deficit gap, or strong ion gap, and excluding ketones and lactate. Usually, the key decision step regarding renal replacement therapy involves uncontrolled hyperkalemia. Renal acidosis can be temporarily controlled by administration of sodium bicarbonate (assuming the patient can clear carbon dioxide), by increasing the strong ion difference.<sup>90</sup> This may be necessary if the risk of delaying surgery exceeds the risk of delaying kidney replacement therapy. It should be noted that in critical illness, delaying kidney replacement therapy was associated with a 4.7% increase in mortality at 90 days.<sup>91</sup>

### HYPERCHLOREMIC ACIDOSIS

Hyperchloremia has been a known cause of metabolic acidosis since the dawn of acid–base chemistry,<sup>12</sup> but, due to difficulty in measuring serum Cl<sup>−</sup> during most of the 20th century, hyperchloremia was largely ignored until recent decades. The extracellular space contains 110 to 130 g of salt. For a 70- kg male (extracellular fluid volume 18 L), this is approximately 58 g of Na<sup>+</sup> (3.22 g/L) and 65 g (3.62 g/L) of Cl<sup>−</sup> . The average American ingests approximately 3 g or more of salt (NaCl) each day (2.3 g is recommended). To maintain the normal ratio of Na<sup>+</sup> to Cl<sup>−</sup> (1.4:1 approximately), the body needs to excrete 30% more Cl<sup>−</sup> than Na<sup>+</sup> . This is one of the major excretory roles of the kidney. This approximates to 15 to 20 mEq of Cl<sup>−</sup> excretion per day; to maintain electrical neutrality Cl<sup>−</sup> is excreted with ammonium (NH4<sup>+</sup> ), a by-product of nitrogen metabolism. In renal failure, Cl<sup>−</sup> accumulates, and this is often the cause of the early metabolic acidosis associated with acute kidney injury. For decades, it has been hypothesized that elevated circulating levels of Cl<sup>−</sup> , usually because of intravenous administration, may actually be nephrotoxic, due, presumably, to increased metabolic demands on the kidney. One liter of isotonic saline solution (0.9% normal saline) contains 9 g of salt, 3.5 g Na<sup>+</sup> and 5.5 g Cl<sup>−</sup> (154 mEq/L of each). For a euchloremic patient (the human body does not store chloride, unlike, for example, calcium), this means that if 1 L of 0.9% normal saline is administered (most or all of Cl<sup>−</sup> must be excreted), there is an 8- to 10- fold increase in metabolic load for the kidney. Moreover, as normal saline has a SID of 0, elevated plasma chloride is usually associated with a non- anion gap (“hyperchloremic”) metabolic acidosis.<sup>92</sup>

Hyperchloremic acidosis is a complication of renal tubular acidosis, which is caused by a defect in chloride excretion. The urine is relatively alkaline. Hyperchloremia also occurs when ureters have been re- implanted in the bowel, after, for example, cystectomy, and excreted chloride is reabsorbed.

Is hyperchloremia and hyperchloremic acidosis clinically significant? As a cause of acidosis, hyperchloremia is less sinister than other causes: in a study of critically ill patients with various acid–base disorders, mortality was highest for lactic acidosis (56%); for strong ion gap (SIG) acidosis it was 39%, and for hyperchloremic acidosis 29%

( _P_ &lt; .001).<sup>93</sup> Nevertheless, hyperchloremia may result in clinically significant organ dysfunction. An observational study of 31,000 surgical patients comparing intravenous saline to intravenous balanced salt solutions demonstrated significant worse outcomes.<sup>94</sup> Complications increased by the use of normal saline included postoperative infections, blood transfusions, and kidney injury requiring dialysis.

A hyperchloremic state may be associated with nephrotoxicity: saline infusion has been associated with reduced renal blood flow, renal vasoconstricton, reduced glomerular filtration, and splanchnic hypoperfusion.<sup>95</sup> In a relatively large before- and- after cohort study of patients treated in an Australian ICU, the use of chloride- rich fluids was associated with a 3.7% absolute increase in the risk for need of renal replacement therapy relative to balanced salt solution.<sup>96</sup>

Two large randomized controlled trials looked at the use of isotonic saline solution versus balanced salt solutions in emergency medicine patients<sup>97</sup> and critically ill patients.<sup>98</sup> Although the volume of fluid administered intravenously was relatively low, certainly in comparison with perioperative patients, in both studies there was approximately a 1% increase in renal complications. Two trials, in 2021 and 2022, randomized critically ill patients to normal saline or balanced salt solutions, but failed to show any significant difference in outcomes.<sup>99,100</sup> These studies were flawed because the patients were frequently fully resuscitated at the time of enrollment, received very little resuscitation fluid, and failed to demonstrate a significant difference in plasma chloride or sodium to chloride levels. A subsequent meta- analysis looked at six trials of normal saline vs. balanced salt solutions and found that there was an 85% probability that balanced salt solution reduced mortality in critically ill patients.<sup>101</sup>

Normal saline, originally introduced by Hamburger in the 19th century, labelled “normal” due to flawed research,

has been the most widely used intravenous fluid for over a century, despite minimal research demonstrating its clinical efficacy and safety.<sup>102</sup> As mounting research demonstrates that this particular fluid may be harmful, and with a variety of other balanced solutions available for clinical practice, the role of normal saline in perioperative medicine is highly questionable.

### PERIOPERATIVE METABOLIC ALKALOSIS

Perioperative metabolic alkalosis is usually of iatrogenic origin. Over- ventilation of patients with chronic respiratory failure results in acute metabolic alkalosis because the presence of chronic compensatory alkalosis associated with chloride loss in urine has not been considered (Fig. 44.10). More frequently, metabolic alkalosis is associated with increased SID due to sodium gain. This abnormality results from the administration of fluids in which sodium is “buffered” by weak ions, citrate (in blood products), acetate (in parenteral nutrition), and, of course, bicarbonate. It is important to recognize that buffer ions such as citrate, acetate, gluconate, and lactate, are, under normal conditions, rapidly cleared by the liver and do not contribute to acid–base balance. Sodium and chloride obey the law of conservation of mass. Sodium gain is “chloride sensitive” alkalosis, treated by administration of net loads of chloride: 0.9% NaCl, potassium chloride, calcium chloride and, occasionally, hydrogen chloride. It is important to correct chloride- sensitive alkalosis, as the normal compensatory measure is hypoventilation, increasing PaCO2 that can lead to “CO2 narcosis.”

Perioperative fluid therapy remains highly controversial. Isotonic fluids are usually administered due to fear of cerebral edema associated with stress- induced water retention. However, this results in large amounts of solute, in particular sodium and chloride, accumulating in the extravascular space. Acquired hypernatremia is associated with adverse clinical outcomes and is very difficult to treat.<sup>103</sup> Balanced salt solutions appear safer than normal saline, but large volume administration of these “resuscitation” fluids are also associated with worse outcomes.<sup>104</sup>

### ACID–BASE DISTURBANCES IN CRITICAL ILLNESS

Critically ill patients may have many confounding acid– base disturbances that are not evident when only a single quantitative measure, such as base deficit, is employed. Patients will frequently have perturbations of PCO2, SID, and ATOT, and significant pathology may be overlooked due to apparently normal blood gas results.<sup>105</sup>

The most common single disturbance in acid–base chemistry in critically ill patients is hypoalbuminemia.<sup>106</sup> This abnormality is ubiquitous and is associated with metabolic alkalosis. Hypoalbuminemia may mask significant alterations in SID, for example, lactic or renal acidosis. Therefore, when the anion gap is used in critically ill patients it should be corrected for albumin.<sup>27</sup> Similarly, the use of base deficit to predict lactate is unreliable in critical illness, particularly in patients undergoing secondary deterioration.<sup>26</sup> Furthermore, prolonged respiratory failure, with associated hypercarbia, leads to additional metabolic alkalosis due to chloride loss in urine (see Fig. 44.10).<sup>107</sup> Kidney injury is associated with accumulation of metabolic by- products, phosphate, renal anions, chloride, and other unmeasured anions, leading initially to metabolic acidosis. However, late, oliguric renal failure may be associated with significant contraction alkalosis, due to loss of sodium, potassium, and free water.

Critically ill patients are vulnerable to significant changes in SID and free water. Nasogastric suctioning causes chloride loss; diarrhea leads to sodium and potassium deficits. Surgical drains placed in tissue beds may remove fluids with varying electrolyte concentrations (the pancreatic bed, for example, secretes fluid rich in sodium). Fever, sweating, evaporation from denuded tissue, and inadequately humidified ventilator circuits all can lead to large-volume insensible loss and contraction alkalosis.

Infusions administered to patients may be responsible for unrecognized alterations in serum chemistry. Many antibiotics, for example piperacillin–tazobactam, are diluted in sodium- rich solutions. Others, such as vancomycin, are administered in large volumes of free water (5% dextrose). Lorazepam is diluted in propylene glycol, large volumes of which will cause metabolic acidosis similar to that seen with ethylene glycol.<sup>108</sup>

Continuous kidney replacement therapy (CKRT) is used in critical illness to hemofilter and hemodialyse patients who are hemodynamically unstable. Rocktaschel<sup>109</sup> and colleagues have demonstrated that CKRT resolved the acidosis of acute kidney injury by removing strong ions and phosphate. However, in the presence of hypoalbuminemia, use of dialysis to correct a metabolic acidosis may unmask a metabolic alkalosis due to hypoalbuminemia. CKRT is not an effective treatment for lactic or ketoacidosis acidosis. Other, seemingly innocuous therapies may cause significant disturbances to acid–base balance. Loop diuretics, such as furosemide, are often administered to critically ill patients. These agents preferentially excrete water over electrolytes and provoke a contraction alkalosis.<sup>110</sup> Similarly, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as acetazolamide may be used to treat patients with hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis or respiratory alkalosis by decreasing the serum SID. This effect is completely explained by the increased renal excretion ratio of sodium to chloride, resulting in an increase in plasma chloride.<sup>111</sup>

Neurosurgical patients are vulnerable to a variety of acid–base disturbances due to osmotherapy or from disturbances caused by brain injury. Most commonly, normal saline solution is commonly administered to these patients and results in hyperchloremic acidosis.<sup>112</sup> Diabetes insipidus (DI) is a frequent complication of severe head injury, particularly when the patient is progressing toward brain death. DI is caused by damage to the pituitary and/or the hypothalamus and results in a loss of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion. Absent ADH, the kidney is unable to concentrate the urine and a massive diuresis follows. The disorder is characterized by an increase in plasma osmolality in the presence of a low urinary osmolality. DI will typically manifest as a contraction alkalosis. The treatment is hormonal replacement with either vasopressin or desmopressin.

## Treating Acid–Base Disturbances

In general, in acute illness and perioperative medicine, acid–base disturbances are clinical indicators of disease processes that are more harmful than hydrogen ion abnormalities themselves. Correcting the pH is usually unlikely to resolve the problem, except in certain circumstances, such as hyperkalemia in acute kidney injury, where acidosis is the major cause of the problem.

The treatment of acid–base abnormalities is determined by whether the acids are organic or minerals. Organic acids can be metabolized, excreted, or dialyzed from the body. Diabetic and nondiabetic ketoacidosis are treated primarily with insulin, intravenous fluid, and glucose. The ketoacids causing metabolic acidosis are metabolized by the liver. Acute kidney injury is treated with dialysis and ultrafiltration, which directly removes unmeasured anions. Surprisingly, there are no clear guidelines regarding the optimal timing of initiation of renal replacement therapy,<sup>91,113</sup> particularly in the perioperative period.<sup>114</sup>

For decades, sodium bicarbonate has been used to “correct” acidosis. The sodium component, as a strong anion, widens the strong ion difference and is alkalizing. Simultaneously, the HCO3<sup>−</sup> moiety provides buffer for hydrogen

ions, generating CO2, which is presumably excreted from the body by increased alveolar ventilation. In respiratory failure, sodium bicarbonate will worsen respiratory acidosis despite increased SID. CO2 also enters cells and may cause intracellular acidosis, the clinical significance of which is unclear.<sup>115</sup>

Sodium bicarbonate is commonly used to treat hyperchloremic acidosis. For patients with renal tubular acidosis, this involves long- term treatment with sodium bicarbonate tablets and chloride restriction. In acquired hyperchloremic acidosis, intravenous sodium bicarbonate corrects the base deficit,<sup>116</sup> but the benefit is unclear. The major drawbacks of sodium bicarbonate therapy include sodium and volume overload, metabolic alkalosis, hypertension, and hypocalcemia.

Sodium bicarbonate therapy has been extensively studied in lactic acidosis and circulatory shock.<sup>117</sup> A randomized trial of 389 critically ill patients with metabolic acidosis compared the administration of 4.2% (hypertonic) sodium bicarbonate to keep pH above 7.3, up to a maximum of 1000 mL in the first 24 hours, versus no intervention.<sup>118</sup> Although there were no differences in the primary outcome, 28- day mortality, the patients who received sodium bicarbonate had a lower incidence of acute kidney injury and requirement for kidney replacement therapy. It is unclear why there was a reduction in the need for CKRT (perhaps the reduction in acidosis delayed the decision to start CKRT) and resulted in the intervention being avoided completely,<sup>113</sup> or reversal of acidosis reduced vasopressor requirement and improved kidney blood flow, but this is merely speculation. Previous meta- analyses have failed to demonstrate benefit from sodium bicarbonate therapy,<sup>119</sup> presumably due to the paucity of available research. Until larger multicenter trials have been conducted, sodium bicarbonate therapy should be used with caution for treatment of metabolic acidosis, due to circulatory shock or ketoacidosis. It is also unlikely that sodium bicarbonate therapy benefits perioperative patients at risk for postoperative kidney failure.<sup>120</sup>

Metabolic alkalosis is rarely seen in elective perioperative care. It is most likely encountered when unmasked by over-ventilation of patients who chronically retain CO2. Minute ventilation should be reduced. Critically ill patients may have metabolic alkalosis due to chloride deficit, free water deficit, or hypoalbuminemia. Short- term therapy, such as carbon dioxide rebreathing, should be followed by treating the underlying problem – for example, with opioids or benzodiazepines.

## Summary

Much of the confusion regarding acid–base chemistry relates to the attempt to apply observational approaches, such as that of Henderson–Hasselbalch, and Schwartz and Brackett, to the entire spectrum of pathologic processes. The use of physical chemistry principles has permitted easier explanation of acid–base balance, and tools to apply to a wide variety of clinical situations.<sup>125</sup> This does not suggest that various traditional approaches are incorrect, and calculations and nomograms derived originally from the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation remain useful. All acid–base disorders can be explained in terms of SID, ATOT, and PaCO2. This is important to anesthesiologists, who may significantly impact acid–base balance with our choice of fluids and mechanical ventilation strategy, and who frequently encounter complex acid–base disorders in the operating room and ICU.
