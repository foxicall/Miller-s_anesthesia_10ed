- **KEY POINTS** The reasons for monitoring the brain’s response to anesthesia are to aid and confirm optimal personalized precision drug delivery, and to prevent or detect adverse events such as unintended consciousness.

   - In response to surgical nociceptive input, cardiovascular responses, somatic movement, and consciousness are controlled by different systems and are not closely correlated with each other.

   - The spectrogram presents the totality of the frontal electroencephalogram (EEG) in one glance. ᑏ An alpha- delta EEG pattern is the primary target for most anesthesia drug delivery because it indicates adequate drug- induced thalamocortical hyperpolarization in the face of surgical nociceptive stimulation.

   - For the same EEG pattern, a somatic response to noxious stimulation is common with propofol anesthesia, less common with sevoflurane, and least common with the addition of an opioid.

   - Use of EEG monitoring decreases intraoperative awareness, with recall by more than a half as compared to using clinical monitoring, but the advantage is much less when compared to endtidal agent monitoring.

   - EEG is very sensitive to all sorts of noise. Muscle (EMG) activity has a huge effect on all EEG indices, and is a signal that the patient is grimacing.

   - Clinicians should be aware that EEG indices are less reliable in the elderly, who may have an acceptable level of anesthesia even at relatively high index values.

   - To use EEG monitoring effectively, anesthesia providers need to fully understand its failings.

## Why Should we Monitor the Brain During Anesthesia?

Before considering the methods of brain monitoring in detail, it is essential to consider the underlying rationale for such monitoring. The vast majority of general anesthetics are delivered using only the most rudimentary behavioral assessment of brain state. However, modern anesthesia is characterized by intense technological monitoring of almost all of the other organ systems of the body.<sup>1</sup> In a recent British study, depth of anesthesia monitors were used in only 2.8% cases,<sup>2</sup> which may be astonishing to any person from outside the specialty, but there are reasons for this situation. Firstly, modern anesthetic drugs have a reasonably wide therapeutic ratio and an acceptably quick offset – so it is possible to follow population- based drug delivery schemas and overdose healthy patients with relative impunity. When using this regimen, the devastating complication of intraoperative explicit recall is very rare. Secondly, it is still unclear whether relative anesthesia overdose actually causes significant adverse intraoperative or postoperative outcomes. Finally, the existing methods of brain

monitoring are demonstrably imperfect in many respects, largely because of our incomplete understanding of the strangeness of the brain.

General anesthesia constitutes an ethical pact with our patients who, quite reasonably, expect absence of experience of intraoperative events. General anesthesia can be defined as a drug- induced syndrome of suppressed responses to surgery. Optimal general anesthesia encompasses explicit amnesia, perceptual disconnection (and maybe full unconsciousness), autonomic stability, immobility, and prevention of hyperalgesia, neuronal apoptosis, stress responses, and immune suppression. To achieve this for every patient requires titration of specific drugs to their effect in individuals, rather than crude estimation of doses from population averages. The main argument in favor of brain monitoring rests on this idea of targeted personalized precision drug delivery. The anesthesia provider needs to be able to accurately titrate drug dosage for the extremes of the probability distribution (i.e., to the “at- risk” very sick patients and to the very resistant patients). ### THE HARMS OF GIVING TOO LITTLE ANESTHESIA

Insufficient anesthesia may result in incomplete amnesia, unconsciousness, and autonomic overactivity. Although awareness with recall is rare, it often has devastating psychological consequences for the patient,<sup>3</sup> so any incidence is unacceptable. Some notable aspects are listed in Box 36.1. The question of whether the use of brain monitoring reduces the incidence of accidental intraoperative awareness and recall caused by relative underdosing of hypnotic anesthetic drugs is addressed in later sections.

### THE HARMS OF GIVING TOO MUCH ANESTHESIA

If hypnotic drug delivery is based on clinical judgment and population estimates then, by definition, many patients will be receiving excessive hypnotic drug. This raises the question of whether overdose of general anesthetic drug is harmful to the brain (contributing to delayed emergence and impaired cognitive recovery), or to the immune system (contributing to increased risk of cancer spread and infection). These are open questions, and the focus of much ongoing research, but could clearly be prevented by using brain monitoring during anesthesia.

## What Would we Like from an Ideal Monitor of Anesthetic Effect?

Anesthesia causes a progressive global neurological depression and the concept of “depth of anesthesia” has been around since the times of diethyl ether. It is widely believed that as anesthetic dose is increased, the effects on the central nervous system and other body systems progress

monotonically, which is more or less true when increasing doses of anesthesia are given to unstimulated individuals. However, in clinical settings anesthetic requirement also depends upon the intensity of surgical stimulation. Interindividual variation in baseline physiology and pharmacodynamic responses bring further difficulty in establishing what constitutes sufficient but not excessive dosing – the elusive “sweet spot” that clinicians seek. It is therefore worth pausing to consider precisely what features are clinically desirable from a monitoring system. It is crucial that our monitoring objectives are clear.

1.  A continuous index that reflects the effects of increasing concentration of hypnotic drug

2.  An individualized measure of anesthetic brain state reliably reflecting risk of conscious experience

   - a.  Definite positive identification of episodes of conscious experience in real time to allow treatment and prevention of recall

   - b.  A warning that the patient is approaching a state where subjective experience is possible

   - c.  A reliable indication of when anesthesia may be safely wound back with low risk of inadvertent awareness to minimize undesirable systemic drug effects, reduce economic and environmental costs, and facilitate faster emergence from anesthesia and health service efficiency

3.  Prediction of (absence) of autonomic or motor responses to stimulus

4.  Other brain pathology

   - a.  An individualized measure of brain frailty to predict postoperative cognitive complications

   - b.  Diagnosis of other neurological conditions such as seizures and ischemia

## Consciousness Terminology

Since anesthesia is generally equated with unconsciousness, some working terminology is required to distinguish between brain states. The modern description of consciousness is discussed in more detail in Chapter 8. However, in order to understand the EEG correlates of anesthesia, it is necessary to be cognizant of some aspects of consciousness.<sup>4</sup> If we define consciousness phenomenologically as _subjective awareness of existence_ , then as patients become more wakeful they are able to perform more diverse and complex cognitive tasks. This is usually conceptualized as a progressive increase in the _contents_ of consciousness.<sup>5</sup> Endogenous conscious experiences—such as dreaming— are described as “ _disconnected” consciousness_<sup>6</sup> (it is not connected to the body or outside world and is a failure of attention mechanisms). In contrast, _“connected” consciousness_ can be used to describe experience of the environment or one’s body (extero- or interoception). _Wakefulness_ is characterized by connected consciousness and the functional engagement of cognitive and behavioral responses to the external world. _Sleep_ is characterized by reduced or disconnected consciousness and relatively inhibited sensory activity but can be overcome with sufficient stimulation. _Anesthesia_ is a reversible drug- induced unconscious state that cannot be overcome by stimulation, whereas _sedation_

For example, the patient’s conscious experience may be vivid or muted, may or may not be accompanied by emotion or sense of self, may have absent volition (cognitive– motor dissociation), and core interoceptive senses of pain and bodily conditions may not reach conscious awareness.

### NOCICEPTION AND ANTINOCICEPTION

“The patient had a depth of anesthesia index of 42 and then they suddenly sat up in the middle of the thyroidectomy. The EEG is useless!” Versions of this common observation are the primary reason for the failure of widespread uptake of EEG monitoring. In this respect the EEG is like all the other monitors used during anesthesia: they can be used effectively only if all their limitations are properly recognized and understood. For example, blood pressure is universally monitored during anesthesia, but the numerical value of

the arterial blood pressure is merely a proxy for adequate organ perfusion. Any experienced anesthesiologist knows that there might be situations of extreme vasoconstriction and tissue ischemia in the presence of a normal blood pressure or, conversely, acceptable tissue perfusion in a vasodilated patient with a low blood pressure, or that preexisting arteriopathy might result in dangerous heterogeneity of tissue perfusion. Similar principles need to be applied for the correct interpretation of EEG patterns.

The cardinal feature that separates anesthesia from sleep is lack of response to noxious stimuli with anesthesia. It is important to acknowledge that the response to nociceptive input has several somewhat independent dimensions:

1. **Somatic movements.** Mediated at spinal cord level.

2. **Generalized autonomic system response.** Usually the sympathetic branch predominates, but not uncommonly extreme vagal effects are seen (e.g., fainting). Although much of the sympathetic nervous system is peripheral, there are important mediating circuits within the central nervous system, and it is on these that anesthetic drugs exert beneficial modulation.

3. **Arousal.** In the brain, noxious input tends to cause wakefulness and demand attention. Sufficient general anesthesia with hypnotic drugs might prevent wakefulness, but it is important to realize that the presence of unblocked underlying nociceptive stimulation induces brain excitatory aminergic and glutamatergic activity that may have various deleterious effects.

## Clinical Signs in Anesthesia Monitoring

### NEUROLOGICAL AND CLINICAL OBSERVATIONS OF ANESTHETIC EFFECT

Since the seminal work by Snow, and later Guedel, the predominant method of giving general anesthesia has been titration to clinically observable end points. Typically, these have consisted of somatic responsiveness to noxious stimulation; brainstem reflexes (respiration, eye movements and reflexes, lacrimation, pupil size and reaction, and laryngeal and pharyngeal reflexes); and autonomic nervous system signs (sweating, blood pressure, and heart rate). Although these observations were derived using historical singleagent anesthesia (chloroform and ether), these clinical signs have been updated by Brown<sup>7</sup> to be applicable in modern multidrug anesthesia and linked with putative brain circuit mechanisms of action for various drugs. The main pressure for the development of specific brain monitoring technology has been the fact that these signs are not reliable indicators of consciousness.

### Responsiveness

Before the advent of neuromuscular blocking drugs, somatic responses were an obvious indication of inadequate anesthesia. Deep paralysis made intraoperative wakefulness without any ability to communicate this to the anesthesia provider possible. Importantly, however, a sizable proportion of awareness reports occur in patients who have not received neuromuscular blocking drugs. Reports of distress

are uncommon in these cases. They are examples of various states of dissociation between a subjective experience of consciousness and the ability to produce a motor response to communicate this to the outside world. The underlying neuropharmacological mechanisms for these states are not well understood. By analogy to other neurological pathologies, they could include anesthetic impairment of volition (i.e., drug- induced impairment of motivation or akinetic mutism); inability to link with self (disembodied states, often linked to inhibition of the insula); drug- induced malfunction of various midbrain (antidopaminergic catatonia/ Parkinsonian); and cholinergic brainstem systems required for purposeful motor activity (sleep paralysis).

With the aforementioned provisos, a number of formal sedation scoring scales have been developed, which are sometimes used in anesthesiology research to quantify anesthetic effects and recovery from anesthesia. The scales include the Glasgow Coma Scale,<sup>8</sup> the Observers Assessment of Alertness/Sedation Scale,<sup>9</sup> the Ramsay Sedation Scale,<sup>10</sup> and the Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale.<sup>11</sup> The scales generally assess responses to verbal and noxious stimuli and sometimes have a component for scoring agitation.

### Brainstem Reflexes

Induction of general anesthesia is associated with loss of response to verbal command and, concomitantly, loss of vestibular–oculocephalic and corneal reflexes. These are signs of dysfunction in regions of the upper pons and midbrain that control cranial nerves III, IV, V, and VI. Loosely based on clinical observations of the coincidence of loss of reflexes and responsiveness to verbal command—and the proximity of these nuclei with important brainstem arousal centers—it is suggested that loss of these reflexes are indicators of unconsciousness. However, large- scale epidemiological studies to confirm the reliability of these observations are lacking. Nevertheless, it is important for the clinician to realize that disrupted brainstem mechanisms underlie the atonia and disturbed respiratory patterns that are commonly induced by anesthetic doses of hypnotic drugs. With increasing dose of anesthesia there is inhibition of progressively more caudal pontomedullary regions. Thus, gag and cough reflexes (cranial nerves IX and X) become impaired, and apnea supervenes, at higher doses of anesthesia than those required for loss of responsiveness to verbal command.

### The Isolated Forearm Technique

The isolated forearm technique (IFT) was first described by Tunstall in 1977<sup>12</sup> for use during cesarean section, which carries a higher risk of intraoperative awareness. A tourniquet or blood pressure cuff is placed on the patient’s arm prior to administration of neuromuscular blocking drugs, so that the isolated limb remains neuromuscularly intact. This allows detection of wakeful episodes, which are indicated with appropriate motor responses to commands such as “squeeze my hand.” Brainstem or spinal cord level somatic reflexes, perhaps reflecting inadequate antinociception, may also be unmasked. Although intuitively appealing for clinical use, and arguably the gold standard in awareness monitoring, the IFT has largely remained a research tool. Nevertheless, there is no reason why clinicians shouldn’t employ the technique, and directions are given in Box 36.2.

### Autonomic Nervous System Signs, Measures, and Indices

During anesthesia, it is almost universal practice to titrate analgesic and hypnotic drugs to reduce overactive sympathetic autonomic nervous system responses to noxious stimuli. Unfortunately, autonomic responses are not specific to noxious signaling, and have many other potential intraoperative causes. Intraoperative tachycardia and hypertension are largely mediated by hypothalamic and brainstem mechanisms, and so are a window on function in these parts of the brain. These autonomic responses clearly play a significant role in important adverse perioperative outcomes, such as myocardial ischemia and strokes, and possibly have other long- term consequences such as impairment of immune responses, hypercoagulability, and the development of chronic pain. In the absence of surgical noxious input, anesthesia causes a reduction in sympathetic nervous system outflow and a reduction in parasympathetic activity.<sup>16</sup> This manifests as reduced systemic vascular resistance and hypotension. The reduction in parasympathetic activity decreases salivation and tearing. Most anesthetic agents have direct vasodilatory and cardiovascular depressant effects, which can activate compensatory autonomic responses to varying degrees. With noxious stimulation, a hypertensive and tachycardic sympathetic nervous system response is observed, although vagal responses are not uncommon.

The pupils are under parasympathetic and sympathetic nervous system control in the awake state. During anesthesia, pupils are typically small with a maintained but reduced light reflex,<sup>17</sup> which reduces further with increasing doses of anesthesia. With noxious stimulation, pupillary dilation occurs, a reflex which persists during general anesthesia.<sup>18</sup> In the awake state, reflex dilation is mediated by the sympathetic nervous system; however, during anesthesia it is unchanged by peripheral sympathetic blockade with dapiprazole,<sup>19</sup> suggesting the reflex is mediated by inhibition of the Edinger–Westphal nucleus.

### Nociceptive Indices

A number of indices have been developed to quantify autonomic nervous system activation, with the hope that these can guide analgesic drug delivery during anesthesia and hence provide better postoperative analgesia. The exact clinical efficacy and role of these indices has yet to be determined.<sup>20</sup> Heart rate variability refers to the variation of the interval between heart beats and reflects the balance between parasympathetic and sympathetic nervous system activity. Higher variability is associated with increased parasympathetic nervous system activity, whereas lower variability is associated with sympathetic nervous system predominance. Frequency analysis can differentiate between high-frequency (0.2–0.4 Hz) and low-frequency (0.04–0.15 Hz) components of heart rate variability.<sup>21</sup> High- frequency variability is thought to specifically represent changes in the parasympathetic nervous system and is linked to respiration, whereas low- frequency variability is reflective of both divisions. The high- frequency component of heart rate variability is one of the components of the commercially available analgesia–nociception index.<sup>22</sup> The “surgical pleth index” has also been developed as a measure of intraoperative nociception.<sup>23</sup> This algorithm analyzes the plethysmographic pulse wave to quantify sympathetically mediated vasoconstriction. Galvanic skin responses<sup>24</sup> have also been used as a marker of nociception- related sympathetic nervous system activity, which causes microfluctuations in sweat gland filling.

## Electroencephalography

The greatest advance in monitoring the effects of anesthesia on the brain has been the advent of EEG monitoring.

The EEG provides a window to the effects of anesthesia on the cortex (and thalamus), noninvasively capturing the dynamics of electrical activity with changing doses of different agents. However, to correctly interpret the significance of the EEG, it is essential that we understand in some detail how it is produced and displayed, and how it is affected by anesthesia.

### WHERE DOES THE EEG SIGNAL ORIGINATE?

The EEG measures population- level neuronal activity (as voltage potential, in microvolts (μV)) from electrodes placed on the scalp. Transmembrane ion flows are the mechanism by which action potentials propagate rapidly along axons, but also underlie the longer-lasting response of the postsynaptic membrane.<sup>25</sup> The ion flows create electrical potentials and hence currents in the extracellular medium, the average of which is the fundamental activity contributing to the EEG,<sup>25,26</sup> although nonsynaptic neural sources and glial cells can also contribute.<sup>25,27</sup>

Large pyramidal neurons in the cortex have multiple aligned dendritic processes that are oriented perpendicular to the scalp in the gyri. Tens of thousands of synapses attach along each of these dendritic lengths. For voltage potentials to be detected at the scalp, individual neuronal voltages need to summate; hence, the scalp EEG is detecting the synchronous activity of a large number of synapses in networks of many neurons.<sup>28</sup> The signal from the cortex must pass through cerebrospinal fluid, the skull and scalp, and is thus a diminished and smeared measure of cortical activity. An average- sized EEG electrode of around 1 cm<sup>2</sup> will detect a voltage potential from an area of cortex approximately 6 cm<sup>2</sup> in size,<sup>26</sup> meaning that the EEG signal has an excellent temporal resolution (in milliseconds), but poor spatial resolution.

Although the EEG detects signals from the cortex, it is important to note that cortical activity itself is modulated by subcortical structures, such as the thalamus<sup>29</sup> and the arousal- promoting structures located in the brainstem,<sup>30</sup> so the EEG can be considered to be an indirect marker of activity in these deeper structures. Also, strong electrical activity from more distant (“far field”) brain sources (tens of centimeters) can sometimes directly contribute to the EEG signal (volume conduction<sup>31</sup> ).

The signal from each active electrode and the reference electrode is differentially amplified by the EEG recording system, and the amplified signal is passed through a filter and then digitized via an analog-to-digital converter before being displayed on the monitor. The choice of electrode placement can have significant effects on any resultant EEG analysis.

### ANESTHETIC DRUG EFFECTS ON THE FRONTAL EEG

Different classes of drugs have widely varying effects on the EEG, mostly in proportion to how sedative the drug is. Anesthetic adjunct drugs that have effects on brainstem arousal systems—such as antimuscarinic drugs like hyoscine—occasionally cause quite marked intra- and perioperative EEG changes,<sup>33</sup> but in this section we concentrate on more generic effects of agents that act (at least partially) via gamma- aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors. Because adequate monitoring of the EEG requires visualization of the raw signal and the spectrogram, we describe each drug effect in terms of its characteristic spectrogram signature. We also concentrate on the simple single frontal channel, which is the one that clinicians will commonly see in their day- to- day practice. The degree to which anesthetic agents alter derived EEG indices of hypnotic depth can usually be deduced from knowledge of these underlying spectral changes.

### Induction

When general anesthesia is induced in a patient using a GABA- ergic anesthetic (such as propofol or volatile agents) and they transition from an awake state to one that allows surgery, an EEG signal from frontal areas shows characteristic amplitude and frequency changes. During the awake state, the EEG is characterized by small-amplitude and highfrequency activity<sup>33,34</sup> (∼5 to 10 μV). The EEG is also often interspersed with artifactual activity originating from eye blinks, head movement, and muscle activity (see section entitled Noise and Artifacts in the EEG).

At anesthetic doses sufficient to induce mild sedation, oscillations in the beta frequency range (roughly 12–30 Hz) begin to occur – the “beta buzz.” Table 36.1 shows the historical names of each frequency band. This activity in the beta range is not normally characterized by a tightly constrained oscillation, but is spread over a broader frequency band ranging from about 15 to 25 Hz. It is often an indication of an excitatory phase. When responsiveness is lost, the alpha oscillation present at occipital areas when eyes are closed disappears, and activity in the alpha frequency range (8–12 Hz) becomes visible in frontal areas – the socalled “anteriorization” phenomenon.<sup>35,36</sup>

### Maintenance

**The “Alpha- Delta” Pattern.** At doses sufficient for surgery there is a “red shift” to a predominance of slow frequencies in the EEG spectrum. There is a marked decrease in beta and gamma power, and the EEG signal is dominated by oscillations in the slow- wave (0.1–2 Hz) or delta frequency bands (0.5–4 Hz), usually together with an oscillation in the alpha range – the “alpha- delta” pattern. Fig. 36.5 shows the spectrogram of a typical patient undergoing GABA- ergic anesthesia. The alpha- delta pattern is clearly distinguished by high power (red horizontal bands) in the delta and alpha frequency bands that remain until the emergence process begins at around 2.2 hours. The details of the spectrum from the middle of the maintenance period are shown in Fig. 36.4.

It is difficult to overstate the importance of the alpha- delta pattern as the primary target for the titration of anesthesia

in almost all cases. This pattern is easily recognized in the spectrogram, has some similarities with natural sleep patterns, and is the signature of appropriate thalamocortical hyperpolarization. Its appearance usually correlates with loss of responsiveness<sup>36</sup> and suppression of cortical responses to noxious stimuli, and is a good indicator that the patient has a resilient brain<sup>40</sup> which is unlikely to suffer postoperative cognitive disturbances.<sup>41</sup> While representing an “ideal” state, it must be recognized that this alpha-delta pattern does not completely preclude connected consciousness,<sup>42,43</sup> and whether it guarantees amnesia also remains to be elucidated.

The EEG during maintenance is also characterized by an underlying non- oscillatory broadband background, where slower waves have larger amplitudes and faster waves smaller amplitudes. This is shown by the straight black regression line in Fig. 36.4. The decay rate (or negative slope in the power spectrum) of this aperiodic component of the EEG is sensitive to drug dosage.<sup>44</sup> It is difficult to visually detect in the raw EEG, but can be easily seen in a power spectrum.

**EEG Effects of Noxious Stimulation During Anesthesia.** The reported EEG responses to noxious stimulation during anesthesia are quite variable and poorly understood.<sup>52</sup> In some instances, there is a shift to higher-frequency desynchronized activity, consistent with the sort of cortical arousal seen at the end of a case that typically precedes awakening. Both increases and decreases<sup>53,54</sup> in alpha band activity—or more specifically the alpha oscillation—have been reported. There are also numerous reports of “arousal” to noxious stimuli, marked by increased delta,<sup>55,56</sup> abrupt onset of burst suppression, and dips in processed EEG index values. The variety of these responses possibly reflects

variations in pre- stimulus patient state, the types of stimuli, and the anesthetic agents and adjuncts administered. The clinical significance of nociceptive- induced disturbance of the EEG is not entirely clear. Obviously, a shift in EEG power toward a wakeful pattern would suggest that the anesthetic delivery is inadequate to maintain unconsciousness, and therefore should be increased. Other EEG changes certainly indicate that the anesthetic suppression of surgical stimuli is incomplete, but it is unknown whether these result in long- term adverse effects for the patient.

**Transition Periods During Maintenance Anesthesia.** Knowing the optimal EEG pattern for maintenance anesthesia allows clinicians to keep track of what is happening in the brain when anesthetic dosing is difficult to estimate, such as transition periods when end- tidal volatile readings are often unreliable. Such transitions include when an anesthetized patient is transferred from the induction room to the operating room, or when a patient undergoing cardiac surgery is weaned from the heart–lung bypass machine back to the anesthetic ventilator.<sup>57</sup> In a retrospective analysis of a prospective cardiac study, we found that EEG patterns very similar to those seen during emergence were occurring in around 5% of patients during ongoing surgery, mainly around the time of being transferred from the heart–lung bypass machine back to the ventilator.<sup>57</sup>

### Emergence

During the emergence period, the EEG changes represent the process of the return to, or reconstitution of,<sup>58,59</sup> connected consciousness. Brain monitoring during this period may give clinicians valuable hints as to the probable state of their patient after they regain responsiveness. The classic spectral EEG changes during emergence have been documented by a number of groups,<sup>33,36,60–62</sup> and show generally converging results. Over the emergence period, power in the slow- delta and alpha bands decreases and the peak frequency of the alpha oscillation increases before alpha power dissipates. During late emergence, there is commonly a significant period where delta and alpha power is absent, but the patient is unresponsive. At this stage they are in a state of disconnected consciousness (often dreaming<sup>32</sup> ). In contrast, around a third of patients will suddenly lose high delta power and transition directly to responsiveness.<sup>61</sup> Immediately on return of responsiveness, alpha activity reoccurs at occipital areas, if patients have their eyes closed.<sup>63</sup> During emergence, the EEG also shows an increase in beta power and thus a shift to a more uniform underlying power distribution (a decrease in slope of the power spectrum).

**Neural Inertia During Emergence.** Neuropharmacological responses as estimated by EEG or behavioral state<sup>64–66</sup> often differ between the induction and emergence. Patients need higher concentrations of hypnotic drugs to become unresponsive at induction than the concentrations at which they emerge – and this difference cannot be fully accounted for by pharmacokinetic effect- site lag. This hysteresis effect is so- called “neural inertia,” which is defined as the resistance to changes in state of the brain or of consciousness.<sup>67–70</sup> The inability to directly measure effect- site concentrations in humans has led to some debate as to

the role of pharmacokinetic estimates in determining the incidence of neural inertia,<sup>71–73</sup> but pharmacodynamic responses consistently show differences between the induction and emergence.<sup>61,64</sup> It may arise from generic network effects,<sup>74–77</sup> but there has also been a large focus on the role of ascending arousal system malfunction.<sup>67,69,78</sup> In essence, the active brainstem arousal systems—as seen in the young and those with analeptic drugs—tend to reduce hysteresis, while suppression of brainstem arousal systems (for example as seen in the elderly, and with dexmedetomidine) will enhance hysteresis.<sup>61</sup> Although it might seem that neural inertia is an arcane pharmacokinetic- dynamic concept, this resistance to change has profound consequences in the practical delivery of anesthesia. The clinician should be aware that the simple sigmoid drug dose–response curve does not always pertain. Sometimes the patient does not change their brain state as expected in response to drug delivery, and sometimes they will maintain their existing brain state even after the hypnotic drug has been withdrawn. Neural inertia explains the classic case of an unresponsive patient at the end of the operation whose end- tidal sevoflurane has been 0.1% for 20 minutes, and who has persistently large delta waves, and then suddenly sits up and expels their laryngeal mask airway.

**Muscle Activity (EMG).** It is also worth noting that scalp electromyographic (EMG) activity is often present during the induction and emergence periods. There is often a covert increase in muscle tone that cannot be observed clinically. EMG can be identified on a spectrogram as an additive increase in power (color) over a wide range of frequencies (a broadband effect, see Fig. 36.4), and in the raw EEG as an increase in small- amplitude, high- frequency waveforms, making the EEG trace appear “fuzzy.” EMG mostly shows up later in the emergence process, often induced by endotracheal tube irritation.<sup>79</sup> Any dissociation between genuine EEG markers (such as an ongoing alphadelta waveform) and additive EMG can be easily seen in the spectrogram. During maintenance of anesthesia these same principles regarding EMG also apply. However, increased EMG should not be considered as “noise.” It is essentially giving the clinician a signal that the patient is grimacing, and probably would benefit from increased anesthesia or analgesia delivery.

### Specific Non- GABA- ergic Drug Effects on the EEG

**Nitrous Oxide.** Nitrous oxide has completely different actions on the EEG to those of the aforementioned GABAergic drugs, which makes its contribution to anesthesia difficult to detect with most indices.<sup>83</sup> When given alone at atmospheric pressure it usually does not result in full unconsciousness, so the EEG changes are minimal, inconsistent, and rather subtle. However large abrupt increases or decreases in nitrous oxide concentration have been shown to cause large short- lived delta waves and loss of alpha waves.<sup>84</sup> This pattern can occur with an abrupt withdrawal of nitrous oxide in the wakeful patient,<sup>85</sup> but also when nitrous oxide is suddenly introduced on top of etherbased anesthesia.<sup>86</sup> It has been suggested that nitrous oxide–induced differential _N_ - methyl- d- asparate (NMDA) blockade in some brainstem nuclei is the origin of this phenomenon.

**Xenon.** Xenon is not commonly used in clinical practice, but has a distinctive EEG signature which is dominated by delta power, and with reductions in all other frequency bands.<sup>87</sup>

**Ketamine.** The EEG effects of ketamine are complex, and depend on the dose given, and whether the drug is used alone or in conjunction with other anesthetic drugs. In a sub- hypnotic dose ketamine causes loss of alpha power and some increased theta and gamma activity,<sup>88</sup> but if given in doses sufficient to induce loss of responsiveness it also produces huge slow waves<sup>89,90</sup> alternating with gamma bursts. In contemporary practice, ketamine is more commonly administered in smaller boluses (25–50 mg) or infusions to supplement volatile or propofol anesthesia, with the purpose of augmenting analgesia. ### The Effects of Age and Preexisting Neurological Disease on EEG Monitoring

The process of aging induces a number of physiological brain changes that can influence the EEG signal, such as reduced cortical thickness, reduced number of dendritic spines on pyramidal neurons, reduced capacity for neural synchrony, and increased skull impedance and levels of neural noise.<sup>94–96</sup> In general, increasing age is associated with decreases in EEG amplitudes at all frequencies often giving an indeterminate EEG pattern.<sup>94</sup> Aging also flattens the slope of the power spectrum<sup>95</sup> by about 20%, so that there is a relative dominance of higher frequencies in both the anesthetized and awake EEG.<sup>96</sup> This decrease in power spectrum slope is associated with an increase in signal complexity,<sup>95</sup> so that intraoperative entropy measures and other indices tend to be higher in the elderly at the point of loss of behavioral response, and for a given age- adjusted hypnotic dose – the so- called “paradox of age.”<sup>97</sup> These effects can also be seen with increasing comorbidity load,<sup>98</sup> giving rise to the concept of “functional brain age” that might be distinct from chronological age.<sup>99</sup> The effects of aging on the frontal alpha oscillation are less clear. While peak alpha frequencies are known to slow with age,<sup>94,100</sup> there are inconsistent results as to age- related changes in relative alpha power.<sup>94,95,100</sup> The likelihood of burst suppression increases with age,<sup>94</sup> and bursts have smaller amplitudes than in younger patients.<sup>101</sup> Thus, optimal delivery of anesthesia in elderly patients is often less well defined and there is more variability between patients. Clinicians should be aware that the indices are less reliable in the elderly, who may have an acceptable level of

anesthesia even at relatively high index values. A plausible anesthesia titration strategy is to increase hypnotics until there are significant slow or delta waves seen in the EEG signal (or spectrogram). If burst suppression supervenes, it would be possible to reduce hypnotic drugs until the burst suppression ceases. Older patients are more likely to show abrupt changes in EEG state during the emergence period, which is an EEG manifestation of the neural inertia concept.<sup>61</sup>

The EEGs of children above about 12 months old under anesthesia are broadly similar to those of young adults, except that they are usually of much higher amplitude. Those of neonates and younger infants are markedly different, and the differences between anesthetized and unanesthetized states are not easily discerned. Depending on gestational age, neonates may show “tracé alternant,” a pattern of low-amplitude activity interspersed with bursts. During anesthesia the EEG is dominated by slow- delta waves, and they do not really develop characteristic anterior alpha rhythms until about 4 months of age.<sup>102,103</sup> There are disappointingly few formal investigations as to how preexisting neurological conditions might alter intraoperative EEG monitoring and drug titration. Whether there is minimal effect is unknown. Certainly, if the cortex underlying the EEG electrode is abnormal, ischemic, or scarred, there will be change in amplitude, and often increased slow waves present.

### TECHNICAL CONSIDERATIONS

### Scaling of the EEG Display

In all three ways of viewing the EEG (raw, spectrum, spectrogram), the scale chosen is vitally important for correct interpretation. In the raw EEG, if the y- axis μV/division scale is too large (e.g., set at 200 μV per 1 cm division) an alpha- delta waveform with peak amplitudes of around 40 μV will appear visually small, and could be mistaken for a low- amplitude awake EEG, or even an isoelectric trace. Likewise, if the μV/division scale is too small (e.g., set at 5 μV per division for a young adult), the same waveform would visually resemble a chaos of near vertical lines, and could be falsely interpreted as noise.

Likewise, most monitors that display a spectrogram have a few basic color scaling options for EEG power. Here, the general principle holds that for younger patients, who have higher-amplitude EEGs, the power value of the maximal color in the scale (in μV<sup>2</sup> or dB) will need to be set higher; elderly patients, in contrast, have smaller EEG amplitudes, and the power value of the maximal color will need to be set lower to see clear detail in the spectrogram.

In general, impedance values of the skin to EEG electrode connection (which are displayed in all monitors) must be low, usually below 5 or 8 kΩ, and need to be at similar values at all electrodes to ensure a reliable EEG signal. ### CLINICAL APPLICATION OF EEG MONITORING

### EEG- Based Indices

In the 1970s and 1980s several indices were designed to capture the anesthesia- induced EEG shift to low frequencies as a pseudo- continuous unidimensional scale. An early example was the spectral edge frequency, which tracks anesthesia quite well, but is sensitive to eye movement artifacts when the patient is awake. Middle latency auditory evoked potentials were also shown to diminish

with increasing anesthetic depth, and predict movement responses during inhalational anesthesia; however, simpler EEG- based methods have gained ascendency. The advent of the BIS in the early 1990s made routine EEG monitoring possible in the operating room. This index—and more importantly, the hardware that implements it—was a big advance in operating room monitoring, because it was relatively robust to noise and showed reasonable correlation with commonly used GABA- ergic anesthetic drugs. It could also reliably detect burst suppression and detect and reject artifacts. A large number of company- associated and independent trials have been done to correlate the BIS values with various clinical signs of anesthesia (lack of movement, unconsciousness, and amnesia<sup>104</sup> ) and with different drugs and surgical situations<sup>105–107</sup> . The results show mixed correlations with the BIS (as described in the rest of this section). Since the turn of the millennium, a number of competitors have entered the market.<sup>108,109</sup> These indices have played an important historical role in introducing anesthesia providers to the EEG. However, it is our contention that there is much more useful clinical information about the state of the brain contained in the EEG signal than can be captured by a single number. EEG indices are analogous to obtaining just the heart rate (and no other information) from the ECG signal. With this caveat in mind, it is useful to consider some of the philosophy behind, and results obtained from, the use of EEG indices.

**Derivation and Design of EEG Indices.** The ideal index would measure some aspect of brain function that is an important part of the mechanisms by which consciousness is maintained. Ideally the output from such indices would simply be “the probability of the patient being conscious.” Because of our fragmentary existing knowledge of the mechanisms of consciousness, this is not possible at present.

Most indices have been derived by empirical observations of transitions into and out of anesthesia, and specialist opinion, using statistical algorithms to maximize prediction of consciousness vs. unconsciousness. Of note, correlation of EEG indices with future response to surgical incision is poor. The indices typically incorporate several EEG components with different weightings, such as power values from different frequency bands, burst suppression, coherence, and autoregression parameters, but the details usually remain a proprietary secret. The study populations used to generate the data on which the indices are based are typically young and relatively healthy, as such studies usually have relatively tight inclusion and exclusion criteria. The reader should be cognizant that application to wider surgical populations is contingent on assumptions that population averages are relevant to the individual patient.

**Validation of Indices.** There is substantial, conflictriddled literature on the value of EEG indices in anesthesia, so it is germane to step back and consider some of the significant methodological difficulties involved in these studies. The ability of indices to correctly diagnose unintended discrete episodes of intraoperative awareness subtly contrasts with the ability of indices to correlate with a broad spread of increasing anesthetic drug effect- site concentrations, or the corresponding sedative behavioral consequences, across a full range from the normal alert state to anesthetic overdose. The statistical assessment of “performance” requires a classification of individuals as either conscious or unconscious, which clashes with both the concept of graded consciousness and the pseudo- quantitative nature of index values. For an index to be diagnostically useful there must be a large difference between index values of those individuals who are conscious and those who are unconscious. With a “perfect” index there would be no overlap (i.e., complete distinction), whereas a useless index will have an extensive overlap in values.

**Volunteer and Early Clinical Studies.** Volunteer studies typically show consistent EEG changes with increasing concentrations of anesthetic agents. The correlations between EEG indices and behavioral state are usually extremely high in carefully controlled unstimulated volunteer or healthy

patient conditions with slowly changing anesthetic doses; typically with predictive values >0.9,<sup>110,111</sup> although there are often notable differences between induction and emergence from anesthesia. This is important because most unplanned awareness is likely to be an unexpected emergence rather than primary failure of anesthesia from the outset. As studies start to more resemble clinical practice (Table 36.2), with heterogeneous populations and the incorporation of anesthetic adjuncts and surgery, weaker correlations between EEG features and consciousness level are observed – usually with predictive values between 0.7 and 0.9.<sup>105,112,113</sup> In routine clinical practice there is even more variation in agents, adjuncts, and stimulation, and even weaker correlation between EEG and behavior. The problem being that a BIS of 50 in one patient does not necessarily equate with a BIS of 50 in another, and different individuals will become aware at BIS values varying from 40 to 90.

### “Diagnosing Consciousness” – EEG Index Performance Against IFT Responses

Studies using the IFT have revealed significant deficiencies in the ability of EEG indices to differentiate between the IFT responsive state (connected consciousness) and unresponsiveness, typically showing predictive values around 0.7, and sensitivities and specificities between 40% and 85%.<sup>105,117–119</sup> Additionally, it has been observed that even the classic alpha- delta pattern during GABA- ergic anesthesia does not preclude IFT responses,<sup>42</sup> suggesting that this is not simply a problem of algorithmic failing or noise that could be overcome by further index refinement.

Another major limitation, detected in an elegant study by Schuller,<sup>120</sup> is that the loss of EMG activity caused by administration of neuromuscular blockade is sufficient to drop BIS values significantly in awake subjects (in many cases to within the target range for general anesthesia) – a truly unacceptable “false-negative” scenario. It should be noted, though, that even cursory visual inspection of the raw EEGs or spectrograms in this study immediately demonstrates lack of alpha- delta pattern, and hence correctly diagnoses the covert state of wakefulness.

## Clinical Outcome Research

### AWARENESS WITH RECALL

AWR is one of the most feared complications of anesthesia and was arguably the main driver of the development of EEG monitoring. Despite the methodological difficulties elaborated on in the previous section, a lot of epidemiological research has been done in this area. A recent meta- analysis<sup>125</sup> has summarized the clinical status of the

effectiveness of EEG monitors, specifically the BIS monitor, which is by far the most clinically studied of the available EEG monitors. After including 52 studies with 41,331 participants, the authors concluded that there is evidence that the use of brain monitoring more than halves the incidence of AWR when compared with clinical monitoring (odds ratio = 0.36, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.21 to 0.60; 27 studies; 9765 participants). When the BIS was used, the incidence of intraoperative awareness was three per 1000 compared to nine per 1000 when anesthesia was guided by clinical signs, but no significant difference when compared with end- tidal anesthetic agent monitoring. The review graded the level of evidence as low certainty due to presumed imprecision of effect estimates because of the infrequency of AWR.

### IS ‘DEEP’ ANESTHESIA HARMFUL?

While the main driver of routine EEG monitoring was the prevention of inadequate anesthesia, attention has also turned to the potential role of EEG monitoring in preventing excessive anesthesia. EEG guidance certainly can reduce total drug consumption and facilitate faster recovery from general anesthesia, but whether it can prevent more serious adverse outcomes remains uncertain. The question of the role of general anesthesia in postoperative cognition is covered in more detail in Chapter 78, but there is a considerable body of evidence suggesting anesthetic agents cause neuronal apoptosis,<sup>128</sup> which could be compounded by surgical stressors. Developing and aging brains are deemed most at risk of cognitive and behavioral sequelae; however, compelling evidence of poor clinical outcomes is lacking. In older populations, deep anesthesia, defined by the burst suppression pattern or low processed EEG index values, has been associated with postoperative delirium and neurocognitive decline. It is unclear whether this is a case of the EEG during anesthesia revealing more sensitive brains, or if relative overdose of anesthesia worsens outcomes.<sup>129</sup> Clinical studies on the effects of anesthesia on developing brains have also been inconclusive, but suggest only minor, if any, harmful global neurological effects of anesthesia.

Aside from effects on the central nervous system, general anesthetics have detrimental effects on most bodily systems. The cardiovascular effects of anesthesia include myocardial depression and reduced cardiac output, decreased sympathetic tone and vasodilation, and subsequent hypotension, redistribution of blood flow, and impaired perfusion of important organs. Anesthetic agents also have immune effects and have been implicated in postoperative infection and cancer recurrence.<sup>130</sup> Observational data consistently demonstrate an association between deeper anesthesia (defined by EEG parameters) and poorer clinical outcomes, although whether this is due to confounding by factors such as age and comorbidity- induced sensitivity to anesthesia or

is a true causal effect remains to be established.<sup>131</sup> The Balanced Anaesthesia study<sup>132</sup> failed to demonstrate a difference in mortality between patients randomized to deep or light BIS values.

### OTHER PUTATIVE FRONTAL EEG MEASURES OF ANESTHETIC EFFECT

In recent years there has been an explosion of new ideas about understanding the mechanisms of anesthesia. While these have not become commercialized into routine practice yet, the reader should be aware of their existence and rationales. These include phase amplitude coupling,<sup>133</sup> which can differentiate between states of sedation and deep anesthesia<sup>134</sup> ; slow- wave activity saturation, which has been linked to functional brain imaging evidence of loss of thalamic and cortical activation to auditory and noxious stimuli<sup>61,135</sup> ; and Lempel–Ziv complexity, which measures the diversity of patterns present in the signal.<sup>136</sup>

### MULTICHANNEL EEG MONITORING OF ANESTHESIA

At present, almost all routine EEG monitoring equipment is based on one or a few frontal channels. This is largely predicated by ease of use and electrode placement. It is also convenient that most anesthetic drugs cause marked anteriorization of alpha EEG activity, thus maximizing the signal in a part of the head with the best electrode impedance. However, there is increasing evidence that the ability for brain regions to communicate and coordinate with each other is a cardinal feature of alterations in consciousness. Information flow between different brain areas underlying multiple EEG channels might be a better marker of state of consciousness than merely gathering information from a single channel. A plethora of different ways to quantify spatial coupling of brain regions have been proposed.<sup>137</sup> Many of these coupling indices show strong correlations with changes in responsiveness.<sup>36,138,139</sup> But it is as yet unproven whether these methods offer sufficient benefits that would be needed to outweigh the substantial technical difficulties associated with putting on the complex caps and getting good electrode impedance. Multichannel EEG allows us to answer the questions: “Are other, more posterior, regions of the brain more important than frontal regions as mediators of consciousness?”, and “Is it the coordination of activity between a network of far- flung brain regions that is actually the underlying mechanism necessary for consciousness?” This has been referred to as the “connectionist” paradigm.<sup>137</sup>

In principle the functional connectivity between cortical regions can be quantified by whether the EEG signals between regions have a consistent phase relationship between EEG waves, or correlations of the envelope of the wave activity, or direct estimation of flow of information. This has been termed “effective” connectivity,<sup>137</sup> and is estimated using symbolic transfer entropy,<sup>138</sup> Granger causality,<sup>140</sup> and dynamic causal modelling.<sup>141</sup> Without going into the myriad of technical details, these metrics involve communication over a variety of different time scales ranging from milliseconds to minutes. Loss of responsiveness is usually associated with some sort of decreased cortical

<!-- PDF page 19 -->

functional connectivity, which has been interpreted as the anesthesia causing loss of “integration” between different brain modules. This is most marked over slower EEG scales and when connectivity is estimated by brain imaging such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).<sup>142</sup> In particular, there seems to be quite consistent loss of frontoparietal feedback loops and isolation of posterior parietal midline structures like the precuneus (the so- called “posterior hot zone”).<sup>143,144</sup> These data have sparked a vigorous—but unresolved—debate about whether the seat of consciousness lies predominantly in the front or back of the cortex.<sup>43</sup> The resolution of this question obviously has extremely important clinical consequences if we are relying on frontal electrodes for monitoring unconsciousness under anesthesia, and may explain some of the anomalous observations.<sup>42</sup> When examining the EEG at the level of phase relationships between individual waves at higher frequencies (5–15 Hz), a different picture emerges of a profound frontal hypersynchrony after loss of responsiveness during propofol and volatile agent anesthesia.<sup>36,145</sup> Somewhat counterintuitively, both hypersynchrony and dis- integration cause impaired information flow between cortical regions. It has been suggested that the changes in alpha connectivity at loss of responsiveness are stronger than in alpha power.<sup>74,146–148</sup> It has also been claimed that alpha- connectivity measures also show greater levels of hysteresis than spectral alpha measures.<sup>74</sup>

The output from multichannel EEG monitoring is a network of electrode activity, whose complexities can be summarized using various graph- theory statistics. General anesthesia usually causes the brain’s complexity in time and space to decrease.<sup>58</sup> An awake brain needs to be flexible and agile to represent the streaming multitude of cognitive events and experiences that make up wakefulness. By constraining this diverse repertoire, anesthesia reduces consciousness by rendering most conscious content inaccessible to representation. As with functional connectivity, the details of the optimal metric of complexity are still undefined. The idea of complexity is also linked with the idea of the wakeful brain being in a state of so- called “criticality.”<sup>145</sup> A system in a state of criticality is one in which it can easily access and move between a wide repertoire of states – similar to that previously described of the wakeful brain. It is also in a state where events at a small (&lt;mm) scale events can produce changes in activity over large (>cm) scales. Loss of criticality has been shown to occur with loss of responsiveness, but whether these will translate into improvements in clinical monitoring is unknown.
