# Biologic, Natural, and Human-Induced Disasters: The Role of the Anesthesiologist

<!-- PDF page 1 -->


DANIEL W. JOHNSON, WILLIAM P. MULVOY, ELIZABETH R. SCHNAUBELT, and STEVEN J. LISCO

- **KEY POINTS** Anesthesiologists possess a combination of knowledge and procedural skills that make them critical participants during the immediate and ongoing response to large- scale disasters. To do so, they must be willing and able to modify their traditional workflow to best meet the needs of victims.

- Natural disasters such as earthquakes, hurricanes, floods, tsunamis, and tornadoes require significant resources to address the ramifications of these events; as a result, they can cause massive interruptions in the normal provision of health care.

   - Anesthesiologists who travel from resource- rich to resource- limited environments to provide rescue medical care must cope with a lack of familiarity with infectious diseases that are uncommon in resource- rich nations.

   - In addition to addressing the acute challenges experienced during these disasters, anesthesiologists and other providers who travel to disaster- ravaged areas are exposed to a variety of physical and mental stressors that impact their immediate response and can have lasting effects after the event.

   - Because of the very high early mortality and personal impact on local health care workers following many disasters, anesthesiologists who travel to countries experiencing them may provide the greatest value by temporarily replacing local health care workers lost in the disaster.

   - While hurricanes tend to have fewer immediate fatalities than earthquakes or tsunamis, the ability of hurricanes and subsequent floods to cripple the provision of medical and surgical care has numerous downstream consequences, including long- term effects on medical education and clinical care because of loss of faculty and flood damage to educational and clinical facilities.

   - When disasters induce shortages of medications and fluids, as Hurricane Maria did in 2017 and the COVID- 19 pandemic did in 2020, distribution of limited resources becomes a challenge, requiring a coordinated approach to global conservation of existing supplies and avoidance of waste.

   - Since many disasters cannot be anticipated, such as the September 11, 2001 attack on New York City, developing and updating existing prospective plans to address triage of victims and coordination of services among diverse hospital systems and trauma centers become critical to appropriate resource use and early access to life- saving care.

   - In the eight years between 2015 and 2022, over 19,000 people were shot and killed or wounded in the United States in a mass shooting.<sup>1</sup> Accordingly, all anesthesiologists must be prepared to provide care to often multiple victims in the aftermath of these heinous events.

ᑏ As acts of terrorism have accelerated in recent decades, anesthesiologists need to have knowledge and skills required to care for victims of chemical, biologic, radiologic, or nuclear (CBRN) disasters. The impact of these events will not only affect those in the immediate vicinity of the act, but also the entire health system. Anesthesiologists must be prepared to alter their traditional roles and develop appropriate triage methods to best meet the needs of all patients and the community following such an event.

- One of the highest priorities for health care workers after a CBRN event is to take precautions against becoming the next (second) victim. Appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE) is extremely important after these events and must be readily available to protect health care workers.

- During an outbreak of a highly dangerous infectious disease, anesthesiologists must understand the mechanisms of transmitting the disease and implement appropriate isolation and management strategies. For many such situations, they also serve as consultants in collaboration with infectious disease specialties in the development of optimal care pathways to minimize risk to other patients and health care workers.

- Pandemics place incredible strain on health care systems around the world. To address these challenging situations and ensure sufficient staff capable of managing the clinical issues, anesthesiologists can often utilize their broad knowledge and clinical and procedural skills to provide patient care outside of their traditional roles and environments.

## Introduction

Large- scale disasters create challenges that require communities to attempt to restore order and normalcy, while also addressing some of the specific issues created by these devastating events. For decades, national, state, and hospital disaster management plans have focused upon preparedness, response, and recovery efforts dedicated to optimizing resources and personnel necessary to respond to mass casualties stemming from natural disasters, large- scale accidents, and more recently, terrorist attacks. Health care providers have a special role to play in all such events. Anesthesiologists, because of their unique combination of skills and knowledge, are an extremely valuable part of the overall effort to optimize the health and recovery of victims of disaster. In- depth knowledge of pharmacology and physiology, expertise in resuscitation, experience in critical care medicine, and the ability to alleviate pain are some of the elements that anesthesiologists offer in addition to their skills in managing patients requiring complicated and extensive surgical procedures.

To fulfill the needs of victims and the health care systems that manage them, anesthesiologists must be prepared to modify and expand upon their traditional roles in patient care to apply their unique skill set to care for patients in a wide variety of possible scenarios. During a disaster, if a given hospital system has adequate personnel to provide intraoperative anesthesia care but inadequate physicians to triage and provide resuscitation in the emergency department or in the field, anesthesiologists can extend their roles and provide care in these and other settings, including traditional hospital environments or temporary alternative sites of care. Critical care anesthesiologists, in addition to other critical care providers, are essential personnel to address many of the issues after a disaster. However, after large- scale biologic and other disasters, most hospitals do not have sufficient critical care physicians to cover all of the critically ill or injured patients. Because of the unique skills required to care for complicated clinical issues in the operating room, other anesthesiologists can temporarily assume the role of intensivist, and play a key role in the management of patients in the intensive care unit and other environments following a disaster, such as took place during the early stages of the COVID- 19 pandemic.

While many of the principles for managing diverse disasters are similar, the causes of disaster require special knowledge and skills to adequately address the clinical, social, and other issues confronting providers. To address the specific features and their implications of biologic, natural, and other causes, this chapter is divided into four distinct

sections. Each section identifies key issues for the anesthesiologist to understand as well as the specific roles they can assume to address the challenges confronting the health care system. The four sections of the chapter are:

1.  Natural disasters

2.  Acts of terrorism

3.  Chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear warfare

4.  Epidemic and pandemic infectious outbreaks

## Section 1: Natural Disasters

Natural disasters such as earthquakes, hurricanes, floods, tsunamis, and tornadoes have the ability to cause massive disruptions in the population at large and to the provision of health care during and after the event. Prehospital care for victims as well as for other clinical needs can be completely disrupted due to damage to roads. Hospital care can be disrupted due to lack of water, oxygen, fuel, electricity, and other necessary utilities. Hospitals themselves can be damaged or destroyed by these types of events. Communication can be difficult or impossible following natural disasters due to a combination of damage to telecommunication systems with simultaneous usage overload as families and victims try to locate one another. To address specific issues related to these natural disasters and their implications, several historical examples of natural disasters will be examined in order to highlight the critical role of the anesthesiologist during the management and recovery from these devastating events.

### EARTHQUAKES

Earthquakes are capable of inducing an incredible amount of damage in a very brief period of time. While earthquakes can seriously impact resource- rich countries, resourcelimited areas of the world that suffer from poor infrastructure at baseline are particularly vulnerable to severe health care and other issues following an earthquake. The magnitude 7.0 earthquake that struck Haiti on January 12, 2010 was one of the most devastating natural disasters in modern history, and was an example of how badly a nation’s health care system can be damaged by these types of events. The death toll in Haiti is difficult to accurately calculate, but nearly all analyses place the number of deaths at more than 130,000, with 1.5 million people immediately displaced following the earthquake.<sup>2,3</sup> More than 80% of Haitian schools and more than 50% of Haitian hospitals were destroyed.

Health care professionals from around the world recognized that a resource- limited nation such as Haiti would

require an incredible amount of assistance to recover from the earthquake. Surgeons, anesthesiologists, and other health care providers from the United States and other nations traveled to Haiti in order to provide initial trauma care, and subsequently to assist in backfilling the jobs of Haitian health care workers who were lost or injured in the earthquake. Médecins Sans Frontières, or Doctors Without Borders, stated in 2013 that its response to the Haitian earthquake was the largest relief operation in the history of the organization. While hundreds of health care workers from around the world expressed interest in assisting in the recovery from the earthquake, a major limiting factor was the inability to access the sole airport in Haiti. The United States Air Force assumed leadership of the airport and provided air traffic control until Haitian authorities could recover adequately to resume control.<sup>4</sup>

In recent years, massive natural disasters like the Haitian earthquake have been supported through the deployment of a United States Navy Hospital ship such as the USNS Comfort or USNS Mercy to replace facilities lost to the disaster and provide clinical care to those in need. These enormous oil tankers converted to hospital ships were primarily designed to deliver health care to US military personnel during wartime, yet they are more commonly deployed to provide humanitarian aid to disaster- ravaged areas of the world. In 2010, the USNS Comfort deployed to the coast of Haiti within 72 hours of the earthquake and subsequently embarked upon the largest disaster- relief operation in the history of these floating hospitals. More than 850 patients received care on the ship during the weeks following the earthquake, including 237 children and one premature newborn.<sup>5</sup> The most common condition treated on the ship was musculoskeletal extremity injury, comprising about 40% of the overall reasons for admission.<sup>6</sup> The average length of stay was 8 days and surgery was performed 843 times on 454 patients over the course of five weeks. 58 of the operations were amputations.<sup>7</sup> While these facilities are generally staffed by military physicians, during disasters, anesthesiologists and other specialists from across the United States volunteered to provide care on the USNS Comfort, some as new volunteers and some with prior experience in military and disaster relief (Figs. 64.1 and 64.2).

Provision of medical and surgical care aboard a “floating hospital” was similar to care provided in other settings, essentially functioning as a relatively normal and well- equipped facility; the task of providing care on the island of Haiti itself, however, required very different approaches. Nearly all medical facilities in Port-au-Prince were either damaged or destroyed by the earthquake, forcing volunteer health care teams to work in makeshift clinics and hospitals within tent facilities throughout the city. One of the largest such facilities was based out of the United Nations compound at the airport.<sup>8</sup> In the initial days after the earthquake, surgical care was very limited because of a lack of supplemental oxygen, unsterile conditions, and a lack of resources to provide anesthesia. Amputations were performed under local anesthesia until a team of anesthesiologists skilled in single- shot block techniques arrived.<sup>7</sup> Following their arrival, these anesthesiologists were able to facilitate the performance of 1000 operations, including major orthopedic surgery. They were also instrumental in the postoperative care and analgesia of the surgical patients (Fig. 64.3).

Anesthesiologists who travel from resource- rich to resource- limited environments to provide rescue medical care must be prepared to address a number of issues not directly related to the disaster, but that have major impact on how care is delivered and the expertise that is required. For example, the physicians must contend with infectious diseases that have been largely eradicated from resourcerich nations, with which they often have had limited experience. During the Haitian earthquake recovery, this issue arose when anesthesiologists encountered patients suffering from tetanus. The Haitian population is at an elevated risk to develop tetanus following contamination of wounds because of the relatively low rates of vaccination compared with resource- rich nations. Caused by the anaerobic bacterium _Clostridium tetani_ , tetanus causes severe neck rigidity, trismus, and chest wall immobility. Some of the symptoms associated with tetanus create difficulty for anesthesiologists during airway management and maintenance of ventilation. Anesthesiologists who provided care for surgical patients on the USNS Comfort reported two cases of tetanus which impacted their anesthetic care.<sup>9</sup> In one case, the patient was noted to have a small mouth opening as a result of trismus. Following induction of general anesthesia with inhaled sevoflurane, the patient’s total body rigidity markedly improved. Unfortunately, postoperatively the patient suffered significant muscle weakness, retention of pulmonary secretions, and ultimately developed pneumonia contributing to death. In another case, the patient required repeated general anesthetics, consisting of inhaled sevoflurane without neuromuscular blocking drugs, and recovered to discharge. Based on these experiences, the anesthesiologists involved in the care of these tetanus victims noted that the nasal airway remains largely unaffected by tetanus, allowing mask ventilation even in the setting of trismus. They also emphasized the importance of conserving precious pharmaceuticals like neuromuscular blocking drugs for use in the most critical clinical situations when one is practicing medicine in a severely resource- limited environment. By applying their broad expertise in pathophysiology and pharmacology, these anesthesiologists were able to safely and effectively deliver surgical anesthesia to high- risk patients with minimal medication administration. The conservation of neuromuscular blocking agents by taking advantage of the neuromuscular blocking properties of potent inhaled anesthetic agents facilitated the care of other patients while maintaining access to muscle relaxants for those patients most in need of them.

The Haitian earthquake response also highlighted another critical issue when dealing with any major disaster or care in an unfamiliar environment with limited resources – the psychological impact of the disaster and its effects on health care workers working within this challenging situation. Anesthesiologists who travel to disaster- ravaged areas are exposed to a variety of physical and mental stressors, which can have an impact on them for months or years after the event. Prolonged work hours, inadequate rest, exposure to gruesome traumatic injuries, and witnessing the prolonged pain and suffering of children are some examples of what disaster responders endure. Despite these challenges, health care providers are expected to provide optimal medical care while simultaneously providing psychological support to devastated family members. Complicating matters,

as patients’ medical and surgical care become prolonged, they develop tolerance to pain medications. This increases the challenge of achieving humane levels of analgesia. During the busy hours and days of the immediate response to a disaster, health care providers must stay focused on the call of duty, which allows them to keep personal emotions at bay. As the pace of the response slows, anesthesiologists and other health care providers are forced to deal with the physical, mental, and emotional trauma that they themselves have sustained. One might wonder why a health care provider would leave the safety of their home nation to purposely expose themselves to this kind of potential anguish. Consistent qualities appear to be present among responders to massive natural disasters: they understand that all human beings have dignity, they sympathize with people who are suffering, and they desire to be part of the healing process. After returning to the United States following humanitarian missions to Haiti, several anesthesiologists composed thoughtful essays on the care that they had provided.<sup>10</sup>

### TORNADOES

While tornadoes tend to be shorter in duration than other natural disasters, they are able to create horrendous damage within seconds to minutes. One of the most destructive storms in American history was an EF- 5 tornado that ravaged Joplin, Missouri on May 22, 2011. During the ensuing power outage, some hospitals’ backup generators failed, requiring anesthesia care and surgery to be performed by flashlight alone.<sup>11</sup> When hospitals are directly destroyed by natural disasters, as occurred at St John’s Mercy Medical Center in Joplin, great attention must be paid to restoring the health care infrastructure of the community, while continuing to provide ongoing care. Having access to electronic health records can facilitate patient care, but only if appropriate backup power is available. To ensure documentation of care and access to anesthesia records and other critical information, transition to paper records becomes essential, though may no longer be easily accessed in emergency situations. With proper disaster preparedness, including contingencies for complete losses of hospitals and clinics, normal patterns of care can be restored within a reasonable timeframe.<sup>12</sup>

Opportunistic infections are a common downstream effect from all natural disasters, including tornadoes. Following the 2011 tornado in Joplin, a cluster of patients suffered from necrotizing cutaneous mucormycosis requiring extensive treatment.<sup>13</sup> Some of these infections occur due to patients being thrown to the ground and sustaining breakage in the normal protective barrier of the skin. Understanding this risk and assessing for mucormycosis is essential to ensuring early and adequate débridement (Figs. 64.4 and 64.5).

Tsunamis tend to produce a very high ratio of mortality to injury. The high death rate is related to the massive influx of water onto the shore subjecting large numbers of people to risk of drowning or direct impact of waves and exposure to debris. The aftermath of a tsunami tends to have three phases. During phase 1, a high number of people are instantly killed. During phase 2, in the hours and days following the initial impact, care is directed toward providing life- and limb- saving care to victims of blunt trauma and water exposure.<sup>14</sup> During phase 3, the impact on the overall infrastructure as well as health care personnel affects availability to address longer-term posttrauma care as well as to provide basic medical care to the community, further complicating the area’s overall recovery.

The 2004 tsunami killed more than 230,000 people, injured tens of thousands, and was estimated to have immediately displaced more than 5 million people. One factor in its extreme lethality was the fact that December is the peak tourist season for many of the beaches of Southeast Asia. As is the case with many tsunamis, there was essentially zero warning that deadly waves were about to make landfall. The Aceh province of the Indonesian island of Sumatra was by far the hardest hit area, with waves reaching greater than 25 m in height resulting in more than 100,000 people killed. People on the beach at the time of the tsunami died immediately, while people farther inland were killed as a result of drowning or impact with massive floating debris (Fig. 64.6).

While there is essentially no way to ensure availability of health care workers during phase 1 of a tsunami, health care workers from nearby regions are often able to respond during phase 2 unless infrastructure destruction prevents mobilization to areas of greatest need. Following the 2004 tsunami, a team of 17 surgeons, 6 anesthesiologists, and other health care workers traveled as part of the Thai Red Cross Society for Humanitarian Mission from Bangkok,

Thailand to Phang- Nga, Thailand to provide care. The multidisciplinary team was led by one surgeon and one anesthesiologist. They were able to arrive within one day after the tsunami. Over the course of the subsequent 3 days, the team provided surgical care for 107 patients. The injury profile for the patients requiring medical care consisted predominantly of soft tissue wounds and bone fractures. Halothane was the only inhalational anesthetic available, highlighting the need for anesthesiologists to be nimble in their ability to utilize medications rarely used in their current practice during disaster relief.

Most cases in Phang- Nga were performed under general anesthesia following rapid sequence induction, although spinal, regional, and local anesthetic techniques were also utilized. The team noted an increased incidence of intraoperative oxygen desaturation for this group of patients compared with their experience during other relief operations. The desaturation was thought to be related to aspiration of seawater and lung contusions secondary to direct impact of waves or debris. Despite the ability of this team to use an inland hospital for the care of tsunami victims, infrastructure was sufficiently damaged to preclude obtaining preoperative or intraoperative laboratory data, limiting access to data to guide management, particularly related to administration of fluids, electrolytes, antibiotics, and blood products (Fig. 64.7).

In addition to the regional response, a massive international relief effort was launched in response to the tsunami. In January 2005, Operation Unified Assistance was started as part of the United States response to the catastrophe. Organized by Project HOPE and the United States Public Health Service, the USNS Mercy was sent from San Diego to the Aceh province in Sumatra, representing the first joint effort of military and civilian relief aboard a US Naval ship.<sup>15</sup> US responders to Indonesia were required to demonstrate up- to- date vaccination records for all typical immunizations, receive vaccines for typhoid and hepatitis A, and take prophylactic medication to protect from malaria. Additionally, negative airflow pods were available on the ship to allow for safe care of patients with active tuberculosis. Other issues that needed to be addressed included educating the providers about aspects of their care not generally considered. Prior to arrival, all staff members were briefed on the differences in culture and religion they would encounter, and the possible implications for providing care. More than 90% of Indonesian citizens are Muslim, and Islamic Law plays an important role in the day- to- day decision- making for many of the families impacted by the tsunami.

As noted previously, the impact of the tsunami on the health care workforce was also overwhelming. An estimated 60% of all health care workers in the Aceh province died or were missing following the tsunami. Thus, Operation Unified Assistance was prepared to backfill the roles of lost health care workers in addition to providing care directly related to the effects of the tsunami. While the USNS Mercy was designed for enormous overall capacity, for this operation the facility included 3 operating rooms, and 50 inpatient beds with appropriate staffing for these locations.

### HURRICANES

A hurricane is a tropical storm with maximum sustained winds of 74 mph (119 km/h) or higher. Hurricanes have the potential to disrupt the normal function of the health care system in a variety of ways. When they make landfall, the high wind speeds and excessive rainfall make any outdoor movement unsafe, thus paralyzing emergency

<!-- PDF page 7 -->

medical systems and preventing patients from seeking necessary care. With major hurricanes, extensive flooding is a common complication due to large volumes of rainfall combined with seawater entrained onto land as part of the storm surge. While these storms tend to have fewer immediate fatalities than earthquakes or tsunamis, the ability of hurricanes to cripple the provision of medical and surgical care has numerous downstream consequences. In this section, Hurricane Katrina and Hurricane Maria will be examined as examples of the destructive forces of hurricanes in general.

In August 2005, Katrina started as a storm over the Bahamas, then gained strength over Florida before reaching the Gulf of Mexico. Over the Gulf of Mexico, Katrina reached category 5 status with top wind speeds of 175 mph. At the time of landfall, the storm was considered a category 3 hurricane. It produced 8–14 inches of rain over southern Louisiana, Mississippi, and Alabama. At some sites on the Mississippi coast, the storm surge was 25–28 feet higher than normal tide level and destroyed the majority of structures in its path. In New Orleans, Louisiana, the storm surge sent water over the levees protecting the city, causing flooding in 80% of the city and the need for widespread evacuation. Hurricane Katrina remains the costliest storm in the history of the United States, at greater than $100 billion in losses, and it was the deadliest hurricane since 1928<sup>16</sup> (Fig. 64.8 and Table 64.1).

The flooding and subsequent displacement of thousands of New Orleans inhabitants following Hurricane Katrina highlight one of the key elements of major natural disasters. In addition to its impact on health care delivery and health care infrastructure, a storm of this magnitude completely disrupted medical education at the undergraduate and postgraduate levels. New Orleans is home to two major medical schools with accompanying clinical and research enterprises. Immediately following Hurricane Katrina, the academic and clinical buildings associated with Tulane University School of Medicine and Louisiana State University (LSU) School of Medicine were both badly damaged by flood waters.<sup>17</sup> Alternative sites to provide ongoing medical education and clinical experiences had to be identified quickly. Tulane University collaborated with Baylor University College of Medicine in Houston, Texas to use facilities for its medical student programs. LSU utilized facilities

Both schools were forced to find new clinical sites within Louisiana and beyond. Compounding the challenge was the closure of the damaged Veterans Affairs Medical Center in New Orleans. This degree of disruption had an impact on the medical school admissions process for both schools, yet both schools were able to enroll incoming classes very much on par with prior years. With regard to the residency match process, Tulane did experience a slight increase in the number of unmatched medical school graduates, likely owing to the reduction in residency positions available at Tulane after the hurricane.<sup>16</sup> Despite all of these formidable challenges, both Tulane and LSU were able to keep their entire medical school enterprises alive, and even improve several physical and academic aspects of their campuses in the years following Katrina. In a sense, these two universities have provided other academic centers with a road map for how to continue to thrive following the harrowing experience of an all- encompassing natural disaster. Their perseverance was essential to the future health care of patients in Louisiana and beyond.

While the medical schools’ survival was a triumph, Hurricane Katrina and subsequently Hurricane Rita had a distinctly negative effect on the field of anesthesiology in Louisiana. The two largest teaching hospitals in New Orleans closed after Katrina, and the largest of these – Charity Hospital – never reopened. These closures and other factors led to a decline in the number of anesthesiologists, as documented by a 2006 statewide survey.<sup>18</sup> Statewide residency positions in anesthesiology fell from 24 graduates per year in 2004 to 13 graduates per year in 2007. Like many states, Louisiana is dependent on new anesthesiology graduates remaining in the state to take the place of retiring physicians. Of the respondents, 37% of anesthesiologists reported that they had difficulty filling open positions within their group. In this setting, 92% reported that they had experienced an increase in the number of daily cases, and that obstetric cases for patients with no prenatal care had risen out of proportion to other cases. This phenomenon likely represents a glimpse into the impact of natural disasters on all medical specialties: the same stresses experienced by anesthesiology programs were simultaneously felt by obstetric–gynecology programs. Compared with the complete annihilation of medical infrastructure caused by the earthquake in Haiti, the plight of post- Katrina New Orleans may seem insignificant. Still, the fact that a large city within the wealthiest nation on earth can have its medical infrastructure completely disrupted for months after a storm highlights the impact of large- scale natural disasters.

### BOSTON MARATHON BOMBING

On April 15, 2013, two home- made bombs made of pressure cookers filled with pellets and nails were detonated 12 seconds apart, near the finish line of the Boston Marathon. The unusual nature of the bombs in this incident caused an unusual injury pattern: three victims were killed by the blasts, while 264 were injured, 66 of whom sustained lower extremity injuries.<sup>26</sup> The close proximity of multiple major trauma centers to the site of the bombing likely played a role in reducing the mortality rate of this event. Despite a collaborative effort among Boston trauma centers, emergency departments were overwhelmed when 78% of the patients treated at city trauma centers arrived within 90 minutes of the initial event. Of these high acuity patients, 45 were taken directly to operating rooms, 11 to intensive care units, and 12 required emergency airway management.<sup>27</sup> Of 127 patients receiving care at Boston’s trauma centers, over 100 likely had some facet of their immediate care provided by an anesthesiologist. It is the anesthesiologist’s unique combination of medical knowledge and technical skills that places them in the epicenter of terrorism disaster management.

Unlike other terror attacks in the United States, these bombs caused injuries similar to those observed in the Afghanistan and Iraq wars, caused by improvised explosive devices (IEDs). This injury pattern in wars has prompted a resurgence of interest in the use of tourniquets in the field to prevent exsanguination from major injuries of the extremities. This management strategy proved to be very helpful in addressing some of the injuries sustained after this incident.

Of the 66 patients with extremity injuries, 29 of them had life- threatening bleeding at the point of injury.<sup>23</sup> Of these 29, 27 had a tourniquet applied in an attempt to reduce bleeding. All of the 27 tourniquets were improvised, that is, they were not commercially produced tourniquets designed expressly for the purpose of arterial occlusion. The most common type used was rubber tubing wrapped around the extremity combined with a Kelley clamp. Sixtythree percent of the tourniquets were applied by non- EMS (emergency medical service) personnel, and several were not applied tightly enough to actually occlude arterial flow. Experts in battlefield trauma care have discussed the use of tourniquets after the Boston Marathon bombing and have suggested an educational campaign to teach the correct

technique for application of purpose- driven tourniquets, and suggested that all EMS providers be provided with these devices. Anesthesia providers have extensive experience with the proper application of arterial tourniquets, as a result of their widespread use during orthopedic operations. In the event of another mass casualty event resulting from IEDs, anesthesiologists, CRNAs (certified registered nurse anesthetists), anesthesia assistants, and anesthesiology residents should instruct health care workers and volunteer first aid workers on the application of tourniquets. In the likely absence of purpose- driven, commercially produced devices, emergency medicine, trauma, and anesthesia providers should inspect applied tourniquets to ensure they are applied in a manner that will occlude arterial flow.

The two perpetrators of this heinous crime were eventually apprehended and taken with severe injuries to a local hospital. One died from his injuries while the other survived, and required medical care to address his injuries. This scenario brings to light one of the challenges of working as an anesthesiologist following a terrorist attack: the physician must continue to provide the best care possible even when the patient has done tremendous harm to other human beings. In situations like this, the physician benefits from focusing on the anatomic and physiologic data at hand, while ignoring the surrounding emotional elements. Under any circumstances, all physicians must remember their oath to “do no harm.”

### HISTORY OF CHEMICAL, BIOLOGICAL, RADIOLOGICAL, AND NUCLEAR WARFARE

Chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) warfare is deeply imbedded in the history of world conflicts. The search for tactical wartime and war- fighting advantages have been documented since Ancient Babylon. Chemical weapons can be traced back to 10,000 years BCE when the San society of Southern Africa utilized natural poisons from snakes on the tips of their spears while hunting antelope. One of the first suspected uses of chemical agents in war can be found between the Persians and Romans in the year 256.<sup>29</sup> According to archeologic evidence, Persia exposed Roman soldiers to a noxious gas derived from combustion of sulfur crystals, causing death by asphyxiation.

World War I represented an uptick in the use of chemical agents as offensive weapons. Chemical agents included phosgene gas, chlorine gas, and mustard gas during the infamous trench warfare encounters from 1915 through 1918. Technology during World War I continued to

advance these chemical and biological weapons into ballistic explosive devices, which ultimately led to approximately 1.3 million casualties.<sup>30</sup> This is why many historians consider World War I as the “Chemist’s War.”

The landmark international regulation of 1925 in Geneva outlined, outlawed, and made utilization of chemical weapons a crime against humanity, which led to a near cessation of chemical and biological weapons development as history moved forward towards World War II.

Several countries have been implicated in the development and advancement of chemical and biological weapons since 1930.<sup>31</sup> A major discovery by the German scientist Gerhard Schrader in 1936 led to the invention of anticholinesterase nerve agents. The Biological Weapons Convention in 1972 put an effective, if temporary, stop to most of the worldwide research and stockpiling of chemical, biological, and nerve agents for weaponization.<sup>32</sup> Some nations continued to conduct research, develop, and produce chemical and biological weapons. The production of these agents as offensive weapons contributed to these weapons being transferred to terrorist groups because of decreased accountability for these weapons by the end of the Cold War.

The 1980s saw a shift to chemical and biological weapons as a true first- line offensive weapon. The United Nations had documented the combined uses of vesicants and nerve agents, such as mustard gas and tabun toxin, to increase the lethality of chemical and biological weapons.<sup>33,34</sup> These agents were not as lethal as expected, and they accounted for about 27,000 Iranian casualties, or about 1% of the

### Decontamination

In most situations the initial hazard is unknown, however, protecting oneself is one of the major principles to ensure on- scene and in- hospital care of the afflicted patients. Many hazards are short- lived or transient, but it should be assumed that the hazard released would be persistent and transmissible through the on- scene decontamination zones. Immediately establishing the level of personal protection required for medical staff, paramedics, and ancillary search and research personnel by the on- scene commander is vital to preventing an increased number of casualties among the medical and rescue personnel. Triage personnel and medical personnel are vital to providing immediate (1) triage, (2) life- support measures (TOXALS – toxicology advanced life support), and (3) antidote and other pharmacologic support.

### Incident Management During a CBRN Attack and PPE

In the face of a mass casualty event from a CBRN attack, panic and chaos are to be expected and systems should be put in place to focus management on the key issues to reduce risk and complications. Successful response to a CBRN attack requires command and control personnel and the medical providers to prioritize the following: personnel safety, personnel protection, command establishment, and site- wide communication. Similar to the US military, the civilian sector has command control entities in the form of local, state, and federal response systems. Once the command structure is established for the incident in question, the commander is responsible for directing and controlling all available resources.

Decontamination is a critical component for addressing the risks associated with the hazard. Establishing a decontamination zone and early life- support measures in the decontamination zone is critical.<sup>45,46</sup> A unique perspective to CBRN on- scene decontamination and resuscitation was introduced in 1996. It was uniquely termed TOXALS (Toxicology Advanced Life Support) to be used in conjunction with ACLS, ATLS, and BLS in the setting of chemical, biological, and/or radiological attacks. TOXALS expands the on- scene and in- hospital resuscitation efforts of medical personnel and ACLS, ATLS, and/or BLS can be expanded to the mnemonic ABCDDEE, which includes: airway, breathing, circulation, disability, drugs, exposure, and environment.

The first rule for CBRN casualty treatment and maintenance states, “Do not become the casualty yourself.” The incident commander or officer in charge must set the protection level to ensure maximal barrier protection and adequate PPE for all providers and first responders. The US military has adapted their personal protective equipment levels from the US Environmental Agencies’ _Hazardous Waste Operations and Emergency Response_ protocols.<sup>42</sup> During any time when intubation, exposure to bodily fluids, and/or airborne infection may occur, the minimum protection level should be Level C. In addition to this protection level in an unknown CBRN attack, all severe casualties or those severely negatively impacted by the CBRN attack should be thoroughly evaluated (Table 64.6).

## Section 4: Epidemic and Pandemic Infectious Outbreaks

In the last edition of this textbook, many of the concepts of the anesthesiologist responding to a pandemic were either on a small scale or remained in the abstract. In 2020 and 2021, anesthesiologists all over the world experienced firsthand how they must respond to an infectious pandemic. The COVID- 19 pandemic stressed hospitals, public health programs, communities, and indeed entire nations in a way that no infectious disaster had in the prior 100 years. During this period of inadequate resources, uncertainty, and fear, anesthesiologists rose to the occasion and helped to save thousands of lives. The COVID- 19 pandemic, its impact on the health care system, and lessons learned will be discussed in more detail later in this section.

Until recently, the role of anesthesiologists in infectious epidemics and pandemics, had been discussed only in the most general terms. While the life- saving application of positive pressure ventilation by Danish anesthesiologist Dr. Bjorn Ibsen saved many lives during the Copenhagen polio epidemic of 1952, the role of anesthesiologists as critical members of the management team in biologic disasters was not fully exploited until the severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) epidemic of 2003, the West African Ebola epidemic of 2014, and the COVID- 19 pandemic spanning from 2020 until present day.<sup>47,48</sup> During these worldwide events, anesthesiologists not only played critical roles as caregivers but also risked personal safety functioning in their role as specialists in airway management and critical care. Knowledge of the disease, management options, and importantly, infection control measures necessary to protect oneself, are all critical for anesthesiologists to contribute maximally and mitigate personal vulnerability during any major epidemic or pandemic.<sup>49</sup>

Health emergencies in the form of regional epidemics and global pandemics have the capacity to kill millions of people across continents and around the world.<sup>50</sup> While this number sounds excessive and perhaps even alarmist, it is in fact a conservative estimate when compared to pandemics over past centuries. Smallpox is the deadliest pandemic in human history. In the 20th century alone, estimates of deaths due to smallpox exceeded 300 million people, with 30% of all persons infected succumbing to the disease.<sup>51</sup> The causative agent, variola virus, is thought to date back possibly as far as 3000 years to the time of the Egyptian Empire. Its global

spread paralleled the advance of modern civilization, exploration, and expanding trade. Thanks to the success of worldwide vaccination, the last person to die of smallpox was in 1978 and on May 8, 1980 the 33rd World Health Assembly officially declared the world smallpox free<sup>52,53</sup> Unfortunately, unlike viruses that have a human reservoir such as smallpox, polio, and measles, and hence are targets for eradication, future pandemics are more likely to be the result of zoonotic transmission.<sup>54</sup>

### PANDEMIC INFLUENZA A

Past influenza pandemics have occurred when genetic mutations in an enzootic cycle resulted in reassortment of gene segments creating what is functionally a novel infectious agent with major changes in surface proteins, capable of sustained human- to- human transmission in a global population with little existing immunity.<sup>55</sup> Pathogenic influenza A viruses arising from this antigenic shift have been the source of influenza A pandemics for centuries. In fact, there have been no less than ten pandemic influenza A events over the past 300 years.<sup>56</sup> In the preceding century alone, there were three episodes of pandemic flu. A recent analysis of the “Spanish Flu” H1N1 pandemic of 1918–1919 estimated that at least 50 to 100 million people died worldwide. Less severe, but also with considerable mortality (approximately 1 million globally; 100,000 in the United States), were the 1957 “Asian Flu” H2N2 and the 1968 “Hong Kong Flu” H3N2.<sup>57,58</sup> Such events underscore the pandemic threat posed by influenza viruses, emphasizing the need for advanced preparation necessary to augment both the clinical and research response to future global health emergencies. In fact, given the incidence and variety of influenza viruses circulating in zoonotic populations, a virus possessing the necessary properties to cause a pandemic can emerge at any time<sup>59</sup> (Fig. 64.16).

In 2009, the world experienced the first influenza A event of the 21st century. This worldwide influenza outbreak has been described by the Centers for Disease Control as the 2009 H1N1 Pandemic. Central to disaster planning for pandemic flu is an understanding of the strategies to minimize transmission of influenza between patients and health care workers. Despite vast clinical experience, there is still debate over how influenza is transmitted. Possible modes of transmission included in past discussions are airborne, droplet, and contact transmission. Each requires a different infection control approach and almost certainly different personal protective equipment (PPE). Recent analysis of multiple human and animal studies concluded that natural influenza transmission in human beings occurs over short distances primarily via droplet and contact routes.<sup>57</sup> Transmission of influenza virus via large- particle droplets generated from the respiratory tract of infected individuals during coughing, sneezing, talking, or during procedures such as suctioning or bronchoscopy requires close contact between the infected source and the recipient (6 feet or less). Large droplets do not remain suspended in the air and hence special ventilation or airflow systems are not required since true aerosolization does not occur. Preferential airborne transmission has not been documented as a method of human- to- human influenza transmission.<sup>62</sup> However, many procedures such as intubation and bronchoscopy have the potential to be aerosolgenerating. The current literature does not define conditions under which influenza could become opportunistically airborne. Given the paucity of data in this regard, it may be reasonable to employ airborne precautions and higher levels of PPE such as N95 masks when such procedures are medically necessary. This perspective is important for anesthesiologists to consider when caring for patients with influenza in the intensive care unit or managing their airway in the emergency department, hospital ward, or operating room.

### SEVERE ACUTE RESPIRATORY SYNDROME

Severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) caused by a novel coronavirus, called SARS- associated coronavirus (SARS- CoV), was first reported in Guangdong Province in China in November 2002. The first case appears to be a businessman from the city of Foshan who subsequently died from the disease. However, it was three months later, when a physician from the same province became ill while staying in a hotel in Hong Kong, that SARS rose to international attention. Twelve guests staying at the same hotel subsequently became infected, including seven whose rooms were on the same floor as the physician. These hotel guests subsequently became the index patients transporting the virus around the globe as they traveled home by airplane. SARS was first identified in Toronto, Canada, in March 2003. Because of the speed of international travel, in less than four months, about 4000 cases and 550 deaths from SARS could be traced to the guests infected in Hong Kong. Ultimately, a total of 8096 people in 29 countries became infected with the SARS- CoV, and 774 of them died<sup>63,64</sup> (Table 64.8).

Toronto was the epicenter of the disease in North America. The Ontario Ministry of Health reported 361 SARS cases, including 33 deaths (9%). Over half infected were health care workers including three anesthesiologists and one intensivist.<sup>54,65</sup> Overall case fatality was approximately 15% but over 50% for patients over the age of 65.<sup>66</sup> On July 4, 2003, the WHO announced the global SARS outbreak was contained.<sup>66</sup>

SARS, like influenza, is of zoonotic origin. The virus is ubiquitous and has been isolated from swine, cattle, dogs, cats, and chickens. In 2002, SARS- CoV was isolated from Himalayan palm civets found in Chinese live animal markets where demand for freshly killed meat and poultry served as the source of SARS- CoV crossing species.<sup>67</sup> This led to culling of the animals, although subsequent studies have not validated infection in either farm- raised or wild civets. More recent data suggest that bats are the likely natural reservoirs of SARS- like coronavirus<sup>68</sup> (Fig. 64.17).

### MIDDLE EAST RESPIRATORY SYNDROME

Like SARS, Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS) is caused by a novel coronavirus, MERS- CoV. The first two cases of MERS- CoV infection as a cause of communityacquired pneumonia were identified in Saudi Arabia and reported to the WHO in September 2012. As of February 2023, laboratory- confirmed cases of MERS number 2604, with 936 associated deaths (36% case fatality rate) in 27 countries.<sup>70,71</sup> The majority of cases have been reported in Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates, and the Republic of Korea (South Korea); 80% of reported cases to date have been in Saudi Arabia<sup>72</sup> (Fig. 64.18).

MERS- CoV, like SARS- CoV, is a zoonotic virus. Dromedary camels are the major reservoir host and animal source of MERS infection in humans through either direct or indirect contact. The specific method of transmission remains unclear, although poorly cooked camel meat and

unpasteurized camel milk remain suspect; consumption of raw or undercooked animal products carries a high risk of infection. Human- to- human transmission in the _non– health care_ setting appears rare, with little to no such cases documented.<sup>73</sup>

Human- to- human transmission from ill people to others through close contact within the _health care setting_ accounts for the majority of reported cases to date. The largest outbreak today outside of Saudi Arabia occurred in South Korea in 2015.<sup>74</sup> In that hospital- associated cluster, a single infected person admitted to a hospital in South Korea led to 186 cases and 36 deaths across multiple health care facilities.<sup>69,74</sup> This particular case highlights the consequences of substandard infection control practices and inadequate use of PPE as the virus does not pass easily from person to person unless there is close contact.<sup>72</sup> Health care workers must remain alert for the continued threat of MERS as global travel continues to accelerate.

Since identification of COVID- 19 infections, a wide range of therapeutics have been investigated as potential treatment options for patients with COVID- 19. Repurposed drugs such as lopinavir/ritonavir, hydroxychloroquine, and ivermectin demonstrated _in vitro_ activity against SARS- CoV- 2, but failed to demonstrate conclusive therapeutic benefit in clinical trials and are not currently recommended for treatment of COVID- 19.<sup>100</sup> Remdesivir was the first antiviral drug to receive approval by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in October 2020 for patients 12 years of age and older for hospitalized patients requiring treatment for COVID- 19, following analysis of three randomized controlled trials including the Adaptive COVID19 Treatment Trial (ACTT- 1), a double- blind, randomized, placebo- controlled trial that demonstrated shortened time to recovery in adult patients hospitalized with COVID- 19 who had evidence of lower respiratory infection.<sup>101,102</sup> Dexamethasone became the first drug to demonstrate a mortality benefit among hospitalized patients with COVID19 receiving supplemental oxygen or invasive mechanical ventilation in the RECOVERY trial, offering an accessible treatment option at relatively low cost.<sup>103</sup> Additional antivirals, immunomodulators such as janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors (baricitinib) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) pathway inhibitors (tocilizumab), and monoclonal antibodies have received authorization by the FDA and European Medicines Agency (EMA) with indications for use that vary based on age, severity of illness, and current data as scientific evidence accumulates and the pandemic evolves.<sup>104,105</sup>

Both vaccines have since received FDA approval based on safety and efficacy and are among 11 COVID- 19 vaccines with WHO’s Emergency Use Listing as of July 14, 2022.<sup>106</sup>

### PERSONAL PROTECTION

The logistics of caring for patients with disease due to highly contagious pathogens is complex and outside of the scope of this chapter as is a detailed discussion of PPE. That said, it is relevant to note some consistencies which are likely to reduce human- to- human transmission whether patient to patient or patient to health care worker. More important than the unique physical structure of the special pathogen units located in the United States and Europe is the culture of safety and preparedness through which the system works to train health care workers to care not only for the patient but also for themselves.<sup>107</sup>

<!-- PDF page 26 -->

Providers caring for patients on hospital wards, in the operating room, and in the intensive care unit have the highest risk of contracting the disease themselves. This is reflected in the experience with the SARS- CoV, MERS- CoV, Ebola virus, and SARS- CoV- 2. In all situations there are preventable infections and on occasion unnecessary deaths due to a lack of appropriate training and a lack of the critical knowledge specific to the pathophysiology of each disease as well as the logistics of caring for patients while protecting oneself.<sup>107</sup>

While adherence to standard infection control universal precautions is advisable when caring for all patients, anesthesia providers need to pay particular attention to elements of hospital protocols that apply to patients with respiratory infections. In addition to precautions necessary to prevent contact routes of transmission _vis- à- vis_ hand hygiene, gloves, and gowns; droplet precautions should be

At a minimum, providers should be wearing a face mask when entering the room of a patient with suspected influenza, including perioperative areas such as preop or postanesthesia care unit (PACU). When moving a suspected influenza patient on droplet precautions from one area of the hospital to another, having the patient wear a face mask during transport is prudent. This would of course include transport to and from the operating room if deemed medically safe and appropriate.<sup>62</sup>

Anesthesiologists routinely perform medical procedures that are aerosol- generating in patients with highly contagious pathogens. When patients have suspected or confirmed disease, procedures should be limited to those deemed entirely medically necessary. As an example, for EVD patients, central venous catheters should generally not be placed for hemodynamic monitoring, but primarily to provide critical vascular access, ensuring low-risk access to blood samples, delivering electrolyte replacement, and for administration of vasoactive medications. Similarly, when considering respiratory support for patients with EVD, early intubation may be preferable despite the inherent risk of aerosolization when compared to noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) and the risk of vomiting or hematemesis. NIPPV may also result in aerosolization of fluids containing viruses, putting caregivers at excess risk not only from Ebola but also from other highly contagious diseases. If intubation is necessary, having a thoughtful predetermined plan for semi- elective non- emergent intubation is essential. Similar approaches should be considered for other infections based on an understanding of routes of transmission and other considerations. In all cases, appropriate airway management options and advanced airway management equipment and all medications must be easily and readily available, and providers must take into account the time necessary for donning appropriate PPE prior to entering the patient care environment.<sup>80,84</sup>

Certainly, when caring for EVD patients, the lowest level of PPE staff should wear includes disposable water- resistant suite or coverall, including a hood, an N95 mask, a disposable full- face shield, two sets of gloves (appropriately sealed), and impermeable foot and leg coverings.
