# Anesthesia and Global Health Equity

BETELEHEM ASNAKE, TYLER LAW, MAYTINEE LILAONITKUL, GUNISHA KAUR, CAROLINA HAYLOCK- LOOR, CORNELIUS SENDAGIRE, FAUZIA KHAN, ODINAKACHUKWU EHIE, and MICHAEL S. LIPNICK

### KEY POINTS

- More than 5 billion of the world’s 7 billion people lack access to safe anesthesia and surgical services, and a similarly significant proportion lack access to fundamentals such as oxygen therapy. Surgical disease accounts for 30% of the global disease burden, yet less than 1% of development assistance for health supports the delivery of anesthesia and surgical care. Lack of access to safe, timely, and affordable anesthesia and surgery kills more than four times as many people as acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), tuberculosis (TB), and malaria combined.

- Lack of access to safe anesthesia and surgical services is among the most neglected crises in global health. Anesthesia- related mortality in some low- income countries (LICs) is reported in the 1:100s and is mostly avoidable. The burden of surgical disease is growing and disproportionately affects low- and middle- income countries.

- Pain is one of the top causes of morbidity worldwide, and inadequate access to analgesia is among the most inequitable global public health crises the world faces today. Five and a half billion people have limited or no access to narcotic medications for analgesia. Six highincome countries (HICs) account for 80% of the world’s opioid consumption. Key drug control policies, organizations, and politics continue to influence issues of access and abuse, with disproportionately negative impacts on underserved populations.

- Critical shortages and the inequitable distribution of anesthesia providers are significant barriers to increasing access to safe anesthesia and surgical care. The density of surgery, anesthesia, and obstetric providers is 0.7 per 100,000 population for LICs compared to 57 per 100,000 population for HICs.

- Modern anesthesia workforce training and practice models vary widely from country to country. Innovative workforce solutions are needed to increase provider numbers while simultaneously ensuring quality and promoting access for underserved populations.

- Anesthesia, analgesia, and surgical services are feasible in resource- constrained settings and are as cost- effective as many other public health interventions (e.g., vaccinations).

- Issues of access, safety, and resource utilization are relevant to all anesthesia providers. Equipping anesthesia providers with the knowledge and skills needed to address global challenges in anesthesia will become increasingly important to expand access to safe and affordable anesthesia care worldwide.

- Health care providers from multiple disciplines (nursing, surgery, obstetrics, anesthesia, and many more) are needed to provide surgical and perioperative care effectively. Anesthesiologists routinely work across the perioperative disciplines and can play a significant role in improving cohesive advocacy efforts and leading progress in global health equity for surgical disease and analgesia.

- The global anesthesia community is lagging behind other health disciplines in addressing global health challenges. It must rapidly expand investment in initiatives to help characterize (research), address (implementation and policy), and support (financing) global anesthesia challenges.

- Developing infrastructure, expanding the workforce, improving data to drive policy, providing financial risk protection mechanisms for surgical patients, improving referral and prehospital systems, and providing essential medicines are actions the global anesthesia community must prioritize. The need to act promptly is immense and must be balanced but not overshadowed by research agendas.

## Introduction

In recent years, considerable efforts have been made to define a global “standard” or “optimal” anesthesia practice.<sup>1–4</sup> These debates, investigations, and innovations have often been framed in maximizing patient safety. More recently, the focus of these efforts has expanded to incorporate the fundamental goals of maximizing not only safety, but also accessibility, affordability, and equity for anesthesia and perioperative services.<sup>5</sup>

With scientific advancement and economic development, anesthesia—like much of medicine—has changed dramatically since the first public display of ether anesthesia in October 1846. However, over the past 175 years, advances in anesthesia have been neither uniform nor universal. This has resulted in vastly heterogeneous anesthesia practice models and massive inequities in access to safe anesthesia care, sometimes even within the same cities and communities. Most of the world’s population does not have access to safe, affordable surgical, anesthesia, pain, or critical care services, and relatively few resources are being invested by governments, donors, or the global anesthesia community to address this crisis.

In this chapter, we build off the work of Dr. Miller and many colleagues from around the world to explore the diversity of anesthesia practice models and the challenges facing the global anesthesia provider and patient communities. A better understanding of the structure of modern anesthesia care models and the challenges they face is a crucial step to improving access and patient safety worldwide.

The first section of this chapter describes the scope and magnitude of the ongoing global surgical, anesthesia, and pain crises. This section also explores why the global public health community has relatively neglected these crises, and reviews potential areas for intervention, advocacy, and change. The section also highlights challenges and health care disparities related to oxygen and critical care that the SARS- CoV- 2 pandemic highlighted.

The second section of this chapter summarizes the diversity in different anesthesia provider care models, cadres, training, and scopes of practice from around the world, including snapshots of current challenges.

The final section of this chapter provides a primer on essential clinical and non- clinical knowledge relevant to anesthesia practice in resource- variable settings. The chapter concludes by discussing the role of anesthesia providers beyond the confines of the operating room or hospital setting, who will increasingly be called upon for solutions that increase access to safe anesthesia, surgery, analgesia, and critical care globally.

The transition away from the term “global health” is not only because of the common and unfortunate misunderstanding that global health initiatives must focus on “outreach” (i.e., going to a poor setting outside one’s community or country) but also because the term’s origins have a significant negative legacy with ongoing negative repercussions. The term “tropical medicine” in the late 19th century was primarily focused on protecting colonizers from disease during conquests, while the term “international health” in the late 20th century inherently promoted initiatives done in other places, typically low- income countries by highincome country leadership. Eventually, “global health” was coined in the 21st century by high- income countries in an attempt to encompass a broad scope of activities. However, “global health” has not been able to correct its legacy. It has frequently become synonymous with programs that have significant power imbalances that favor wealthier nations or participants, that do not adequately account for or elevate local priorities, and that provide inconsistent and inadequate allyship.

### Determinants of Health

The majority of health (i.e., physical and mental well- being) is not determined by health care. For example, the US spends more on health care than any country in the world, yet life expectancy and health outcomes for many conditions are not even in the top 25.<sup>9</sup> Health is shaped by numerous factors, including both structural and social determinants of health. The social determinants of health (SDOH) start in our homes, schools, communities, and places of work (e.g., income, education, access to food, safety, etc.). These SDOH are downstream and shaped by structural determinants such as governing, economic, and social conditions.

The emergence of social medicine and the notion that determinants of health, not simply disease, must be addressed to improve individual and population health can be traced to the mid- 19th century. Rudolf Virchow (perhaps best known for his contributions to pathology) described a significant increase in disease and poverty during the industrial revolution and a typhus epidemic: “If medicine is to fulfill her great task, then she must enter the political and social life.”<sup>10</sup> Social determinants of health are frequently grouped into five general areas:

1.  Economic stability

2.  Education access and quality

3.  Health care access and quality

4.  Neighborhood and built environment

5.  Social and community context

Economic stability impacts health through factors such as poverty, employment, food security, and housing

stability.<sup>11</sup> This is a universal issue. It affects health in low- , middle- , and high- income country communities with disparities associated with race, gender, and geography. For example, in the United States, despite a relatively high median annual income ($61,417 for men and $50,982 for women), 1 in 10 (∼37 million) people live in poverty (&lt;∼$13,000 annual income or &lt;$35/day).<sup>12,13</sup> Worldwide 1.2 billion people live in extreme poverty (&lt;$1.90 per day) with little to no access to safe housing, clean water, food, or health care.<sup>14</sup> Numerous studies have also shown that people living in poverty are more likely to experience illnesses such as obesity, developmental delay, asthma, diabetes, maternal mortality, and heart disease.<sup>15</sup> Globally, the effect of economic instability on health outcomes is even more pronounced.<sup>14</sup> Although the global poverty level has fallen over the last 15 years, the SARS- CoV- 2 pandemic may have set back this progress by up to 10 years.<sup>16</sup>

Education access and quality also have significant impacts on health through factors such as language and literacy, early childhood education and development, completion of higher education, and education attainment in general. Higher education correlates with better- paying jobs and fewer safety hazards. It not only provides people with the increased financial capacity to access quality resources, including higher- quality housing and higher social status, but it can also enable them to reflect critically on ways to optimize their well- being.<sup>17</sup> Globally, 85% of the world population is literate; however, in some communities and countries, literacy rates are still below 30%.<sup>18</sup> Poor literacy affects peoples’ health directly by limiting their personal, social, and cultural development and hindering health literacy development.<sup>19</sup>

Health care access and quality are the connection between timely access to health care and health. This topic includes key factors such as access to primary care, health literacy, health insurance coverage, or even physical distance to health care services. While many poor communities, especially in HICs, may have access to emergency services, this is not equivalent to access to care. It is well known that lack of access to primary care and dependence on emergency services are linked to worse health outcomes, including cancer, heart disease, and strokes. In low- and middle- income countries, a significant shortage of medical providers often drives access to quality health care, especially for anesthesia and perioperative services. This is also true for many rural communities in low- , middle- , and highincome countries. The shortage of anesthesia providers and its impact on global access to health care is discussed further in this chapter.

Neighborhood and built environment impact health and well- being through factors such as where a person lives, including their housing, neighborhood, and environment. This also includes access to transportation, healthy food, clean water and air, and neighborhood safety. A child born in Japan is expected to live ∼85 years, while a child born in the Central African Republic is expected to live only ∼54 years.<sup>9</sup> Similar disparities exist even within high- income countries, where life expectancy varies by more than 20 years depending on the neighborhood within the same cities.<sup>20</sup> People living in houses with mold, poor plumbing, or limited ventilation can experience increased asthma, respiratory problems, lead poisoning, and carbon monoxide poisoning. Living in neighborhoods that lack grocery stores that sell fresh, nutritious foods (i.e., food deserts) or lacking the money to purchase these foods puts people at a greater risk for poor health.

On the contrary, environments with multiple wellstocked grocery stores, extensive public transportation, safe places to walk, and green spaces for exercise and leisure promote healthy behaviors (e.g., diet and exercise) and health.<sup>21</sup> Access to safe drinking water and sanitation is considered a fundamental human right and a key development goal for all communities, yet billions of people worldwide lack access.<sup>22</sup> This is not only in lower- income countries but also in high- income countries. Two million Americans do not have running water or sanitation services, and this lack of access in the United States, like many settings, disproportionately affects indigenous, Black, and Latino households.<sup>23</sup> Recently in Flint, Michigan (United States), poor governance and systemic racism resulted in several communities consuming public water that was contaminated with lead with significant health implications.<sup>24</sup> Finally, crime and violence often lead to physical and/or psychological injury and poor health outcomes in the form of stress, mental health illness, or conditions that require hospitalization. For example, chronic stress related to crime, violence, or poverty has been correlated with cardiovascular disease. Not only does trauma and violence disproportionately affect poor communities around the world, but also outcomes from trauma are worse according to multiple socioeconomic factors,<sup>25</sup> even in high- income countries. Moreover, people who live in high crime areas may not feel safe to leave their homes. This sedentary lifestyle can lead to negative health outcomes including obesity.<sup>26</sup>

Social and community context is the connection between peoples’ health or well- being to where people live, learn, work, and play. This includes factors like conditions in the workplace, incarceration, civic participation, and cohesion within the community. As a result, structural conflict and unconscious bias can play a major role within this context. The interaction of people with family, friends, co- workers, and community can have a major impact on health and wellbeing. Many people face health risks outside of their control due to unsafe neighborhoods, discrimination, and/or lack of income. Relationships that are positive at home, with coworkers, and other community members at large can help reduce negative impacts. Traditional practices and societal pressure can lead to adverse health outcomes in a global context. For example, female genital mutilation, still practiced in many parts of the world as part of cultural tradition, leads to significant pain and obstetric and birthing complications.<sup>27</sup>

### Global Surgery and Anesthesia

Global health has received unprecedented levels of interest in recent decades, expanding from its infectious disease origins to now incorporating a wider range of diseases, including the social and environmental factors affecting health.<sup>28,29</sup> Despite this, surgery and anesthesia have remained relatively forgotten by the global health community, with little to no direct investment by international aid and development agencies (Fig. 2.1).

In June of 1980, then Director- General of the World Health Organization (WHO), Dr. Halfdan Mahler, gave a presentation to the International College of Surgeons in Mexico City entitled “Surgery and Health for All.” In this speech, Dr. Mahler stated that “Surgery clearly has an important role to play in primary health care and in the services supporting it. Yet, the vast majority of the world’s population has no access whatsoever to skilled surgical care, and little is being done to find a solution. I beg of you to give serious consideration to this most serious manifestation of social inequity in health care.”<sup>30</sup>

Despite the recognition of the global anesthesia and surgical crises several decades ago, it has been only recently that the global health community has begun to take notice and action.<sup>31–36</sup> In 2004, the WHO created the Emergency and Essential Surgical Care Program (EESC), and in 2005, the WHO Global Initiative for Emergency and Essential Surgical Care (GIEESC) was formed to convene multidisciplinary stakeholders interested in the surgical disease. In 2007, the Bellagio Essential Surgery Group and Burden of Surgical Disease Working Group (later renamed the Alliance for Surgery and Anesthesia Presence [ASAP]) formed as two of the earliest concerted efforts to raise international awareness for surgical disease by advocating for the integration of surgery into health systems and the promotion of research and collaboration across disciplines.<sup>37</sup> These efforts followed in the wake of a seminal chapter on surgery in the second edition of the _Disease Control Priorities in Developing Countries_ (DCP2) book in 2006.<sup>38</sup> In 2008, the WHO launched the Safe Surgery Saves Lives initiative along with the WHO Safe Surgery Checklist.<sup>39,40</sup> Also in 2008, multiple leaders in global health highlighted surgery as the “neglected stepchild” and as the “other neglected disease” in global health, drawing a comparison with the then emerging term “neglected tropical diseases.”<sup>41,42</sup>

### GLOBAL ANESTHESIA, SURGERY, AND PAIN INEQUITIES: SCOPE AND SCALE OF THE PROBLEMS

### Global Burden of Surgical Disease

It has been estimated that nearly 30% of global morbidity and mortality is surgically treatable, with tens of millions of lives lost each year due to surgical conditions.<sup>5</sup> This burden has likely increased significantly due to the SARS- CoV- 2 pandemic, due to global operating room closures and the diversion of perioperative resources to the pandemic response.<sup>52</sup>

The burden of surgical disease predominantly affects LMICs and kills four times more people than human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), TB, and malaria combined (Fig. 2.2).<sup>53</sup> In addition to negative impacts on health and wellbeing, there is also a significant economic burden associated with surgical morbidity and mortality. By 2030, morbidity and mortality from surgical conditions could reduce annual gross domestic product (GDP) growth by an estimated 2% in LMICs. In the past, similar calculations were used to generate global investment in malaria successfully, but had estimated much lower (1.3%) decreases in GDP due to malaria.<sup>54</sup> Without significant and immediate intervention, the surgical disease will produce economic productivity losses of more than US$12 trillion for LMICs between 2015 and 2030. While it is generally agreed that surgical conditions account for a large proportion of global morbidity and mortality, precise data to support this have been lacking. This challenge has been attributed partly to a lack of resources invested in such research and several inherent difficulties in quantifying surgical diseases.

<!-- PDF page 7 -->

The term “global burden of disease” (GBD) uses the disability- adjusted life year (DALY) as a unit to quantify premature death (years of life lost) and disability (years of life lived in a state of less than full health) (Fig. 2.3). The DALY was initially developed for the seminal GBD 1990 study to quantify the burden of different diseases around the world and has since become commonly used in public health and health economics.<sup>55,56</sup> Because the DALY is routinely used to inform resource allocation, it has also been utilized to describe surgical and pain disease burdens. The global health community has moved away from using terms such as the third world, developed, or developing when describing countries’ levels of economic development. Leading causes of DALYs are often reported geographically by World Bank income level (see Table 2.1) or, more recently, by socio- demographic index (SDI) (Figs. 2.4 and 2.5). The SDI is a composite average of three indicators predictive of health outcomes: income per capita, average educational attainment (for a population >15 years old), and total fertility rate.

### Global Burden of Pain

As with surgical disease burden, there is consensus on the staggering prevalence and incidence of pain worldwide, though data on pain burden in the global context are limited, and significant challenges to quantifying this burden remain. Pain is one of the most common reasons for seeking medical attention, is among the top five causes of DALYs worldwide, and directly accounts for four (low back pain, neck pain, musculoskeletal pain, migraine) of the leading 10 causes of years lived with disability (YLDs).<sup>53,58</sup> These statistics do not even account for pain secondary to oncology, injury, or postoperative etiologies, which likely increase these numbers substantially. It is estimated that 10% to 25% of the world’s population suffer from recurring and chronic pain, with increasing numbers caused by intentional physical harm such as war, violence, and torture.<sup>59</sup> For example, studies have found that over 80% of refugees globally experience chronic somatic pain after trauma.<sup>60</sup> Uncontrolled pain has many potential negative impacts on health, well- being, and economic productivity, including increased risk of myocardial infarction and chronic pain.

The 2017 Lancet Commission on Palliative Care and Pain Relief defined serious health- related suffering (SHS) as suffering associated with illness or injury that compromises physical, social, or emotional functioning and cannot be relieved without medical intervention.<sup>50</sup> Approximately half of all deaths worldwide involve SHS, and more than 80% of people who die with SHS are from LMICs. It is estimated that 2.5 million children die with SHS each year. Ninety- eight percent of these children live in LMICs, and more than 90% of these deaths are avoidable. Among the top 10 conditions associated with SHS are HIV, malignancy, and injury. Though not as apparent as injury or cancer, HIV/AIDS is a significant source of pain and analgesia need.<sup>61</sup> Pain related to HIV is not routinely managed by anesthesiologists when compared to other more common pain etiologies; however, the relevance of HIV as a rallying point to advocate for greater access to analgesics in LMICs is discussed further in this chapter. In many countries, conditions such as diabetes, sickle cell, and leprosy are also responsible for significant pain burdens. With increasing longevity and industrialization, the disease burden for malignancy and injury is expected to increase significantly in LMICs. As providers with often the most experience administering analgesics, anesthesia providers play an expanded and critical role in pain management, especially in resource- constrained settings.

### Global Burden of Critical Illness

Over 80% of the world’s population resides in LMICs, where anesthesiologists not only provide perioperative

and pain services but also play a central role in critical care services.<sup>62,63</sup>

Tens of millions of people become critically ill each year; however, due to multiple challenges, there is limited epidemiological data on the global burden of critical illness.<sup>64</sup> It is generally accepted that critical illnesses (e.g., sepsis, trauma, obstetric emergencies, and respiratory illness) contribute a large proportion of the global disease burden, and much of this can be averted with cost- effective, lowtech strategies (e.g., access to oxygen, fluids, monitors, and antibiotics).<sup>65,66</sup> Low- and middle- income countries have a significantly higher burden of critical illness than HICs, experience proportionally greater mortality for potentially recoverable critical illness, and see a higher burden among younger patients.<sup>67,68</sup> Historically, respiratory failure has been one of the most common indications for intensive care admissions globally. Hypoxemia, associated with both communicable and non- communicable diseases, leads to substantial mortality, yet in many countries, up to 90% of the population may lack access to oxygen.<sup>69</sup> According to three studies of critical illness from sub- Saharan Africa (SSA), the prevalence of hypoxemia ranged between 4% and 12%, with mortality ranging between 50% and 80%.<sup>70,71</sup> In one of those studies, only 6% of the hypoxemic patients who needed invasive mechanical ventilation received it.<sup>70</sup> Care for critically ill patients can be resource- intensive, and fraught with challenges related to the cost of care, staffing, equipment, and consistent supply chains.<sup>72</sup> Supplemental oxygen, for example, is one of the most ubiquitous medical therapies for acutely ill patients. Despite being listed by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an essential medicine for hypoxemia management, a survey of over 200 hospitals in 12 African countries revealed that less than 50% had consistent, uninterrupted oxygen sources, and less than 25% had an oxygen concentrator.<sup>73,74</sup>

The SARS- CoV- 2 pandemic, which affected over 600 million people globally and led to over six million deaths in two years, exposed these long- ignored global challenges in critical care, highlighting the vulnerability and inequity in global oxygen security that affects all countries.<sup>75</sup> The burden of critical illness due to SARS- CoV- 2 significantly challenged highly resourced settings in HICs and exacerbated the inequities in intensive care unit (ICU) resources and capacity between HICs and LMICs.<sup>76</sup> It was estimated that over half a million SARS- CoV- 2 patients in LMICs needed oxygen daily, without which potentially preventable deaths occurred.<sup>75</sup> These estimates did not include hypoxemic non- SARS- CoV- 2 patients who require oxygen therapy, like children with pneumonia, malaria, or HIV/ AIDS patients, who contribute a substantial disease burden in LMICs every year. In the wake of the SARS- CoV- 2 pandemic, global investment in oxygen ecosystems, including research to define the magnitude of this challenge, has significant potential to improve global health equity.

### Disparities in Access, Outcomes, and Affordability

The exact disease burden attributable to surgical disease or pain is a focus of ongoing debate and research. Yet, it is generally accepted that the surgical, anesthesia, and pain crises are massive, largely avoidable, and disproportionately affect poor communities. Disease burden is staggering when including the burden of critical care (as well as lack of

<!-- PDF page 10 -->

access to oxygen). As previously discussed, injuries, maternal and child health conditions, and non- communicable diseases like malignancy comprise the largest share of the surgical disease burden. Significant disparities in access and outcomes exist for each of these conditions in LMICs and many HICs.

**Access.** Of the roughly 300,000 maternal deaths worldwide, 99% occur in low- resource settings (66% in subSaharan Africa), and the majority are preventable with relatively basic surgical, anesthesia, and perioperative care.<sup>77</sup> The major complications that account for nearly 75% of all maternal deaths are severe bleeding (mostly bleeding after childbirth), infections (usually after childbirth), high blood pressure during pregnancy (preeclampsia and eclampsia), complications from delivery, and unsafe abortion.<sup>78</sup> The African Surgical Outcomes Study (ASOS), a prospective international cohort study observing 7- day perioperative outcomes in sub- Saharan Africa, identified that maternal mortality rates following cesarean section were 50 times higher than in high- income countries.<sup>79</sup> The factors that prevent women from receiving or seeking care during pregnancy and childbirth include poverty, distance to facilities, lack of information, inadequate and poor- quality services, and cultural beliefs and practices.<sup>80</sup> Disparities in maternal health outcomes and maternal health care are not exclusive to LMICs. Significant maternal morbidity and mortality exist in HICs, often stemming from racial and ethnic care disparities. For example, in the United States, non- Hispanic Black women are three to four times more likely to die from pregnancy- related causes than non- Hispanic White women.<sup>81</sup> Moreover, non- Hispanic Black women had the fastest increase in maternal deaths between 2007 and 2014 and have maternal death rates up to 12 times higher in some cities than non- Hispanic White women.<sup>82</sup> A growing body of research acknowledges the role that structural racism plays in generating these disparities.<sup>83</sup> A comprehensive approach to quality improvement from preconception to postpartum is required to reduce racial and ethnic disparities in maternal morbidity and mortality rates.

As previously discussed, injury comprises the largest share of the global surgical disease burden. Nearly 1 billion injured people require health care each year, and the vast majority of almost 5 million annual deaths from injury occur in LMICs. Within HICs like the United States, injury and violence disproportionately impact poor communities and communities of color, with significantly worse outcomes from trauma by race, income level, and insurance type.<sup>25</sup> Scaling access to basic surgical and anesthesia services in LMICs could avert 77 million DALYs and 1.5 million deaths annually.<sup>45</sup> Injury represents the majority (77%) of this avoidable morbidity and mortality, followed by maternal and neonatal conditions (14%).

Based solely on the inequitable distribution of operating theaters, more than two billion people have no access to surgical services.<sup>84</sup> When also accounting for the timeliness, facility capacity, safety, and affordability, nearly 5 billion people—most of the world’s population—lack access to surgical and anesthesia care. These disparities in access disproportionately affect lower- income areas like subSaharan Africa or South Asia, where more than 95% of the

population does not have access to surgical and anesthesia services. In some higher- income regions like North America and Europe, access varies considerably, but more than 95% of the population generally has access (Fig. 2.6).<sup>85</sup> Even in HICs, like the United States, access to anesthesia, surgical, and analgesia care can be limited for rural and underserved populations.<sup>86,87</sup> It is estimated that universal access to “essential” surgical procedures (which include treatments for injury, obstetric complications, abdominal emergencies, cataracts, and congenital anomalies) would prevent approximately 1.5 million deaths per year or 6% to 7% of all preventable deaths in LMICs.<sup>45</sup> The Global Initiative for Children’s Surgery (GICS) estimates that 1.7 billion children lack access to surgical care.<sup>88</sup> It is estimated that approximately 143 million additional surgeries are required annually.<sup>5</sup> Of the 234 million major surgical procedures performed annually, only 3% of them are estimated to occur in LMICs, where 30% of the world’s population lives.<sup>5,89</sup>

Inadequate access to analgesia is among the most neglected and inequitable global public health challenges. The global burden of pain disproportionately affects the poor due to the high- burden conditions associated with pain (e.g., HIV, malignancy, and injury) in LMICs, and the general lack of access to analgesics. Although pain is the most common reason for seeking medical attention, and analgesia is considered a fundamental human right, gaps in access to analgesia are significant and among the most striking global health disparities worldwide.<sup>90–92</sup> Injury and malignancy, two conditions with significant analgesia needs, represent a substantial proportion of surgical disease burden in LMICs. Yet, the vast majority of LMIC populations have limited or nonexistent access to opiate analgesia. Six HICs account for 80% of the world’s opiate consumption, and countries that contain 17% of the world’s population (Canada, United States, Western Europe, Australia, and New Zealand) are responsible for 92% of the world’s opiate consumption (Figs. 2.7 and 2.8).<sup>50,93–95</sup> Pain and palliative care remain relatively neglected by the global health community and disproportionately affect vulnerable populations in LMICs.<sup>50,96</sup> In recent decades, access to opiates has improved in some LMICs, but not all (e.g., consumption in Africa and South Asia has declined).<sup>95</sup> Inequitable access to analgesia is not unique to LMICs and has been repeatedly described in many HICs, including the United States.<sup>97–99</sup> Reasons for the inequitable distribution of analgesia are discussed further in the next section of this chapter.

Inequitable access to critical care services has been highlighted by multiple recent events like the SARS coronavirus (2002–2003), influenza H5N1, Ebola, and most recently, the SARS- CoV- 2 pandemic. Nonetheless, global access to critical care remains limited, especially for poor communities. Critical care beds in HICs are more than 20 times higher (per 100,000 inhabitants) than those in LMICs.<sup>63,100</sup> Despite recent increases in capacity in some LMICs, it is unclear the extent to which access is available to the public or the impact on health equity.<sup>101,102</sup> For patients with access to critical care services, there are significant disparities in care and outcomes between and within many countries. For example, mortality for patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is approximately 70% higher in many LMICs than in HICs.<sup>103,104</sup>

**Affordability.** Affordability is another significant barrier to accessing surgical and anesthesia services. Each year, approximately 33 million people face catastrophic out- ofpocket expenditure due to payment for surgery and anesthesia (Fig. 2.9), with an additional 48 million people facing catastrophic expenditure related to nonmedical costs of accessing surgical care (e.g., transportation, lodging, and food).<sup>5,125</sup> Nearly half (3.7 billion) of the world’s population is at risk of catastrophic expenditure if they were to have surgical and anesthesia care. Most at- risk people live in sub- Saharan Africa and South and Southeast Asia. The affordability issue applies not only to the surgery but also to perioperative care components, including analgesia and transfusions.<sup>126</sup> For example, in South America, a monthly prescription of opiates for chronic pain may cost 200% of annual income.<sup>105</sup> In India (where nominal gross national income per capita is $1670), a unit of blood often costs up to $247 despite a legal limit of $25.<sup>127,128</sup> Global health and development leaders, including the WHO and World Bank, prioritize financial risk protection as a critical component of achieving universal health coverage goals for all countries. Despite relatively high out- of- pocket costs at the individual level for some procedures, surgery is a highly cost- effective public health intervention, as discussed further in the following sections.

### GLOBAL ANESTHESIA, SURGERY, AND PAIN INEQUITIES: ORIGINS AND AREAS FOR INTERVENTION

Workforce shortages, inadequate infrastructure, lack of policy and prioritization, and increasing burden of surgical

<!-- PDF page 14 -->

disease are among many factors contributing to current limitations in access, affordability, and safety of anesthesia and surgical services worldwide.<sup>129</sup> As previously discussed, global industrialization and an “epidemiological transition” in many LMICs have resulted in rising noncommunicable disease and injury burdens with lagging investment to address these issues. While these fundamental imbalances between health care needs and health care resources underlie much of the current global surgery, anesthesia, and pain crises, several additional factors have also contributed.

### Misperceptions and Limited Data

Two common misperceptions about surgery and anesthesia have contributed to the delayed recognition of these fields as global public health priorities: (1) the scope and scale of surgical disease and pain burdens were vastly underestimated, and (2) surgical and anesthesia care were erroneously assumed to be too expensive and technology- dependent to be done safely or cost- effectively in resource- constrained environments. In this section, we discuss why surgical disease and pain burdens are historically poorly characterized. Misperceptions about the cost- effectiveness and feasibility of safe surgery and anesthesia care also have roots in a historical data void, especially from LMICs.

Several innovative service delivery models in public and private sectors of LMICs (e.g., Indus Hospital, Aravind Eye Hospitals, and Narayana Hrudayalaya Heart Hospital) have demonstrated that it is feasible to provide costeffective, safe, and affordable surgical and anesthesia care in resource- constrained countries.<sup>130–133</sup>

Recent data have consistently demonstrated that surgical services are among the most cost- effective public health interventions (Fig. 2.10). Several methods define cost- effectiveness thresholds, each with pros and cons.<sup>134</sup> One of the most commonly cited methods uses GDP- based thresholds. This approach was suggested by the WHO Commission on Macroeconomics and Health and has been defined by authors from the WHO’s Choosing Interventions that are Cost- Effective project (WHOCHOICE) as “interventions that avert one DALY for less

than average per capita income for a given country or region are considered very cost- effective; interventions that cost less than three times average per capita income per DALY averted are still considered cost- effective; and those that exceed this level are considered not cost- effective.”<sup>135</sup>

In 2003 in Bangladesh, one of the earliest costeffectiveness studies on surgical care reported the cost of emergency obstetric care at less than $11 per DALY averted.<sup>35</sup> This was three times more cost- effective than the measles vaccine (in 2003 dollars). The DCP2 and DCP3 demonstrated the cost- effectiveness of the first- level surgical hospital, with essential surgical services all being highly cost- effective and many costing $10 to $100 per DALY averted. This is comparable to the cost- effectiveness of other public health interventions such as immunizations ($13–$26 per DALY) or bed nets for malaria prevention ($6–$22 per DALY) and much more cost- effective than other high- priority public health interventions such as HIV treatment ($500 per DALY).<sup>136</sup> A recent analysis of the cost- effectiveness of a pediatric operating room in Uganda found a cost of $6.39 per DALY averted and $397.95 per life saved, with a net economic benefit of over US$5 million per year (cost of $41,000 per year).<sup>137</sup>

In a 2012 forum hosted by the Copenhagen Consensus, five leading health economists, including four Nobel laureates, were asked how best to spend US$75 billion over four years to “advance global welfare,” especially in LMICs. The leading priority identified by the group was the expansion of surgical care capacity (US$3 billion per year).<sup>138</sup>

In the past five years the global surgery and anesthesia communities have expanded the volume of data that dispel prior misconceptions and support the prioritization of surgical and anesthesia care in the global health agenda. During this timeframe, there has also been an explosion of articles that expand available data on a wide range of global anesthesia and surgical topics. Most of these articles have been published in the surgical journals, though recently anesthesia publications have begun actively supporting global anesthesia research. ### Advocacy and Policy

There are many reasons why surgery, anesthesia, and pain have historically not been prioritized by national health systems, donors, or the broader global health community. Although surgical disease accounts for 30% of the global disease burden, less than 1% of development assistance for health supports the delivery of anesthesia and surgical care.<sup>139</sup> Imbalance between resource allocation and disease burden is found in many health conditions, though the degree of this disparity for surgical disease and pain is particularly striking. As discussed previously, the lack of disease burden data and misperceptions about safety and cost- effectiveness have significantly hindered advocacy efforts for global anesthesia and surgery. In a qualitative analysis of factors that have hindered political prioritization for global surgery, several additional factors were identified, including: fragmentation of the global surgery community, lack of leadership and consensus, and inadequate political strategy (e.g., not capitalizing on opportunities such as the MDGs).<sup>140</sup> In another analysis to determine why certain disease- specific global health networks are relatively more or less effective than others, four common challenges were highlighted, each with relevance to global anesthesia and surgery: (1) defining the problem and how it should be addressed; (2) positioning the issue in a way to inspire action by external audiences; (3) building coalitions that include stakeholders outside the health care sector (HIC providers too often dominate coalitions); and (4) creating governance institutions that facilitate collective action (Fig. 2.11).<sup>141</sup> Another study that examined factors influencing the prioritization of surgery in national health systems concluded that sustained advocacy, effective framing of problems and solutions, robust country- level data, and support from regional and international partners were critical for success but often lacking.<sup>142</sup> Advocacy efforts for surgery, anesthesia, and pain face a few additional and relatively unique challenges. Unlike

infectious diseases such as HIV or Ebola, surgical disease and pain are not pandemic and do not incite similar action by the HIC donor community. Furthermore, most surgical conditions and pain are difficult to advertise and are not disease specific. While select pediatric conditions (e.g., cleft lips) are readily marketable, other conditions like trauma, hernias, and cesarean section are more difficult to compassionately portray in the media in attempts to improve public awareness.

The WHA resolution 68.15 emphasized advocacy and resource development in five key focus areas for global surgery (workforce, essential medicines, information management, service delivery, and advocacy). The LCOGS provided precise framing of problems (see Box 2.2), outlined priorities for country- level data collection, and helped to provide frameworks for national surgical, obstetric, and anesthesia plans (NSOAPs) in LMICs. As more NSOAPs are produced, they have the potential to serve as crucial rallying points for national advocacy efforts.

Anesthesia, surgery, and pain need global champions. Only recently has advocacy by leading global organizations (e.g., the World Bank, WHO) and select local governments in LMICs focused attention on anesthesia and surgery in the global context. This is partly due to advocacy efforts by several relatively recent initiatives (e.g., LCOGS, The Global Alliance for Surgical, Obstetric, Trauma, and Anaesthesia Care [G4 Alliance], WFSA, GICS, and several more). These multidisciplinary efforts must be expanded and sustained to reach the critical mass needed to overcome prior misperceptions and affect change. Advocacy efforts for surgery, anesthesia, and pain must be consistent with the critical messages of LCOGS, the Global Health 2035 Report, WHA resolution 68.15, and the SDGs, and emphasize that anesthesia, surgery, and analgesia are indispensable components of “universal health coverage.” No longer can surgery be seen as a vertical (i.e., disease- specific) program.

One key objective for advocacy efforts will be to identify new funding streams from global donors, national budgets, private sectors, and innovative models. ### Workforce Shortages and Strategies for Expansion

The critical shortage of trained anesthesia providers in many settings is one of the most significant barriers to expanding access to safe surgical, anesthesia, and pain services for billions of people worldwide. Although many key members of the surgical workforce exist (including surgeons, obstetricians, pathologists, radiologists, laboratory technicians, nurses, biomedical engineers, and more), anesthesia provider shortages in LMICs are particularly striking and relatively neglected. Countries like the Central African Republic have only two physician anesthesiologists and

only three non- physician anesthesia providers (NPAPs) for a population of nearly 5 million. Ethiopia, with a population of over 100 million people, has just over 100 physician specialist anesthesiologists. A survey of emergency obstetric care capacity at facilities in Uganda found that lack of staff had the most significant correlation with observed mortality rates.<sup>144</sup> In a survey of 64 public and private hospitals in Uganda, 84% did not have a physician specialist anesthesiologist, and 8% had no trained anesthesia providers at all.<sup>145</sup> In another survey of anesthesia providers from five main referral hospitals in East Africa (Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania, Rwanda, and Burundi), only 7% reported adequate anesthesia staffing.<sup>146</sup> In settings where providers exist, but in small numbers, the workforce shortage is compounded by heavy administrative burdens and non- clinical duties. While the anesthesia workforce shortage is most severe in LICs and particularly pronounced in sub- Saharan Africa, regional workforce shortages also exist in HICs and can significantly limit access to care for rural populations.<sup>147,148</sup> In one survey of rural hospitals in the United States, 36% reported delay or cancellation of surgery due to a lack of anesthesia providers.<sup>149</sup> Data from HICs demonstrating differences in trauma mortality are significantly worse starting at five miles from a trauma center in a major urban city.<sup>150</sup> One can only imagine outcome differences in settings where there may be no surgical or anesthesia provider for tens or even hundreds of miles.

The optimal number of specialist surgical, anesthetic, and obstetric workforce (SAO) providers needed to give access to safe surgery is unknown and likely to vary significantly based on local resources and needs. The SAO density correlates with life- expectancy and in one study, as SAO providers increased from 0 to 20 per 100,000 population, maternal mortality decreased by 13.1% for each 10 unit increase in provider density (Fig. 2.12). ### Infrastructure Challenges

The provision of surgical and anesthesia care requires infrastructure that is often lacking in many LMICs. As discussed elsewhere in this chapter, such infrastructure does not necessarily need to be high- cost or based on advanced technologies to provide safe care. Based on data from the 2019 African Surgical Outcomes Study (ASOS), a clusterrandomized trial was conducted (ASOS- 2) to implement a package of 5 low- tech interventions to reduce perioperative mortality: (1) admitting patients to higher-care wards; (2) increasing frequency of nursing checks; (3) placing patients in view or nursing stations; (4) allowing family to

stay on wards; and (5) placing postoperative surveillance guides visible at the bedside.<sup>161,162</sup> Although the trial did not meet enrollment goals or show a mortality difference, most enlightening was the observation that of the 160 intervention hospitals, only 59% were able to implement two or more of these interventions. In addition to the provider shortages already discussed, additional infrastructure challenges that frequently limit surgical and anesthesia service delivery include underdeveloped patient referral and prehospital systems, medication and equipment shortages, and inadequate supply chains and facility resources.

In maternal health, and more recently for surgery and anesthesia, a “three delays framework” has been used to describe factors delaying timely access to safe health care services.<sup>5</sup> The “first delay” in this model (delay in seeking care) may occur due to financial, cultural, educational, or other patient factors. Anecdotally, fear of bad surgical outcomes (whether true or not) and, more specifically, a fear of anesthesia may also contribute to the “first delay.” The second and third delays are more directly linked to infrastructure limitations. The “second delay” (delay in reaching care) occurs when health facilities cannot be achieved in a timely manner and may be due to geographic distance or challenges with transportation (e.g., bad road conditions, no access to a car, no money for public transport). Although efforts to improve health care infrastructure often focus on facility- based interventions, emphasis on infrastructure that minimizes this “second delay” is underappreciated despite evidence of cost- effectiveness.<sup>163</sup> For example, the lack of prehospital care systems in many LMICs is a significant source of avoidable morbidity and mortality. chapter, in Section 3: Essentials for Practice in ResourceVariable Settings.)

Equipment donations are common in LMICs; however, they often have limited long- term impact and multiple unintended consequences. Donations are frequently made with little understanding of local needs, lack of communication between donor and recipient, and absence of ongoing technical support or access to consumables. Wellintentioned donors are often unaware of WHO Guidelines for Medical Device Donation, or of the fact that purchase costs account for only 20% of total lifetime costs for a piece of equipment.<sup>179</sup> As a result, up to 30% of donated equipment may only transiently or never become operational and commonly accumulates in equipment graveyards because of a sense of obligation by recipients to not throw anything away. For example, donations of modern anesthesia machines from HICs are commonly encountered in many LMICs. Without reliable power, pressurized gas supplies, biomedical support, or access to disposables (e.g., carbon dioxide absorbent, humidity filters, circuit tubing), it is sometimes hard to argue in favor of these machines.

Reliable access to medications and blood are two additional common infrastructure challenges limiting access to safe surgery and anesthesia services worldwide. The WHO Essential Medicines List includes medications to perform general anesthesia safely, monitored anesthesia care (MAC), neuraxial, regional, and local anesthesia, and acute and chronic pain management (Box 2.3).<sup>180</sup> Despite the list’s existence for more than 40 years, critical shortages of many medications persist. A WHO survey of all health facilities in Uganda examined the availability of twelve anesthetic drugs (atracurium, bupivacaine, halothane, isoflurane, desflurane, sevoflurane, ketamine, lidocaine 2%, lidocaine 5% heavy spinal injection, midazolam, nitrous oxide, and suxamethonium) and found that only 2% of the surgical health facilities had all of these medications and only 19% had half.<sup>177</sup> The same WHO survey in the Democratic Republic of Congo found that only 33% of hospitals had bupivacaine, 21% thiopental, and 16% halothane. Factors affecting access to analgesics are discussed further in the next section, though supply chain, cold storage, and cost are common challenges limiting many drugs’ availability. Drug quality is also a factor in some LMICs where variable manufacturing quality and fraud (i.e., counterfeiting) are commonly encountered.<sup>181</sup> It is estimated that 1 in 10 medical products (e.g., medications) found in LMICs are substandard or falsified, resulting in hundreds of thousands of deaths each year.<sup>182,183</sup>

The WHO Essential Medicines List also includes blood products. Despite more than four decades of global advocacy (1975 WHA28.72) for universal access to safe blood, limited transfusion capacity significantly impacts the ability to provide safe surgery and anesthesia care, especially for rural and LMIC populations. Most rural surgical facilities are unable to provide consistent access to safe blood. Patients lack timely access to safe blood as a result of several common challenges, including inadequate blood supply, distribution networks, rural blood banking capacity, safety protocols, workforce (e.g., pathologists, laboratory technicians), testing supplies, and policy implementation, as well as high costs of blood and high prevalence of infectious diseases.<sup>127,184,185</sup> The whole blood donation rate

### Analgesia Regulation and Policy

Five billion people worldwide have little or no access to analgesia. As with the surgical disease crisis, inequities in analgesia access are partly due to the limited availability of data that quantify the problem. Unlike most traditional global health metrics that focus on extending healthy life and productivity, alleviating pain may not always target these same goals (e.g., acute surgical pain relief and palliative care), thus adding to the complexity of quantifying pain burden. Massive inequities in access to analgesia are also attributed to several additional factors, including advocacy challenges (pain is not disease- specific or easy to associate with a “face”), limited training opportunities for health care professions, fear of addiction and diversion, financing, supply chain limitations, cultural attitudes, and regulatory and legal barriers (Box 2.4).<sup>95,96</sup> In the discussion, we consider some of these barriers and potential areas for intervention by the global anesthesia community.

Limited financial resources and infrastructure hinder the ability of health care systems to deliver analgesia (i.e., effective supply chains) and limit the ability of patients to afford such care. Countries with a lower GDP or human development index have less access to and use of opiates.<sup>96</sup> Opiate prices in LMICs are often significantly higher relative to GDP. In the recent past, in Argentina and Mexico, the monthly cost of an opioid prescription for oncologic pain was reported to be more than 200% of the average monthly income.<sup>126,188</sup> In Rwanda, the reported price of injectable morphine is six times the lowest cost reported in the International Drug Price Indicator Guide.<sup>50,189</sup> Drivers of these inflated prices include import costs, taxes, licensing fees, storage, and sales restrictions, and limited public finance programs. International regulations and misconceptions about pain treatment and addiction form the basis for many of these interventions.

Misinformation about analgesia has biased knowledge and attitudes among providers, patients, and policymakers and has resulted in significant undertreatment of pain. Several studies have demonstrated that the risk of addiction associated with appropriate analgesic administration is significantly exaggerated and especially low for acute, oncologic, or end- of- life pain.<sup>190</sup> Nonetheless, widespread and excessive fears over diversion and addiction have resulted in unnecessarily onerous regulatory barriers that often confuse tolerance and dependence, users and addicts, and disproportionately affect poor and marginalized populations.<sup>191</sup> In China, for example, a domestic ketamine abuse problem has led to repeat lobbying of the International Narcotics Control Board (INCB) by the Chinese government to reschedule ketamine as a Schedule I drug for all countries. Such regulation would effectively remove the nearly ubiquitous access to ketamine in LMICs, and further exacerbate the ongoing global pain crisis.<sup>192</sup> Ketamine is inexpensive, accessible, and safe, and as a result, has become a critical resource for the provision of safe and accessible anesthesia and analgesia services worldwide. Fortunately, attempts to restrict ketamine have been promptly recognized as violating international regulatory policies.

Contemporary legal and regulatory/policy frameworks have significantly exacerbated disparities in access to analgesia care. In the wake of several key events in the 1800s that fueled skyrocketing opioid abuse, including the Opium Wars and the invention of the hypodermic syringe, the beginning of the 20th century produced several of the first national

and international efforts to restrict and regulate narcotics. This included the first international conference on narcotics in 1909 (International Opium Convention in Shanghai) and the first multilateral drug treaty in 1912 (The Hague Opium Convention). One of the most notable regulations of the 20th century is the 1961 UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs. The opening sentence of the “Single Convention” recognizes “…that the medical use of narcotic drugs continues to be indispensable for relieving pain and suffering and that adequate provision must be made to ensure the availability of narcotic drugs for such purposes….” The Single Convention set out to control the use and trade of plant- based drugs (opium, cannabis, and cocaine) while assuring medical availability.<sup>195</sup> This regulation mandated government participation (reporting national estimates of narcotic needs, meticulous record keeping), regulated drug cultivation, restricted trade to authorized international parties, and created the INCB. The INCB was tasked with monitoring UN drug convention implementation and required each country to designate an enforcement office (e.g., the Drug Enforcement Administration [DEA] in the United States). While most countries adopted this onerous regulation, most poor countries did not have the extensive resources to enact these changes. The Single Convention’s “global estimates” system has been difficult for many countries to implement. This system requires all countries to provide an estimate of opiate need (for medical purposes) to inform global production goals and limit excess opiate supplies at risk for illicit use. Accurate estimates require significant resources, and for many LMICs, the inability to provide accurate estimates translates directly to inadequate supply. For example, 2017 opiate estimates for Chad (population 14.5 million) predicted 341 g fentanyl, 249 g morphine, and 105 g codeine. Compared to estimates from Canada (population 36.3 million: 150,000 g fentanyl, 4,750,000 g morphine, 40,020,000 g codeine, and 70 other controlled substances), the disparity and gross underestimation are apparent.<sup>196</sup> By approving such gross underestimates, the INCB effectively is legally prohibiting patients from having access to opiates. In times of humanitarian crisis, the Single Convention impedes the ability to emergently augment local analgesia capacity, as became evident in the wake of the 2010 Haiti earthquake.<sup>197</sup>

Global regulations to curb illicit use have dominated efforts to ensure access to analgesics for medical purposes (or to ensure access to substance abuse treatment). In 1985, the Parliament of India passed the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act to enforce international drug policy (e.g., the Single Convention). The Act’s onerous regulations and extreme penalties (including the death penalty for certain drug- related crimes) resulted in more than a 90% decrease in the medicinal use of morphine.<sup>198,199</sup> This imbalance, referred to by some as “collateral damage from the war on drugs,” disproportionately affects LMICs and continues to cripple access to analgesics for most of the world’s population.<sup>197</sup> Such bias has also impaired the scaleup of addiction treatment initiatives. For example, addiction treatment (methadone and buprenorphine) was not added to the WHO Essential Medicines List until 2006, approximately 100 years after the passage of the earliest narcotic regulations and 40 years after the list’s creation.

The prohibitory biases of prior international drug policy have resulted in numerous national regulatory barriers that include limitations on who can prescribe, dispense, or administer opiates, as well as constraints on indications (e.g., postoperative but not cancer pain), dosages, or duration of therapy.<sup>191</sup> For example, in Armenia, only oncologists with the approval of multiple providers can prescribe outpatient opiates. In contrast, in the Ukraine, health care providers must travel to the patients’ homes to administer outpatient opiates. In Jordan, the maximum duration of an opiate prescription for oncologic pain is ten days, and only three days for all other conditions.<sup>200</sup> These myriad regulations create an additional barrier by inducing fear of prosecution for noncompliance among health professionals, thereby reducing provider willingness to prescribe. Lack of adequate training opportunities for health care providers, especially in LMICs, coupled with the occasional use of analgesics in clinical practice, create a cycle whereby fewer and fewer providers feel comfortable prescribing opiates.

Several amendments to the Single Convention have been enacted, including the 1971 Convention of Psychotropic Substances, the 1988 UN Convention Against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances, the inclusion of morphine in the first WHO Essential Medicines List in 1977, and the introduction of the WHO Pain Ladder in 1986. Despite these efforts, most of the world’s population remains without reliable access to analgesia. Nearly 60 years after the Single Convention, the number of drug abusers in many countries is near all- time highs, while 150 countries still lack reliable access to analgesia. Even the UN’s SDGs, which aim to set development priorities for the world, reflect the prohibitory bias of prior international policies. SDG 3.5 calls for improved prevention and treatment for substance abuse, including narcotics, but fails to mention pain or palliative care anywhere else in the SDGs.<sup>49</sup>

Advocacy and education efforts by the global stakeholders, including the anesthesia community, as well as rethinking of international analgesic policy (including the Single Convention) are essential for progress in addressing the global pain crisis.<sup>179,201</sup> Education for health care providers, policymakers, and the public can play a significant role in expanding access to analgesia. Such efforts should focus on pharmacologic treatment strategies and emphasize better understanding and management of causes and contributors to pain, including social and economic determinants. Efforts by numerous Ministry of Health agencies have had small successes. In Vietnam and India, grassroots- and civil- society- led policy initiatives to allay opiophobia and reduce institutional licensing regulations have significantly increased access to analgesia without an increased diversion.<sup>202–205</sup> In Uganda, advocacy efforts by the nongovernmental organization Hospice Africa Uganda helped convince the Uganda Ministry of Health in 1993 to import morphine powder and to provide domestically made morphine liquid free to all cancer or HIV/AIDS patients. In 2004, the Uganda Ministry of Health legalized opioid prescribing by nurses and clinical officers with palliative care training, significantly expanding analgesia access, especially in rural communities. ## Section 2: Evolution of Anesthesia Care Models and Challenges Around the World

### THE DEVELOPMENT OF GLOBAL ANESTHESIA PRACTICE

Though Morton is known for demonstrating the first use of contemporary inhaled anesthesia, forms of anesthesia had been practiced worldwide for many years prior. For instance, the Aztecs and Mayans were among the earliest anesthetists in Mexico and known for using herbal beverages to produce analgesia, with adjuncts such as rattlesnake fangs, leeches, porcupine needles, and the maguey needles to produce local numbness.<sup>207</sup> The Mayan witch doctors were divided into specialty groups according to the diseases they either cured or produced. The first mention is a witch doctor who produced “sleepiness” called Ah Pul Uenel, who used plants that produce hallucinations and nervous system stimulants. Also mentioned is Pul- Yahob, who treated wounds and pain with different rituals, plants, and animals. The oldest medical writings in the Middle East were found in cuneiform tablets and Eber’s papyrus in Mesopotamia and the Nile valley, describing herbal treatments like belladonna, cannabis, and _Mandragora_ . Phoenicians also obtained artificial sleep using calming herbs, opium poppy, and juniper leaves.

Following Morton’s 1846 demonstration, the use of ether anesthesia spread quickly around the world. In 1847 uses of ether anesthesia occurred in several countries, including Canada, Mexico, South Africa, Norway, Romania, India, and Australia.<sup>208–211</sup> In many other countries, the modern history of anesthesia began with war, as for the initial use

of ether in Paraguay (during the war against the Triple Alliance of Brazil, Uruguay, and Argentina, between 1864 and 1870), or modern anesthesia in Vietnam during the Vietnam War.<sup>212</sup>

Since the 19th century, the field has gradually become formalized and professionalized, away from the “chloroformists” practicing in the late 1800s in Australia or the medical students and ad hoc personnel delivering anesthesia in Colombia.<sup>213</sup> Professional anesthesia societies began to appear worldwide in the early and mid- 1900s, with physician anesthesiologists appearing on staff rolls. The World Federation of Societies of Anaesthesiologists was founded in 1956 with 26 founding members.

Since then, anesthesia practice has become continually safer. Landmark advancements have changed the face of the provision of anesthesia. These included the introduction of the Epstein–Macintosh–Oxford inhaler (EMO) for ether administration, the introduction of halogenated vapors, locoregional anesthesia with cocaine, and spinal anesthesia. Modern advances in the systematic practice of anesthesia care, including the use of the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist, a push for the universal availability of pulse oximetry and capnography, and the adoption of the International Standards for the Safe Practice of Anesthesia, have continued the endeavor to make anesthesia even safer.

Nonetheless, today, more than 170 years later, ether is still used in some LICs, and modern anesthesia is out of reach for many communities (Fig. 2.15). Though the practice of anesthesia has changed significantly, these changes disproportionately occurred in high- income countries. The practice of anesthesia varies widely between and even within countries. Of the numerous challenges and factors that have shaped this heterogeneity, many are shared by multiple countries regardless of income level. These include common challenges such as inadequate workforce (number, composition, or distribution), high cost of care, limited access for rural and poor populations, and lack of standards for practice and training.

### DEMOGRAPHICS OF COUNTRIES WHERE INCREASED ACCESS TO ANESTHESIA IS NEEDED

Almost all countries could benefit from increased access to anesthesia care. This is true in the United States, where the amount spent on health care is very high ($11,000 per person per year), or Madagascar, where the amount is very low ($20).<sup>214</sup> Anesthesia providers work at every step in between. Anesthesia is provided in well- resourced countries with high per- capita GDP; these anesthesia providers face high costs for their patients, an aging population, and long wait times for surgery. Anesthesia is provided in mixed public and private systems; providers in these dual- practice systems face low public- sector remuneration and emigration of professionals to higher- paying areas. Anesthesia is provided in some very low- income and low- resource settings; these providers face challenges with lacking basic infrastructure and equipment, a high burden of disease and mortality, and burnout.<sup>215</sup>

Public health care systems in low- and middle- income countries are most affected. Small provider numbers and scarce resources are matched with a large, financially insecure population often forced to purchase surgical supplies, analgesics, antibiotics, or intravenous fluids to obtain surgery. In regions like East Africa, a patient’s family might be asked to go to the nearby pharmacy to buy 250 mL of volatile anesthetic, propofol, suxamethonium, and paracetamol. Anesthesia providers face complex clinical situations with extremely limited resources, increasing the potential risks to safe practice and good patient outcomes.

In contrast, anesthesia providers in urban areas or wealthy countries with public systems, like Norway, face less inequity, with more resources and better access to major health services. Here the anesthesia provider has almost everything necessary for safe and high- quality practice, leading to reliably good patient outcomes.

Lastly, the private health system, while an outstanding strength in some countries, can capture the majority of health care expenditure. While this can provide timely and quality care, it may come at the expense of financial bankruptcy for some families. As in the United States, the anesthesia provider may have everything necessary for safe and quality practice but cannot offer it to everyone. This private system also includes a non- negligible amount of care by charitable or religious organizations, which typically focus on providing care for the neediest.

### PROVIDER TYPES, TRAINING, AND PRACTICE MODELS

The fundamentals of anesthesia care (the safe delivery of operative anesthesia for surgery) can be accomplished in myriad ways. Likewise, the clinicians who provide anesthesia can differ significantly from each other. As a result, in most countries, there is more than one way to become a certified anesthesia provider. In this context, “certified” refers to a provider that has undergone a nationally recognized

or accredited training program specific to anesthesia. Providers including physicians, nurses and those with other educational backgrounds may undergo certified anesthesia training. The result is a team of mixed providers who each contribute to anesthesia care in a country.

### Physicians

In nearly all countries, there is a certified pathway to becoming a specialist physician anesthesiologist, whose primary clinical task is providing anesthesia, for which they have undergone specialty training. A typical pathway is for a medical student to enter a postgraduate training program after medical school and sometimes a year of internship. These anesthesia residency programs last 3–5 years and sometimes involve the completion of a year of research, a dissertation, or a thesis. In some cases, the resulting qualification is a master’s level degree (e.g., Uganda); in others, qualification results from the passing of a certification exam (typically national, as in Canada, Mexico, or optionally in a regional exam for many European countries), or a license to practice from the Ministry of Health without examination (Colombia). Specialty training is often in the apprenticeship model, wherein a student spends time with graduated supervision in the operating room, taking on increasingly complex cases. Other countries, such as Canada and Romania, follow a competency- based model, where training is not necessarily time- based but tracked by the successful achievement of clinical competencies.<sup>216–218</sup> Competencybased training is becoming more popular worldwide,<sup>219,220</sup> with programs in the United States and India. Sometimes more than one type of training results in an anesthesia qualification. In these countries, the longest pathway typically correlates with physician specialist anesthesiologists, as they are often named.

Physician specialists typically have a broad scope of practice. This often includes preoperative optimization, intraoperative management, and recovery from anesthesia. In many places, anesthesiologists also practice critical care and acute and chronic pain management. These may practice independently once qualified, without additional supervision.

Some countries have alternate pathways for a physician to train to provide anesthesia. These pathways may involve a shorter amount of training, practice restrictions, or lack a research component (examples are “second specialist” training in Vietnam, where residents choose an academic or practical training pathway after initial “first specialist” qualification or Diploma in Anesthesia as in India and Pakistan). For example, holders of a Diploma of Anesthesia in India often practice under the supervision of senior anesthesiologists. One program in India provides six months of training to physicians post- medical school to carry out cesarean sections and lifesaving anesthetic skills for emergency obstetric care in rural facilities where no anesthesiologist exists.<sup>221,222</sup>

Some programs are intended for medical students who are not admitted to residency. For instance, in Vietnam, these students can then enter a one- year training program and, after four years of additional training, can become “first specialists” as above. In Mexico, students who do not pass exams to be admitted to residency can be enrolled in rural training programs up to 2 years in length and are eligible to

<!-- PDF page 27 -->

write a national certification exam, entitling them to practice in rural areas only. These programs have been controversial. Rural access is sometimes extended by general practitioners who complete formal training in anesthesia. As in Canada and Australia, these general practitioner (GP) anesthetists might not practice anesthesia full- time but practice independently, mainly in rural areas. They may frequently limit their cases to healthier patients or more routine operations.<sup>223,224</sup> In South Africa, GPs can obtain a Diploma in Anesthesia after completing a fully supervised training program at an accredited institution.

### Non- physicians and Nurses

**Nurse anesthetists.** The training and scope of practice for nurses are variable but are most commonly called nurse anesthetists. Training is usually at least one year but may be up to four years, and nurses are frequently required to have some work experience before entering. Curricula may be well defined, as in the United States, Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetists (known as CRNAs) undergo a training program up to 42 months after having worked as an intensive care nurse, requiring at least 465 hours of classroom education, exposure to a minimum of 600 clinical cases, and 2000 clinical hours, with a move toward conferring a Doctor of Nursing Practice on graduation.<sup>225</sup>

The scope of practice of nurse anesthetists is broad and encompasses the breadth of anesthesia. This includes preoperative evaluation, intraoperative induction, maintenance, emergence, and immediate postoperative care. Non- physician providers like CRNAs more frequently practice in rural areas.<sup>226,227</sup> Nineteen US states have opted out of the requirement for physician supervision for CRNAs, while in other states, CRNAs must practice under indirect supervision with a physician available.<sup>228</sup> Similar variability in supervision is seen in health systems around the world: nurse anesthetists may be fully autonomous, work alongside physicians (with physicians available to consult on patients), or work with physicians under indirect supervision.

**Non- nurse, Non- physicians.** Non- physician anesthesia cadres also come from backgrounds other than nursing. The nomenclature for these providers is less standardized, but the term “anesthesia assistants” is frequently used. In many African countries, “clinical officers” or “anesthetic officers” practice. They may occasionally enter training from a nursing background, and training typically may be two years before independent practice, but this is highly variable. In Africa alone, there are more than 30 different distinct training pathways to becoming an anesthesia provider.<sup>229</sup> In other countries, a higher- skilled group of anesthesia providers may hold a Bachelor of Science in Anesthesia. In Uganda, these providers complete two years of medical school, after which they undergo clinical anesthetic training.

Unlike nurse anesthetists, where typically the scope of practice reliably includes induction, maintenance, and emergence from anesthesia, the scope of practice of nonnurse providers varies considerably. Anesthetic officers, or the equivalent, are the primary anesthesia providers in many African countries (as there are many fewer physician anesthesia providers), as are Anesthetic Scientific Officers in Papua New Guinea.<sup>230a</sup> As such, they provide anesthetics independently, and there is no physician on staff in many

facilities. Certified Anesthesiologist Assistants in the United States offer full operative care under a physician’s direct or indirect supervision. In contrast, Anesthesia Assistants in Canada provide monitored anesthesia care and a variety of procedures (including intubation in some cases).

**Anesthesia Technicians.** Various technical providers contribute to the care of patients undergoing anesthesia. Although these assistants and technicians do not provide operative anesthesia, they contribute to the smooth functioning of the anesthesia care team and high- quality care in many jurisdictions. In the United States, anesthesia technicians are a common part of anesthesia teams, helping to check and prepare equipment for the anesthesia provider without clinical care provision. Other examples would be Operating Department Practitioners in the United Kingdom or Operation Theatre Technologists (OTTs) in India. They assist the anesthesiologist with equipment and medications, with limited involvement in clinical care, such as placing intravenous cannulas.

### Practice Models

The provider types described above work in every combination to provide anesthesia services for a health system, sometimes fluidly exchanging roles, emphasizing that the provision of anesthesia is necessarily provided by a team. Some countries have a “physician- only” model but make use of other types of physicians to extend anesthesia services, as described above in Canada, Australia, India, Pakistan, and South Africa. In countries with predominantly specialist anesthesiologists, like the UK and Norway, trainee physicians fill an important role in service provision; anesthesia technicians and assistants contribute in essential ways to safe care and are sometimes mandatory presences at critical parts of the case.

In countries that have incorporated non- physician anesthesia providers into operative care through nurse anesthetists or other non- physicians, non- nurse anesthesia providers also work in many models. Nurse anesthetists may practice with indirect physician supervision (as in Norway, Vietnam, or some parts of the US) or independently (as in other parts of the US). Non- physician anesthesia providers administer most of the anesthesia in places where the workforce shortage is the worst, such as Fiji, Paraguay (under surgeon supervision), or many African countries. Despite practicing independently, variations in training and supervision lead to vast differences in quality of care. In Latin America, despite variable practice models, the Confederación Latinaoamericana de Sociedades de Anestesiología (CLASA) promulgates anesthesia must be provided by Anesthesiologists only, citing “anesthesia is a strictly medical act.<sup>230b</sup>

Complicating accurate descriptions of anesthesia teams are discrepancies between what providers are regulated to do and what they do in practice. For example, anesthesia technicians in multiple countries are know to perform intubation or spinals despite being prohibited by license or laws. In South Korea, CRNAs have been in practice for >50 years and are nationally recognized providers, yet their legal ability to practice anesthesia has been cast into doubt. These ambiguities make it difficult to describe the global anesthesia workforce and thus engage in health systems planning for anesthesia care.

### GLOBAL AND REGIONAL CHALLENGES

The challenges countries face in delivering anesthesia globally have some recurring themes. As discussed earlier in this chapter, there is a significant overall global deficit of anesthesia providers. Additionally, access to anesthesia care is compounded by geographic maldistribution within countries. Physicians, including anesthesiologists, tend to cluster in urban areas, and ensuring access for patients in rural areas is challenging in both high- and low- resource countries.<sup>147</sup> For example, 70% of the anesthesiologists within a 300- mile radius of Mexico City work in the urban area. One study of 1169 rural counties in the United States found greater than half had no anesthesia provider,<sup>231</sup> and another showed that nearly one- third of the United States pediatric population lives more than 50 miles from the nearest pediatric anesthesiologist.<sup>232</sup> This can exacerbate existing disparities within countries, such as how Black and Hispanic women are less likely to receive epidural analgesia or increases in postoperative mortality rates among Indigenous Canadians.<sup>233</sup> The use of short- term contracts to staff rural areas is a common but unstable staffing model, and relying on additional financial incentives is common worldwide.

Salary considerations compound workforce deficits. Despite being a highly remunerated specialty in the United States, anesthesiologists consistently seek opportunities for dual practice (public and private) where they exist to augment income.<sup>234,235</sup> This often leads to a loss of providers to better- resourced and better- paying areas: providers from Africa and South Asia leave to the Middle East and Europe, and providers from eastern Europe like Romania leave for western Europe.<sup>160,236</sup>

Heterogeneity in access to subspecialty anesthesia, drugs, and modern equipment is a pervasive concern, even in countries where access is reliable in urban areas.<sup>237,238</sup> Access to common drugs like fentanyl or amiodarone is inconsistent in countries like Romania, India, Pakistan, and Vietnam. At the same time, some hospitals in China lack gas analyzers or defibrillators. In hospitals in Africa, anesthesia is commonly provided in facilities without reliable oxygen or electricity.

The lack of consistent standards in training and certification contributes to variations in quality. Different cadres of anesthesia providers within the same country may be held to further training and certification standards but provide the same care. Cadres with the same name and job description may have additional education requirements between the two countries. International standards like the WHO Safe Surgery Checklist and the WHO-WFSA International Standards for a Safe Practice of Anesthesia have helped to establish a minimum threshold for practice. Still, adoption is not universal, and standards to address variation between cadres and countries have yet to emerge.<sup>146</sup>

## Section 3: Essentials for Practice in Resource- Variable Settings

### CLINICAL AND TECHNICAL SKILLS

The practice of anesthesia anywhere in the world heavily depends on four factors: personnel, equipment, medications, and patients. Disruption, change, or lack of any

of these factors may limit the safe practice of anesthesia. Whether practicing in a rural HIC setting, an LMIC, a military or humanitarian field hospital, or a well- resourced environment during a time of exceptional surgery (e.g., SARS- CoV- 2 pandemic), all these factors will vary. Data to guide clinical practice during resource constraints are limited and may be interpreted differently.

Clinical practice in these settings may be highly different from what one may be accustomed to and often diverges from “standard care” due to local challenges related to personnel, equipment, medication, and patients. Numerous instances of well- intentioned, evidence- based practices from HICs produce unintended negative consequences in LMICs.<sup>239</sup> A “copy- and- paste” framework from HICs to LMICs may not necessarily improve outcomes.<sup>239,240</sup> Therefore, it is essential to understand the local context before caring for patients in these settings or attempting to change local practice. The SARS- CoV- 2 pandemic highlighted that no setting is immune to resource constraints and that all providers should develop skills for practice with limited resources. This section will briefly discuss selected aspects of anesthesia practice in resource- variable settings through the lens of these four factors.

### Personnel

Limitations in the anesthesia workforce are among the most significant barriers to the safe provision of anesthesia care worldwide. Beyond low absolute numbers of providers,<sup>241</sup> the competencies of existing anesthetic providers may be highly variable. Many practitioners who provide anesthesia in resource- constrained settings may have completed inadequate training and often work in isolation with negligible supervision or colleague support.<sup>242</sup> Many are highly skilled and competent within a narrow spectrum of clinical cases. They often learn to deliver a “recipe” type anesthetic that may not be adaptable or appropriate for more complex cases.

When working in resource- constrained environments, it is essential to understand the level of knowledge and experience of the local anesthetic provider. Regardless of training level, there are always opportunities for mutually beneficial bidirectional learning. The role of the anesthetic provider in many resource- constrained settings is often limited to the operating theater, as the concept of perioperative care is underdeveloped and not part of training. Personnel shortages routinely limit the capacity for adequate preoperative evaluation and optimization of patients. Perioperative nursing may be entirely lacking, and routine elements of postoperative care, such as monitoring or providing regular pain assessments with analgesic administration, may not be available. The postanesthesia care unit (PACU) may consist of an unstaffed corridor or a small designated space near the operating room complex exit. In this setting, it is paramount to ensure patients are more fully recovered from their anesthetic when they arrive in the recovery area than may be routine in well- resourced PACUs. Thoughtful planning is required, especially if higher- level care is needed postoperatively (e.g., ventilation), as this usually will become the responsibility of the anesthesia provider or even family members, who are frequently recruited to help with monitoring or even manual ventilation.

**Anesthesia Machines.** Most modern anesthetic workstations cannot function without pressurized gases and electricity, both of which may be in short supply in many resource- constrained environments. In these settings, familiarity with drawover anesthesia may be essential. An in- depth discussion of drawover anesthesia is beyond the scope of this chapter. However, knowledge of drawover anesthesia is highly relevant in many practice settings, and several comprehensive resources for learning drawover are included at the end of this chapter. Here we provide a brief overview of the basics of drawover anesthesia. An inhalational anesthesia system must be able to deliver accurate concentrations of volatile agents, avoid rebreathing of carbon dioxide, have a provision for intermittent positive- pressure ventilation (IPPV), and provide oxygen- enriched gases. **Monitors.** Many operating theaters worldwide have minimal monitoring options despite the international standards set out by the WHO and WFSA.<sup>3</sup> In this environment, there is increased reliance on clinical skills (e.g., change in palpable pulse volume, degree of chest expansion, pupil size, and change in compliance of bellows, self- inflating bag, or bag on Ayre’s T- piece). Because electricity may be unreliable, ideally, monitors should have a functional internal battery. Pulse oximetry is invaluable but not universally available. Some inexpensive pulse oximeters meet international quality standards; however, few are designed for harsh environments, many are highly inaccurate, and some may not work as well, especially in patients with dark skin pigment.<sup>250,251</sup> Capnography and ECG monitoring are often unavailable in low- resource settings.<sup>252</sup> Precordial stethoscopes are particularly useful and can be made from

an ordinary stethoscope with an extension (e.g., a suction tube or oxygen tubing). Precordial stethoscopes provide an excellent beat- to- beat heart rate monitor and continuous, audible verification of respiratory rate, airway patency, and endotracheal tube placement. Hypovolemia may be detectable by a decrease in heartbeat tone that is apparent before alarms on other monitors.

### Airway Equipment and Airway Management Tech-

**niques.** In resource- constrained settings, the range of available airway equipment may be extremely limited. Face masks, oro- and nasopharyngeal airways, and endotracheal tubes may be available but not in all sizes. Many hospitals providing surgical care will have only a Macintosh size three laryngoscope blade, and rarely additional equipment such as a bougie or supraglottic airways. When managing a difficult airway in these settings, safety is critical and it is often essential to maintain spontaneous respiration and oxygenation until the airway has been secured. Face mask ventilation, regional anesthesia, or ketamine- based anesthetics may be safer than attempting complex airway management. An awake look with direct laryngoscopy can often assist in airway management planning. In some cases, it may be preferable to opt for a tracheostomy under local anesthesia as the surgical skills may be superior to what is feasible with the limited anesthetic equipment and ability.

As advanced airway equipment and experienced assistance are likely to be lacking, excellent technique and positioning for laryngoscopy are crucial for success. Retrograde intubation and blind nasal intubation can be valuable skills to master. Airway topicalization can be accomplished in many ways, including transtracheal lidocaine injection or by gargling or spraying water- based lubricant jelly, lidocaine, and epinephrine. For blind nasal intubation, the tube is pushed forward during inspiration following the floor of the nasal cavity, listening to the noise of breathing (a similar technique to a Patil guide). If the tube enters the esophagus, the neck should be extended, and if it impinges on the anterior part of the larynx, the neck should be flexed. Lifting the jaw will stop the endotracheal tube from hitting the epiglottis. Rotating the tube or inflating the cuff slightly may also assist with entering the trachea. If capnography is not available, then condensation in the tube, movement of the bag with respiration, auscultation of the chest, and in rare circumstances, an esophageal bulb can help verify correct placement. In LMICs, airway equipment is often washed and reused. There are several products that can be used to decontaminate endotracheal tubes, supraglottic airways easily, and other airway adjuncts; however, access is highly variable. When processing and reusing such equipment, damage to the cuff and increasing stiffness of the plastic are significant challenges.

Endotracheal tube stylets are infrequently available. Improvised endotracheal tube introducers have been crafted by carefully inserting a piece of wire into an appropriately sized suction catheter, ensuring the metal tip is not protruding. Learning safe, sensible techniques that are reproducible without the use of expensive, specialized equipment is fundamental. Bulb suction (for neonates) and foot pedal suction devices are invaluable when traditional electric suction is unavailable.

**Equipment for Regional Anesthesia.** Spinal anesthesia is universally taught and used in many settings for a wider variety of cases than is commonly done in HICs (e.g., appendectomy). The needles vary in quality, and blunt tips can be overcome by using a 21- gauge hypodermic needle as an introducer. The most commonly used local anesthetic is hyperbaric 0.5% bupivacaine. All anesthetic providers are taught to do spinals, but not all are taught how to manage spinal- induced hypotension and complications of neuraxial anesthesia. Teaching how to fabricate a simple wedge for a uterine tilt with pregnant patients or learning to reposition patients in varying degrees of reverse Trendelenburg position may prevent untoward incidents, especially where an adjustable operating table may not exist. Although a spinal might seem an easier and safer option, appropriate patient selection is critical, especially where preferred vasopressors and fluids may be in short supply. In that situation, 0.5 mg epinephrine has been diluted in a 500 mL bottle of 5% dextrose and administered cautiously to treat the hypotension. Epidurals are rarely available in low- resource settings for several reasons: providers may not be proficient with the technique; epidural needles, preservative- free local anesthetic drugs, and infusion pumps are not readily available; and more importantly, the staffing may be inadequate, and the knowledge may be insufficient to safely manage a patient with an epidural in the postoperative setting. In some settings where electronic drug infusion pumps are unavailable, low- cost elastomeric bulb pumps can be used to deliver a fixed rate of medication. Caudal blocks using a 22- G cannula are commonly used in children for postoperative pain management, although the only safe preparation of local anesthetic may be bupivacaine for intrathecal use. Landmark techniques are often used for regional anesthesia, as ultrasound machines and nerve stimulators are rarely available. Commonly performed blocks include ankle, digital, axillary, penile, wrist, fascia iliaca, femoral, and rectus sheath blocks. Local field blocks may be used for various procedures, including hernia repairs, often done under local anesthesia in the outpatient setting. Where ultrasounds are available, other regional supplies such as ultrasound gel, probe covers, echogenic needles, and appropriate local anesthetics may be lacking. Several workarounds to these common challenges have been well- described. Although many blocks can be easily and safely done without ultrasound or nerve stimulation, serious complications can still occur. Anecdotal reports of local anesthetic toxicity fatalities during penile blocks for circumcision are still encountered in some LICs and attributed to preventable causes such as incorrect dosing or technique.<sup>253</sup> In general, peripherally distal blocks with lower volume and shorteracting local anesthetics may be safer options, especially where safety equipment such as intralipid may be limited.

**Equipment for Venous Access.** Intravenous cannulas are almost universally available, but variable quality may limit use. Needles may be blunt or extend quite a few millimeters beyond the tip of the cannula. Scalp veins are a common site for insertion in critically ill infants. With appropriate training, rapid placement of a cannula in the right internal jugular vein or the peritoneal cavity can be lifesaving in a child dying from hypovolemic shock (e.g., cholera) as you look for a more permanent solution. Intraosseous needles

are not easily found, but 18- G or 16- G needles can be used in the tibia of an infant. Butterfly needles, if available, are handy because of their limited length. Twisting is essential to pierce the cortex until a sudden give when entering the medullary cavity. It is feasible to perform anesthesia for an entire operation with such access (Fig. 2.19).

Infusion pumps are often unavailable in LMICs and, when present, are frequently limited by the availability of compatible disposables (i.e., appropriately sized syringes or proprietary tubing). It is essential not only to be proficient with calculating infusion rates based on drop factor (drops per mL) but also to be aware that infusion sets vary in size and quality, often making accurate calibration challenging.

### Drugs

**Volatile Agents.** Ether has now been removed from the WHO Essential Medicines List, and its use has reduced dramatically since 2013. Isoflurane, halothane and most recently sevoflurane (added in 2023) are included in the WHO Essential Medicines List, and due to the price differential (e.g., in Uganda, 250 mL of halothane ∼$21; 250 mL of isoflurane ∼$39; 250 mL of sevoflurane ∼$250), halothane is the most commonly used agent in many rural or nonteaching hospitals in LICs. Halothane is potent (MAC 0.8%) and sweet smelling, but the blood gas partition coefficient is 2.4, making the onset and offset of anesthesia slower than other agents. This can be useful if managing a difficult airway and intubation under halothane alone. Halothane is a cardiac- depressant and arrhythmogenic, especially in the presence of elevated end- tidal carbon dioxide. It is helpful for inhalational induction, but it is essential to rapidly reduce the inspired concentration once intravenous access is secured. Nitrous oxide may be obtainable in some locations (primarily urban areas) but can be extremely expensive. Because medical- grade air (FiO2 0.21) is often 4 to 5 times more

**Induction Agents.** The use of propofol is widespread, but ketamine (in addition to volatile agents) remains the most ubiquitous induction agent in many LMICs. Thiopentone (thiopental) is also frequently encountered outside of HICs. Ketamine resembles an intravenous preparation of ether (sympathomimetic, does not depress airway reflexes, causes excessive salivation, and has excellent analgesic properties). S- ketamine is a valuable adjunct for regional anesthesia. Ketamine can be used as an infusion (intravenous or subcutaneous injection) postoperatively for pain management, especially if opioids are in short supply.<sup>254</sup> Ketamine can also be used for total intravenous anesthesia for a variety of procedures (e.g., laparotomies). Still, the wakeup can be very prolonged, and the patient may experience disturbing hallucinations as benzodiazepines are often not used (providers more likely to encounter diazepam than midazolam in resource- constrained settings). Ketamine is the “go- to” induction agent for any patient in hypovolemic or septic shock, but it can cause cardiac arrest in maximally sympathetically stimulated patients. Ketamine may be advised against as the sole anesthetic for pregnant patients undergoing non- obstetric procedures due to its potential oxytocic effects. Ketamine can also cause apnea in neonates, despite the appearance of the neonate moving in response to surgery. Special attention and caution must be emphasized in these patient populations. Because of ketamine’s overall safety profile, it has been utilized as the agent of choice for anesthesia in some of the most austere environments.<sup>255</sup> The drug has been used as the basis for a 5- day course to teach mid- level nonanesthesia providers to administer emergency anesthesia in rural sub- Saharan Africa when no anesthesia provider is available. This program has generated significant controversy and highlights many challenges facing the provision of anesthesia in LMICs.<sup>256,257</sup>

**Analgesics.** Morphine is often the most readily available opioid, although for many LMICs, it is frequently challenging to obtain. In some countries where palliative care is more developed (e.g., Uganda), oral formulation is often available for postoperative analgesia. Pethidine (meperidine) is commonly encountered in LMICs and is used routinely for perioperative analgesia. Of note, pethidine is not included in the WHO Essential Medicines List and is not recommended for routine acute postoperative pain because of its variable efficacy and side effects. Nonetheless, pethidine may be the only option for analgesia in some LMICs, and general familiarity with its pharmacology could be highly useful in select settings (see Chapter 22). As discussed earlier in this chapter, opiophobia and access limitations significantly restrict the use of opiates. As a result, if available, opiates are rarely prescribed beyond 48 hours postoperatively in many resource- constrained settings. Many analgesics (e.g., ketamine, morphine, and pethidine) are frequently given by intramuscular injection, which may have benefits in terms of feasibility and safety, although supporting data are limited.<sup>254</sup> Patient- controlled analgesia is generally unavailable, but in some settings has been substituted by using a small subcutaneous cannula, often placed over the deltoid muscle, used solely for parenteral opiate analgesics.

This avoids repeated intramuscular injections, especially in children, and can remain in place for 48 to 72 hours. Tramadol is pervasive, inexpensive, and often used for perioperative pain management in resource- limited settings. The use of tramadol requires an understanding of its limitations, including side effects, abuse potential, and inferiority in many instances to opioid analgesia.

Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is widely available as tablets, suppositories, syrups, and an intravenous preparation, as are nonsteroidal anti- inflammatory drugs. Parenteral preparations vary from country to country, as does the potency depending on the country of manufacture. Diclofenac is commonly found and intramuscularly injected. One of the significant challenges with prescribing analgesics is conveying the concept of multimodal analgesia. Although the full spectrum of analgesic modalities may not be available, multiple analgesic options, including regional anesthesia, are often feasible in low- resource settings.

**Muscle Relaxants.** Suxamethonium (succinylcholine) is a universally available muscle relaxant and may be the safest paralytic to use if there are no means for mechanical ventilation or availability for reversal. It rarely causes malignant hyperpyrexia, although it can induce masseter spasm, especially in children with neurologic or muscular disorders. Both atracurium and suxamethonium lose potency with exposure to heat, and maintaining cold storage is a significant challenge in many settings. For cases where surgeons prefer paralysis for closure (e.g., laparotomy), a small dose of suxamethonium can be administered at the end of the case to facilitate closure of the peritoneum and rectus while hand ventilating the patient and allowing faster offset of the volatile agent. The availability of nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocking agents is highly variable, and providers should be prepared to use drugs they may not readily use in HICs.

Lack of access to transfusion services (as discussed in Section 1: Anesthesia and “Global Health Equity”) is another commonly encountered problem that may require improvisation. Providers in resource- constrained settings may be tasked with ABO typing (e.g., EldonCard) and crossmatching and should be familiar with these techniques. Whole blood transfusion may be more common than component therapy, although there are several notable limitations (including the potential loss of platelet and clotting factor activity with refrigeration). Absolute transfusion thresholds are lacking but can create significant dilemmas. For example, is it safe to perform a hernia with a hemoglobin of 9, 8, 7, or lower if no blood products are available? Direct donation from patients or relatives and mandatory exchange (i.e., someone must donate for units to be released from the blood bank) is widely practiced in LMICs. Other practices such as preoperative normovolemic hemodilution and makeshift intraoperative blood salvaging (e.g., by using a cup, gauze filter, anticoagulant, and a 60 mL syringe) have been reported. Still, there is no safe, adequate substitute for transfusion services in many settings.<sup>258,259</sup>

hemorrhage), and pediatric patients. In many LMICs, more than 50% of the population is under 18, and most have fewer comorbidities and less polypharmacy than in HICs. On the other hand, patients often present late with advanced pathology. This may be due to a wide range of factors affecting access to care, including physical access to the health unit (impassable roads in the rainy season or insecurity on the roads), or they have been consulting the local healer or bone setter and have not seen any improvement, or they are financially destitute. All health care is economically and socially draining.

As relatives often provide most of the in- hospital care (e.g., cooking the food and washing the bedding and clothes), an inpatient stay is highly time- consuming. No family will want their patient to be in the hospital longer than necessary. Patients will mobilize more rapidly but may also be less compliant with postoperative instructions. Although patients may have a lower incidence of chronic cardiac and respiratory comorbidities, many may be anemic, malnourished, or harboring low- grade malaria or helminth infections. If major surgery is being considered, existing maladies may need to be treated first and can prolong the inpatient stay. Multiple preoperative investigations may not be affordable or feasible, and many decisions are made solely on clinical judgment.

Anesthesia providers practicing in LMICs must be familiar with perioperative optimization of commonly encountered conditions. Treatment for universally common conditions such as hypertension or diabetes may require contextspecific approaches and insights (e.g., where and how to provide temperature- stable, non- counterfeit medications).

The practice of anesthesia in the resource- limited setting is shackled by many issues, and it can be challenging to administer a high level of care as advocated by many professional entities. Anesthesia safety is an essential component of care. Are there standards of care that are immutable across the globe? Suppose a full- term pregnant woman is admitted to hospital with a severe antepartum hemorrhage that requires an emergency cesarean section to save the mother and baby. What is the anesthetic of choice? Is it local anesthetic infiltration alone, intermittent aliquots of intravenous ketamine and oxygen via nasal cannula, or rapid sequence induction with ketamine, suxamethonium, and halothane? Then add to that decision the reality that the anesthetic provider may be a nurse with one year of training in anesthesia, only 250 mL of compatible blood is available in the hospital, and there is no pediatrician to resuscitate the baby. What is the safest anesthetic technique? It is difficult to answer that question, but for anyone planning to work in an LMIC as an anesthetic provider, it is indispensable to learn about the local resources, to listen to the local providers, to have the humility not to have all the answers, but above all to focus on the care and safety of the patient.

### GLOBAL HEALTH COMPETENCIES

Although the number of global health opportunities for medical trainees has skyrocketed in recent years, most are short- term, elective- based rotations, and relatively few programs offer structured training in global health competencies.<sup>260</sup>

Many anesthesia providers may not be directly involved with formal global health initiatives or careers that focus on underserved populations; however, fundamental global health competencies are relevant to all anesthesia providers (whether in high- , middle- , or low- income countries). A broad understanding of key concepts is universally pertinent and should be incorporated into anesthesia training for all providers. These include access and affordability, local and global disease burden, structural and social determinants of health, medical ethics, health equity, and social justice. The Consortium of Universities for Global Health (CUGH) has identified 11 competency domains, with four levels of competency per domain (ranging from level I for all providers with any relation to global health to level IV for providers planning to have significant lifelong engagement in global health)<sup>261</sup> (Box 2.5). Multiple efforts are underway to develop new competency frameworks with increased input from diverse stakeholders and relevance across settings to reflect the true nature of global health equity.<sup>262</sup>

For anesthesia providers who intend to spend a portion of their career focused on global health, additional training should be considered in topics such as epidemiology, statistics, qualitative research, health policy, health systems, health economics, medical anthropology, demography, ethics, and implementation and management sciences, among others.

Providers from relatively well- resourced settings benefit from preparation before working in resource- constrained settings, even if only for short- term visits.<sup>263</sup> Several nonclinical challenges and pitfalls are routinely encountered in resource- constrained settings, including failure to provide context- appropriate interventions (see discussion on equipment donations in the section on infrastructure challenges), failure to have a follow- up plan, failure to adequately engage local stakeholders, overemphasizing shortterm solutions, and draining local resources.<sup>264,265</sup> Ethical challenges are also commonly encountered and often relate to the appropriate scope of practice, the equitable distribution of local and external resources, and an inadequate understanding of local social and cultural norms.

In a survey of surgery and anesthesia trainees at a large Ugandan university with numerous visiting international collaborators, most trainees (75%) agreed that visiting groups improve their training. However, 40% of trainees reported that international groups had a neutral or negative impact on patient care. Only 15% felt that research projects conducted by international groups were in local priority areas, and approximately one- third of trainees (31%) reported discomfort with the ethics of clinical decisions made by visiting faculty.<sup>266</sup>

It is incumbent upon institutions that offer global health opportunities to provide formal training. Numerous resources exist to help equip trainees, providers, and institutions with the knowledge necessary to manage these challenges and maximize the positive impact of global health efforts (see Appendix 2.1 for links and valuable resources).<sup>267–270</sup>

In some countries (e.g., the United Kingdom), anesthesia trainees can pursue 6- month to 1- year- long global health experiences with formal curriculum and relatively long- term clinical, research, and educational components. Trainees are required to complete standardized assessments, including clinical evaluations and case- based discussions, as outlined by the RCoA.<sup>271</sup> A relatively small yet increasing number of anesthesia programs provide formal global health tracks for interested residents, formal anesthesia global health fellowships, and integrating global equity training into core anesthesia training.<sup>272,273</sup> Academic institutions and the global anesthesia community at large must continue to expand training and career development opportunities that increase the number of anesthesiologists interested in and capable of improving equitable access to safe anesthesia worldwide.

## Conclusions

Despite dramatic changes in anesthesia worldwide over the past century, safety, access, and affordability improvements have not been universal. Currently, most of the world does not have access to safe anesthesia, surgical, or analgesia services, this includes poor or marginalized communities in all countries. The massive and growing surgical and pain crises disproportionately affect LMICs, where severe shortages of workforce and infrastructure and impractical international regulations limit access to care. Despite many common misperceptions, anesthesia, analgesia, and surgical services are feasible in resource- constrained settings and are as cost- effective as many other public health interventions (e.g., vaccinations).

The neglect of pain and surgical disease by the global health community has resulted in one of the most inequitable global public health crises the world faces today. It has been only in recent years that the surgery, anesthesia, and global health communities have accelerated investment in research, education, and advocacy initiatives that aim to improve access to safe anesthesia and surgical services for the world’s poor. These efforts are in their infancy and must be expanded and supported by the global anesthesia community. Factors affecting access to care, safety, and cost are relevant in all countries regardless of income level. All anesthesia providers should maintain awareness

of the fundamental challenges facing the global anesthesia community, hoping that increasing numbers will devote their careers to addressing issues of global health equity. There are many ways anesthesia providers can engage in global health at the patient level or system level, including research, advocacy, education, and clinical service.

The role of anesthesia in global health is easy for many to overlook. Still, the global anesthesia community must quickly increase efforts to actively lead global health initiatives that aim to improve infrastructure, expand the workforce, increase translation of data into policy and practice, improve financial risk protection mechanisms for surgical patients, expand referral and prehospital systems, provide essential medicines, and ultimately improve access to safe and affordable anesthesia, surgical, and pain services worldwide.
