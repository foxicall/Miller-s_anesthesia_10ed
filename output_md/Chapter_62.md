## Introduction

### EPIDEMIOLOGY

Death and disability caused by injuries significantly threaten public health worldwide. For children and adults younger than 45, traumatic injuries remain the leading cause of death in the United States (US) and the fourth most common cause of death overall, all ages inclusive.<sup>1,2</sup> Injury- associated deaths have substantial economic consequences; the total estimated lifetime medical and work- lost costs associated with fatal injuries have been reported to be over $240 billion and are projected to increase.<sup>1,3</sup>

### MODERN TRAUMA SYSTEMS AND REGIONALIZATION

Following the landmark National Academy of Sciences report “Accidental Death and Disability: The Neglected Disease of Modern Society” in 1966,<sup>4</sup> a framework for trauma systems was developed by the American College of

Surgeons Committee on Trauma.<sup>5,6</sup> Composed of governmentally designated or internally validated trauma centers, trauma systems represent a coordinated, organized, and patient- centric approach to the injured patient’s care.<sup>7,8</sup> A distinction between trauma center _designation_ and _verification_ is made to identify the types of resources available and the number of patients treated annually. Trauma center designation is a process outlined and developed at a state or local level. Trauma center verification is an evaluation process done by the American College of Surgeons (ACS) to evaluate and improve trauma care; the verification process is voluntary and identifies the presence of resources considered to be required for the optimal care of the injured patient.<sup>5,9,10</sup> Trauma center levels range from Level I (24- hour in- house coverage, referral resource for communities in nearby regions, leadership in the prevention, research, etc.) to Level V (basic emergency department [ED] facilities to implement advanced trauma life support, after- hours activation protocols, limited surgery, and critical care).<sup>9,10</sup> Level I and Level II centers represent tertiary care centers; the standards for the provision of clinical care

### THE ROLE OF THE ANESTHESIOLOGIST

Anesthesiologists are subject matter experts regarding all aspects of resuscitation. They are uniquely trained to provide advanced care for the entire spectrum of traumatic injuries, from advanced airway management to damage control resuscitation, pain management, and critical care. At all levels of trauma care, anesthesiologists are uniquely juxtaposed with the multidisciplinary trauma team, serving an administrative role in preparing the operating room (OR), allocating resources for resuscitation, and providing direct patient care through definitive airway management and advanced resuscitation.<sup>23</sup> Anesthesiologists also play a significant role as intensivists and pain management experts. Moreover, trauma patients represent a substantial proportion of all OR cases handled during night and weekend shifts.<sup>24</sup> Regrettably, very few anesthesiologists in the United States consider trauma their primary specialty. This is distinct from European practice, where it is not unusual to find an anesthesiologist working in the prehospital environment, as a trauma team leader, or even as a director of an emergency department.<sup>25,26</sup> The US model, in which nearly all anesthesiologists treat trauma patients—but few do so exclusively—has led to a relative deficiency of research, publication, and education in this field.<sup>24,27</sup> This situation is unfortunate because trauma is an ever- evolving field of study that presents unique challenges to the clinician and one in which improvements in care can dramatically impact society.

## Prioritizing Trauma Care

### PREHOSPITAL TRIAGE

Ensuring that injured patients are transported to the correct level of trauma care can improve patient outcomes, but the process is sometimes fraught with difficulty. Prehospital estimation of blood loss is imprecise, and classically taught shock classifications are commonly confounded by extremes of age and variations in physiologic reserve.<sup>28</sup> In 2011, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), along with the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), collaborated with the American College of Surgeons’ Committee on Trauma (ACS- COT) to revise previous field triage decision schemes to reduce overtriage of patients with non- life- threatening injuries, and to help direct patients in most need of lifesaving interventions to appropriate trauma centers.<sup>29</sup> Following feedback from emergency medical service (EMS) clinicians and trauma physicians, the guideline was updated and simplified in 2022 to align better with how assessments occur in the

field to make more accurate judgments about the appropriate level of care required for an injured patient.<sup>10</sup> Current US guidelines recommend color- coded criteria for the field triage of trauma patients (Fig. 62.1).

Briefly summarized, patients meeting any injury patterns and/or age- adjusted mental status and vital sign thresholds (i.e., red criteria) are considered at high risk for severe injury. They should be transported to the highest- level trauma center available within the geographic constraints of the trauma system.<sup>10</sup> Patients at moderate risk for serious injury (i.e., yellow criteria) should be transported to the next available level of trauma care within the geographical constraints of the traumatic system.<sup>10</sup>

### BLUNT VERSUS PENETRATING TRAUMA

Traditionally, the mechanism of traumatic injury has been referred to as _blunt_ versus _penetrating_ with no further delineation of how much energy was imparted or information regarding anatomic and physiologic insults. Some studies have suggested that the mechanism of injury alone is a poor predictor for trauma center referral.<sup>30,31</sup> Others have demonstrated that distinct mechanisms, such as ejection from a vehicle or prolonged extrication time, clearly warrant trauma center team activation.<sup>32,33</sup> Additional studies have justified the mechanism of injury as a parameter that helps reduce inappropriate transport of patients with major trauma to nontrauma centers.<sup>34,35</sup>

Blunt and penetrating injuries are regularly disparate in presentation but may share similarities in the extent of the injury.<sup>23</sup> Penetrating injuries are identified as ballistic and non- ballistic. The point of injury in the patient with penetrating trauma may be utterly discernible—even to the inexpert provider—but the extent of tissue damage and depth of shock may be less detectible compared to the patient suffering from a blunt traumatic injury. Conversely, the patient with penetrating trauma will lose blood volume externally together with loss into body cavities. In contrast, the patient with blunt trauma may present in hemorrhagic shock with no apparent signs of hemorrhage. Multiple blunt traumatic insults, bleeding into compartments (e.g., unstable long bone fractures), retroperitoneal hemorrhage (e.g., pelvic fractures, major vascular injury, solid organ damage), and bleeding into other body cavities may present as indolent hemorrhagic shock.<sup>36</sup>

### ADVANCED TRAUMA LIFE SUPPORT

Thorough patient assessment, application of rapid diagnostic tests, and early activation of resources are vital for ensuring optimal outcomes in patients with severe traumatic injuries.<sup>23</sup> The advanced trauma life support (ATLS) course of the American College of Surgeons is the most widely recognized training program for trauma physicians of all disciplines.<sup>37</sup> Though not comprehensive in subspecialty areas, the ATLS curriculum provides a framework and a common language for the care of injured patients.<sup>38</sup> ATLS is based on a “primary survey” that includes simultaneous efforts to identify and treat life- and limb- threatening injuries, beginning with the most immediate. ### INDICATIONS FOR A DEFINITIVE AIRWAY

The goal of emergency airway management is to ensure adequate oxygenation and ventilation while protecting the patient from the risks of aspiration. A definitive airway is defined as a cuffed tube in the trachea.<sup>38,52</sup> Endotracheal intubation is commonly required and is indicated categorically in the following conditions:

- Cardiac or respiratory arrest

- Respiratory insufficiency (see Box 62.1)

- Airway protection

- The need for deep sedation or analgesia, up to and including general anesthesia

- Transient hyperventilation of patients with spaceoccupying intracranial lesions and evidence of increased intracranial pressure (ICP)

- Delivery of a 100% fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) to patients with carbon monoxide poisoning

- Facilitation of the diagnostic workup in uncooperative or intoxicated patients

### APPROACH TO ENDOTRACHEAL INTUBATION

The anesthesiologist should generally insist on the same monitoring standards for airway management in the ED as in the OR, including an electrocardiogram (ECG), blood pressure, oximetry, and capnography. Appropriate equipment, including an oxygen source, bag–valve–mask ventilating system, mechanical ventilator, suction, and a selection of laryngoscope blades, endotracheal tubes, and devices for managing difficult intubations (e.g., video laryngoscope), should be available in any location where emergency intubation is likely, including the ED.

Endotracheal intubation is best accomplished in almost all cases with a modified rapid- sequence approach by an operator with experience in this practice. Although concern may exist that using neuromuscular blocking drugs and potent anesthetics outside the OR will be associated with a higher complication rate, the opposite is more likely correct. Anesthetic induction and neuromuscular blockade allow the best first- pass intubating conditions, which is advantageous in an uncooperative, hypoxic, or aspirating patient.<sup>53–55</sup> Attempts to secure the airway in an awake or lightly sedated patient increase the risk for airway trauma, pain, aspiration, hypertension, laryngospasm, and further combative behavior. Experienced providers, supported by appropriate monitoring and equipment, have achieved results of medication- assisted intubation outside the OR that are equivalent to those for emergency intubation within the OR.<sup>56–59</sup>

### PROPHYLAXIS AGAINST ASPIRATION OF GASTRIC CONTENTS

A trauma patient should always be treated as having a full stomach and at risk for gastric contents aspiration during anesthesia induction (see Chapter 40). Reasons include ingesting food or liquids before the injury, swallowing blood from oral or nasal injuries, delayed gastric emptying associated with the stress of trauma, and administration of liquid contrast medium for abdominal CT scanning.

Cricoid pressure—the Sellick maneuver<sup>60</sup> —has previously been recommended to be applied continuously during emergency airway management from when the patient loses protective airway reflexes until endotracheal tube placement and cuff inflation are confirmed. The Sellick maneuver involves elevating the patient’s chin (without displacing the cervical spine) and then pushing the cricoid cartilage posteriorly to close the esophagus. More recent evidence suggests that cricoid pressure may worsen the laryngoscopic grade of view in up to 30% of patients<sup>61</sup> without providing effective prevention of aspiration.<sup>62</sup> In a prehospital study evaluating the impact of cricoid pressure on subsequent intubation success, discontinuing cricoid pressure and further laryngeal manipulation improved success fo endotracheal intubation.<sup>63</sup> Thus, it is appropriate for the intubator to request that cricoid pressure be released in the trauma patient if this problem is suspected during a difficult intubation. The lack of evidence supporting the use of cricoid pressure and its potential to make intubation more difficult has led the American Heart Association to recommend discontinuing its use during cardiac arrest situations.<sup>64</sup> Additionally, the Eastern Association for the

Surgery of Trauma Practice Management Guidelines for emergency tracheal intubation have removed it as a Class 1 recommendation.<sup>54</sup>

In the traditionally defined rapid- sequence induction of anesthesia, any attempt at ventilation between medication administration and intubation is avoided, presumably because positive- pressure ventilation may force gas into the patient’s stomach, leading to regurgitation and aspiration. Sellick’s original paper described ventilation during cricoid pressure in patients with full stomachs. Applying cricoid pressure during mask ventilation was believed to prevent this gastric inflation.<sup>60</sup> While this may be true, cricoid pressure may reduce tidal volumes, increase peak inspiratory pressure, or prevent ventilation.<sup>62</sup> On the other hand, the increase in oxygen consumption in trauma patients necessitates preoxygenation whenever possible and because preoxygenation may be difficult in a trauma patient—because of facial trauma, decreased respiratory effort, or agitation— rapid desaturation is a possibility. Positive- pressure ventilation during all phases of induction provides the largest possible oxygen reserve during emergency airway management and will help mitigate hypoxia if intubation proves difficult. In this situation, large tidal volumes and high peak inspiratory pressures should be avoided.

In classic experiments with adults, the lower esophageal sphincter may be violated at peak airway pressures above 25 cm H2O, thus potentially increasing the risk for aspiration.<sup>65</sup> Application of cricoid pressure during attempts at positive- pressure ventilation may be considered to reduce gastric inflation but may prevent adequate ventilation in some patients necessitating discontinuation. Cricoid pressure may be more effective in preventing gastric insufflation in children than in adults. With appropriately applied cricoid pressure, even with peak inspiratory pressures of up to 40 cm H2O, gastric insufflation was not observed in a small study that included infants and children from 2 months to 8 years of age.<sup>66</sup>

### PROTECTION OF THE CERVICAL SPINE

Standard practice dictates that all blunt trauma victims are assumed to have an unstable cervical spine until proven otherwise. The airway management of these patients receives much attention from anesthesiologists because direct laryngoscopy causes cervical motion, with the potential to exacerbate spinal cord injury (SCI). Stabilization of the cervical spine will generally occur in the prehospital environment, with the patient already having a rigid cervical collar. The presence of an “uncleared” cervical spine mandates the use of in- line manual stabilization (not traction) throughout any attempt at intubation.<sup>37</sup> This approach allows removal of the front of the cervical collar to facilitate wider mouth opening and jaw displacement; however, this may slightly lengthen the time to intubation and worsen laryngeal visualization during laryngoscopy compared to intubation attempts without manual in- line stabilization.<sup>67</sup> In- line stabilization has been tested through considerable clinical experience and is the standard of care in the ATLS curriculum. The risk of spinal cord injury during tracheal intubation appears to be minimal, even in the presence of gross cervical spine instability.<sup>68</sup> Furthermore, the magnitude of the force applied during laryngoscopy seems to be the most

<!-- PDF page 9 -->

significant factor associated with cervical spine movement during intubation in cadaver studies.<sup>69,70</sup>

Emergency awake fiberoptic intubation, though requiring less manipulation of the neck, is generally very difficult because of airway secretions and hemorrhage, rapid desaturation, and lack of patient cooperation and is best reserved for cooperative patients with known cervical instability under controlled conditions (see also Chapter 40). Indirect video laryngoscopy offers several potential advantages, including decreased cervical motion and improved visualization.<sup>71,72</sup> Among non- physician intubators, video laryngoscopy is associated with enhanced first- pass and overall success. Still, for experienced physicians, no definite advantage has been observed compared to direct laryngoscopy.<sup>73</sup> For critically ill patients, video laryngoscopy has been shown to reduce the risk of difficult intubation and esophageal intubation while increasing first- pass success.<sup>74</sup> In comparative studies of direct laryngoscopy, video laryngoscopy, fiberoptic, blind nasal intubation, or cricothyrotomy—in patients with known cervical cord/spine injuries—there is no difference in neurologic deterioration based on the technique used, and no clear evidence that direct laryngoscopy worsens outcome.<sup>75</sup>

### PERSONNEL

Emergency intubation requires more assistance than intubation performed under controlled conditions (see Chapter 40). Three providers are required to ventilate the patient, manage the airway, administer medications, and provide in- line cervical stabilization; a fourth provider may be needed to provide cricoid pressure if deemed appropriate. Fig. 62.5 is an illustration of this approach.

Additional assistance may be required to restrain a patient who is combative because of intoxication or traumatic brain injury (TBI).

The immediate presence of a surgeon or other physician who can expeditiously perform a cricothyroidotomy is desirable. Even if a surgical airway is not required, other experienced hands may prove helpful during difficult intubations. The surgeon may also wish to inspect the upper airway during laryngoscopy if there has been trauma to the face or neck. A video laryngoscope may prove invaluable in this scenario. Urgent tube thoracostomy may prove necessary in some trauma patients to treat a tension pneumothorax that may develop with the onset of positive- pressure ventilation.

Any intravenous (IV) anesthetic administered to a trauma patient in hemorrhagic shock may potentiate profound hypotension and even cardiac arrest because of the inhibition of circulating catecholamines. The most commonly used induction agent in the United States in the ED or trauma setting is etomidate.<sup>79</sup> When used in a range of 0.2 to 0.3 mg/kg, etomidate is associated with hemodynamic stability and has an onset of action and duration of effect similar to succinylcholine. Whereas the safety profile of etomidate in trauma patients has been challenged, most of the studies have been retrospective and fraught with methodologic limitations such as selection bias.<sup>80,81</sup> Even a single dose of etomidate is associated with transient adrenal insufficiency. Still, this effect is not considered clinically significant in trauma and mixed surgical– medical patients undergoing rapid sequence induction and intubation.<sup>82–84</sup> Myoclonus may be observed in over 30% of patients who receive etomidate, but the use of a rapidly acting neuromuscular agent, such as succinylcholine or rocuronium, mitigates this effect.<sup>85</sup>

Ketamine is also a frequently used induction agent for hypotensive trauma patients due to its centrally mediated increase in sympathetic tone and catecholamine release.<sup>86</sup> The use of ketamine has previously been questioned in patients with traumatic brain injury based on older reports of ICP elevation.<sup>87,88</sup> More recent analyses have shown that cerebral perfusion is preserved through the maintenance of mean arterial blood pressure in hemodynamically unstable patients. This may be more important than any theoretical risk of harmful CNS effects due to ketamine’s tendency to increase cerebral activity and ICP.<sup>87,89</sup> Some investigators have also raised concerns that the psychotropic effects associated with ketamine may increase the risk of acute and posttraumatic stress disorder in trauma patients,<sup>90,91</sup> although this was not found in a study examining its intraoperative use in burn patients.<sup>92</sup> A 2019 systematic review noted no differences in outcomes (mortality,

length of hospital stay, or number of blood transfusions) when ketamine ( _n_ = 634) rather than etomidate ( _n_ = 699) was selected to induce anesthesia in trauma patients (one randomized trial and two non- randomized trials).<sup>93</sup> Experimentally, ketamine has been shown to have direct myocardial depressant properties<sup>94,95</sup> ; however, the indirect effect of catecholamine release and inhibition of catecholamine reuptake may result in tachycardia and hypertension.<sup>96</sup> In hemodynamically stressed patients, the cardiac depression may be unmasked and lead to cardiovascular collapse.<sup>97</sup> Due to its abuse potential, ketamine may not be as readily available in some resuscitation areas compared to other induction agents. Nevertheless, ketamine continues to be a commonly used drug for rapid sequence induction and intubation in trauma patients.<sup>79,93,98</sup>

The patient’s hemodynamic state dictates the choice of induction agent for a trauma patient.<sup>98</sup> Hypotension will develop in hypovolemic patients with the administration of any anesthetic because of interruption of compensatory sympathetic outflow and the sudden change to positivepressure ventilation.<sup>99</sup> Previously healthy young patients can lose up to 40% of their blood volume before experiencing a decrease in blood pressure, thereby leading to potentially catastrophic circulatory collapse with induction of anesthesia, regardless of the anesthetic chosen. The dose of anesthetic must be decreased in the presence of hemorrhage, including no anesthetic at all in patients with lifethreatening hypovolemia. The shock index (heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure) may guide appropriate dose reductions for unstable patients. A suggested nomogram is presented in Fig. 62.6.

Rapid- sequence induction and intubation may proceed with muscle relaxants alone, although onset time may be prolonged in a patient with circulatory impairment. Subsequent patient recall of intubation and emergency procedures is highly variable and affected by the presence of coexisting TBI, intoxication, and the depth of hemorrhagic shock (see Chapters 27 and 40).

Decreased cerebral perfusion appears to inhibit memory formation but cannot be reliably associated with any particular blood pressure or chemical marker. Small doses of midazolam may reduce the incidence of patient awareness but can also contribute to hypotension. Although the recall of ED or peri-operative events is not unusual in this circumstance, anesthesia provider liability appears to be limited; an analysis of intraoperative awareness lawsuits in the ASA Closed Claims Database revealed no claims related to surgery in trauma patients.<sup>100</sup>

### NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKING DRUGS

Succinylcholine remains the neuromuscular blocker with the fastest onset—less than 1 minute—and the shortest duration of action – 5 to 10 minutes (see Chapters 11 and 40).<sup>98</sup> These properties make it popular for rapid- sequence induction of anesthesia. Although the use of succinylcholine may allow the return of spontaneous respiration before the development of significant hypoxia in the “cannot intubate, cannot ventilate” situation, this is unlikely to be beneficial in an emergency intubation in a trauma patient. The anesthesiologist should not rely on the return of spontaneous breathing in time to salvage a difficult airway management problem but should instead proceed with efforts to obtain a definitive airway, including cricothyroidotomy, if other possibilities have been exhausted.

The administration of succinylcholine is associated with several adverse consequences. Increases in serum potassium of 0.5 to 1.0 mEq/L are expected, but in certain patients, potassium may increase by more than 5 mEq/L.<sup>101</sup> A hyperkalemic response is typically seen in burn victims and those with muscle pathology secondary to direct trauma, denervation (as with SCI), or immobilization. Hyperkalemia is not seen in the first 24 hours after these injuries, and succinylcholine may be used safely for acute airway management. Patients at risk are those with underlying pathology before their traumatic event or those undergoing subsequent surgery in the weeks to months after injury.

Succinylcholine causes an increase in intraocular pressure and should be used cautiously in patients with ocular trauma.<sup>102</sup> Succinylcholine may also increase ICP,<sup>103</sup> so its use in brain trauma victims is controversial. Hypoxia and hypercapnia may be as damaging as the transient increase in pressure caused by the drug.<sup>40</sup> If succinylcholine use leads to faster intubation, its benefits may outweigh its risks. Succinylcholine must be evaluated for each situation based on the acuity of CNS injury, the anticipated speed with which intubation can be accomplished, and the likelihood that hypoxia will develop.

Alternatives to succinylcholine include rocuronium (0.9 to 1.2 mg/kg) and vecuronium (0.1 to 0.2 mg/kg). Because these drugs have no significant cardiovascular toxicity, large doses can be administered to achieve rapid (1- to 2- minute) systemic relaxation. Unfortunately, at this dose, the duration of action of either rocuronium or vecuronium will be 1 to 2 hours, which may be significant if inadequate sedation leads to patient awareness of paralysis or if it prevents ongoing neurologic assessment. In a 2015 Cochrane review, succinylcholine was superior to rocuronium for achieving superior intubating conditions; however, when appropriately dosed (1.2 mg/kg), no differences in intubating conditions were observed between the two agents.<sup>104</sup>

Specific situations will always exist where maintaining spontaneous ventilation during intubation is the preferred way to proceed. If patients can maintain their airway temporarily but have clear indications for an artificial airway, slow induction with ketamine or an inhaled induction with sevoflurane through cricoid pressure is an alternative strategy that may enable the placement of an endotracheal tube without compromising the patient.

### ADJUNCTS TO ENDOTRACHEAL INTUBATION

Equipment to facilitate difficult intubation should be readily available wherever emergency airway management is performed (see Chapter 40). The equipment available depends on the preferences of the anesthesiologist; the usefulness of most special equipment depends more on previous experience than on any intrinsic properties of the device. However, certain items deserve mention because of their frequent citation as aids in managing a difficult airway.

The gum elastic bougie _,_ or intubating introducer, is an inexpensive and easily mastered adjunct for managing a difficult airway. The bougie is placed through the vocal cords via direct laryngoscopy, and the endotracheal tube is then advanced over the bougie into the trachea. Placement of the bougie is easier than direct placement of an endotracheal tube because of its smaller diameter and the ability of an experienced operator to feel it enter the trachea (tracheal rings) even when the glottic opening cannot be visualized. The bougie is passed under the epiglottis and gently advanced; if resistance is met, it is withdrawn, rotated slightly, and advanced again. In this fashion, the anesthesiologist can blindly “palpate” the larynx until the bougie advances into the trachea. The bougie can also be used with indirect video laryngoscopy systems and is especially useful in the ED when the sniffing position cannot be used because of uncertainty about the cervical spine. A bougie with a cudette tip may facilitate placement in this scenario.

Supraglottic airway (SGA) devices such as the laryngeal mask airway (LMA; LMA North America, San Diego, CA) are recommended in the ASA algorithm for the management of a patient with a difficult airway. The LMA can be used as a guide for intubation when unsuspected difficult intubation is encountered in a trauma patient; an endotracheal tube may be placed blindly through the lumen of the LMA and into the trachea, or a fiberoptic bronchoscope may be used to guide the tube through the LMA. The LMA is an appropriate rescue device for a difficult airway situation in trauma if there is no significant anatomic injury or hemorrhage in the mouth and larynx.

### FACIAL AND PHARYNGEAL TRAUMA

Trauma to the face and upper airway poses particular difficulties for the anesthesiologist.<sup>105</sup> Serious skeletal derangements may be masked by minor soft tissue damage. Failure to identify an injury to the face or neck can lead to acute airway obstruction secondary to swelling and hematoma. Laryngeal edema is also a risk in patients who have suffered a chemical or thermal injury to the pharyngeal mucosa. Intraoral hemorrhage, pharyngeal erythema, and change in voice are all indications of early intubation.

In general, maxillary and mandibular fractures make ventilation by mask more difficult, whereas mandibular fractures may make endotracheal intubation easier. Palpation of the facial bones before airway manipulation will alert the anesthetist to these possibilities. Patients with injuries to the jaw and zygomatic arch often have trismus. Although the trismus will resolve with neuromuscular blocking agents, preinduction assessment of airway anatomy may be difficult. Bilateral mandibular fractures and pharyngeal hemorrhage may lead to upper airway obstruction, particularly in a supine patient. However, intubation may be easier because of the loss of skeletal resistance to direct laryngoscopy. A patient arriving at the ED in a sitting or prone position because of airway compromise is best left in that position until the moment of anesthetic induction and intubation.

### THE AIRWAY PLACED IN THE FIELD

In many settings, prehospital clinicians work under protocols that allow for advanced airway management, including endotracheal intubation or placement of an SGA (see also Chapter 63). It is recommended that the individuals responsible for airway management at the ED level be familiar with the protocols and devices available in their region to facilitate early assessment and airway exchange. All intubations accomplished in the field should be immediately confirmed using capnography. In the event of cardiac arrest in an inability to ensure correct placement by observation of end tidal CO2, direct laryngoscopy or video laryngoscopy must be employed to visualize the position of the endotracheal tube (ETT). When an SGA device is used in the prehospital setting, the positioning and ability to ventilate should be confirmed by capnometry, breath sounds, and chest movement. If there is adequate ventilation, replacement of the SGA can be delayed during the initial evaluation.

<!-- PDF page 12 -->

Since the SGA is not considered a secured definitive airway, it should be changed to a cuffed ETT as soon as practicable. When faced with changing out in SGA, preparation is the key concept. Unfortunately, a paucity of literature discusses the technique for “changing out” an SGA. This procedure can be challenging, with an increased risk of airway loss. Before deciding which method to use, it is essential to discuss with the prehospital clinician that managed the patient’s airway in the field to determine the following information:

- What was the reason for placing an SGA?

- Were attempts at oral or nasal intubation made? How many?

- What laryngoscope/video laryngoscope was used? What size blade was used?

- What anatomy was visualized during attempts?

- What, if any, medications were administered during the attempts?

The answers to these questions may suggest a difficult airway, prompting more careful management during the exchange process. The three options for SGA exchange and conversion to an endotracheal tube are: (1) remove the SGA and replace it under direct or video laryngoscopy; (2) use the SGA to place an ETT or exchange catheter; or (3) proceed to a surgical airway. The second option will largely be guided by the specific SGA, channel size, and available equipment.<sup>106–108</sup> Of note, there have been reports of pharyngeal, glottic, and lingual edema with various SGA devices, likely secondary to exaggerated anatomic distortion and indirect vascular compression.<sup>107</sup>

Prehospital clinicians occasionally perform blind nasotracheal intubation in the spontaneously ventilating trauma patient. Successful exchange of a nasotracheal tube on arrival to the ED is consistently more accessible than the SGA. Nasotracheal tubes placed in the field are usually done when prehospital clinicians are not credentialed for drugfacilitated intubation. As a result, most of these patients have not had laryngoscopy performed and therefore are less likely to have airway edema from intubation attempts.

## Resuscitation from Hemorrhagic Shock

Resuscitation refers to the restoration of normal physiology after injury. Resuscitation from hemorrhagic shock refers to restoring normal circulating blood volume, normal vascular tone, and normal tissue perfusion. Resuscitation begins immediately after injury via the patient’s compensatory mechanisms and continues through the prehospital, ED, OR, and intensive care unit (ICU) phases of care.

### PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF HEMORRHAGIC SHOCK

An imbalance occurs between systemic O2 delivery and O2 consumption during massive hemorrhage. Blood loss leads to hemodynamic instability, coagulopathy, decreased O2 delivery, tissue perfusion, and cellular hypoxia. The initial response to hemorrhage takes place on the macrocirculatory level and is mediated by the neuroendocrine system. Reduced arterial blood pressure leads to vasoconstriction

and the release of catecholamines to preserve blood flow to the heart, kidney, and brain, whereas other regional beds are constricted. Pain, hemorrhage, and cortical perception of traumatic injuries lead to the release of hormones and other inflammatory mediators, including renin, angiotensin, vasopressin, antidiuretic hormone, growth hormone, glucagon, cortisol, epinephrine, and norepinephrine.<sup>109</sup> This response sets the stage for the microcirculatory response that follows. As oxygen debt mounts, the release of damage- associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), mitochondrial DNA, and formyl peptides contribute to the systemic inflammatory response.<sup>41</sup>

Individual ischemic cells respond to hemorrhage by taking up interstitial fluid, thus further depleting intravascular fluid.<sup>110</sup> Cellular edema may restrict blood flow in adjacent capillaries and result in the “no- reflow” phenomenon, which prevents the reversal of ischemia, even in the presence of adequate macroperfusion.<sup>111</sup> Ischemic cells produce lactate and free radicals, which accumulate in the circulation if perfusion is diminished. These compounds cause direct damage to the cell and form the bulk of the toxic load that washes back to the central circulation when the flow is re- established. Ischemic cells also produce and release inflammatory factors: prostacyclin, thromboxane, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, endothelin, complement, interleukins, tumor necrosis factor, and others.<sup>41,112</sup> Fig. 62.7 depicts the inflammatory response to shock, emphasizing immune system amplification.

This inflammatory response, once begun, becomes a disease process independent of its origin. Such alterations lay the foundations for the subsequent development of multiple organ failure (MOF), a systemic inflammatory process that leads to the dysfunction of different vital organs and accounts for high mortality rates.<sup>113</sup>

Specific organ systems respond to traumatic shock in specific ways. The central nervous system is the prime trigger of the neuroendocrine response to shock, which maintains perfusion to the heart, kidney, and brain at the expense of other tissues.<sup>114</sup> Regional glucose uptake in the brain changes during shock.<sup>115</sup> Reflexes and cortical electrical activity are depressed during hypotension; these changes are reversible with mild hypoperfusion but become permanent with prolonged ischemia. Failure to recover preinjury neurologic function is a marker for a poor prognosis, even when normal vital signs are restored.<sup>116</sup>

The Host Response to Injury research program (i.e., “Glue Grant”) provided additional data regarding the genomic response to injury.<sup>41,117</sup> A patient’s initial response to injury was previously thought to involve a profound systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) followed by relative immunosuppression (i.e., compensatory antiinflammatory response syndrome [CARS]), followed by recovery. Following subsequent surgeries or delayed treatment of secondary injuries, the SIRS cycle would repeat, followed by CARS. More recent work has revealed that proinflammatory and anti- inflammatory innate immunity genes are upregulated following injury while adaptive immunity genes are concurrently downregulated.<sup>117</sup> Some patients may exhibit amplified response that may be slower to return to baseline.

The heart may be protected from ischemia during shock because of maintenance of or even an increase in nutrient blood flow, and cardiac function is well preserved until the late stages. Lactate, free radicals, and other humoral factors released by ischemic cells may act as negative inotropes and, in a bleeding patient, may produce cardiac dysfunction as the terminal event in the shock spiral.<sup>121</sup> A patient with cardiac disease or direct cardiac trauma is at significant risk for clinical decompensation because a fixed stroke volume inhibits the body’s ability to increase blood flow in response to hypovolemia and anemia. Compensatory tachycardia ensues, with potentially disastrous consequences as the myocardial oxygen supply is rapidly depleted. The lung is the filter for the inflammatory by- products of the ischemic body. Immune complex and cellular factors accumulate in pulmonary capillaries and lead to neutrophil and platelet aggregation, increased capillary permeability, destruction of lung architecture, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).<sup>123,124</sup> The lung is the sentinel organ for developing multiple organ dysfunction (MOD) in a traumatic shock patient.<sup>125,126</sup> Pure hemorrhage, in the absence of hypoperfusion, does not produce pulmonary dysfunction.<sup>127</sup> Traumatic hemorrhagic shock is far more than a hemodynamic disorder.

The gut is one of the earliest organs affected by hypoperfusion and maybe the prime trigger of MOD. Intense vasoconstriction occurs early and frequently leads to a “noreflow” phenomenon, even when the macrocirculation is restored.<sup>128</sup> Intestinal cell death causes a breakdown in the gut’s barrier function, resulting in increased translocation of bacteria to the liver and lung, thereby potentiating ARDS.<sup>129</sup>

The liver has a complex microcirculation and may experience reperfusion injury during recovery from shock.<sup>130</sup> Hepatic cells are also metabolically active and contribute to the ischemic inflammatory response and irregularities in blood glucose.<sup>131</sup> Failure of the synthetic function of the liver after shock is almost always lethal.

Skeletal muscle is not metabolically active during shock and tolerates ischemia better than other organ systems. The large mass of skeletal muscle makes it integral in generating lactic acid and free radicals from ischemic cells. Sustained

### TRAUMA- INDUCED COAGULOPATHY

Trauma- induced coagulopathy (TIC) is composed of two pathophysiologic processes, the coagulopathy caused by severe shock and traumatic injury (Acute Traumatic Coagulopathy, ATC) and a dilutional coagulopathy or “iatrogenic traumatic coagulopathy” (ITC), also referred to as systemic acquired coagulopathy (SAC) or resuscitation associated coagulopathy (RAC).<sup>133–135</sup> Moore and colleagues demonstrated that the multivariate predictors of coagulopathy included the TIC risk factor of the Injury Severity Score (ISS) but not a base deficit or lactate. The risk factors for RAC, such as hypothermia and acidosis, were not significant predictors as well. Hypothermia, defined as 34°C, and a low transfusion ratio have been implicated in increased mortality from coagulopathy.<sup>136</sup> The only relevant multivariate predictor of RAC was crystalloid volumes in the shock setting. Clinically relevant cutoffs ranged from 2 L to greater than 4 L. Patients who received less than 2 L of crystalloid prehospital were unlikely to become coagulopathic regardless of admission lactate. Patients who received between 2 L and 4 L of crystalloid were at risk for coagulopathy only if their arrival lactate was elevated. Patients who received greater than 4 L of prehospital crystalloid elevated the risk for coagulopathy regardless of arrival lactate.

The presence of TIC—a “multifactorial, global failure of the coagulation system to sustain adequate hemostasis after major trauma”—is an endogenous component linked to hypoperfusion and tissue injury referred to as acute traumatic coagulopathy (ATC).<sup>137,138</sup> There are several proposed mechanisms for the development of ATC. One mechanism may be the endothelial activation of protein C secondary to the traumatic, inflammatory response.<sup>139</sup> Activated protein C (APC) is generated by thrombomodulin–thrombin complex production due to tissue hypoperfusion. APC inactivates factor Va and factor VIIIa, and, in combination with the reduction in thrombin availability for fibrin formation, supports the development of ATC.<sup>140</sup> Another proposed mechanism is the disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) hypothesis. This hypothesis suggests coagulopathy is closely related to tissue hypoperfusion, shock, endothelial activation, and endothelial injury. This sequence of events leads to a prolonged prothrombin time (PT) and a picture of a consumptive process that seems to meet the criteria for DIC. Low fibrinogen and antithrombin levels, increased fibrinogen degradation products, and D- dimer levels are frequently observed. The disproportionate increase in plasmin levels relative to thrombin activation is thought to cause the hyperfibrinolytic state.<sup>141</sup> There is a lack of evidence to suggest that thrombus formation in the microcirculation of trauma patients and consumption of clotting factors (thrombocytopenia) are valid mechanisms. As described by Rizoli _et al._ , within the first 24 hours after a traumatic injury, almost all severely injured trauma patients (ISS >16) have an ISTH (International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis) score of “overt DIC” or “suggestive of non- overt DIC.” Additionally, degradation of the endothelial glycocalyx due to hypoperfusion may play

a supporting role in ATC.<sup>142</sup> Experimental work by Kozar _et al._ using blood samples from trauma patients in hemorrhagic shock has shown that admission syndecan- 1 concentrations significantly predicted mortality, coagulopathy, and massive transfusion.<sup>143</sup>

TIC may be functionally detected with the use of viscoelastic monitoring, thromboelastometry (ROTEM<sup>®</sup> ), or thromboelastography (TEG<sup>®</sup> ) as well as standard laboratory testing (prothrombin time, INR, activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen, etc.) (see also Chapter 46). Whole blood clotting tests such as ROTEM and TEG define the relationship more closely between laboratory- based abnormalities and clinically relevant bleeding than standard laboratory testing.<sup>144–147</sup> Evidence of TAC is demonstrated by the early presence of reduced clot strength, as shown by viscoelastic monitoring. Changes in laboratory- based coagulation testing are associated with increased mortality and a higher likelihood of receiving a massive transfusion.<sup>148</sup> Davenport and colleagues<sup>148</sup> have proposed a clot amplitude threshold of &lt;35 mm at 5 minutes using ROTEM® (Tem Innovations GmbH, Munich, Germany) analysis which predicted the subsequent need for a massive transfusion with a detection rate of 77% and false- positive rate of 13% (see also Chapter 46). Similar results have been obtained using RapidTEG® (Haemonetics Corp., Niles, IL, USA) viscoelastic testing.<sup>149</sup> Laboratory- based coagulation testing is of limited utility in the early detection of ATC due to time considerations; however, conventional coagulation tests are predictive of outcomes. Frith _et al._ found patients with a prothrombin ratio (PTr) of >1.2 on admission to have higher transfusion requirements and increased mortality.<sup>150</sup>

Most causes of traumatic bleeding are not related to TIC or SAC. Most traumatic bleeding is caused by a primary injury or disruption of the large blood vessels (arteries and veins) and not the coagulopathy caused by the failure to form adequate clots at the level of the capillary beds, the microcirculation. Bleeding at the level of the capillary beds is often impossible to control by conventional surgical methods.<sup>151</sup> The extent of a coagulation deficit at the capillary bed level appears to depend on the extent of tissue damage, degree of hypoperfusion, and injury to the endothelial surface layer. The perturbations at the microcirculatory level will be aggravated further by acidosis, hypothermia, and crystalloid use in resuscitation. Trauma patients with TIC and large vessel injury must be taken to the operating room without delay. Hence, damage control surgery (DCS) and damage control resuscitation (DCR) must occur in parallel to address the immediate causes of bleeding.<sup>133</sup> Hemorrhage control, correction of hypovolemia and hypotension via a high ration resuscitation or fresh whole blood (FWB), avoiding the phenomenon of dilutional coagulopathy, as well as a detailed approach in the prevention and correction of TIC is paramount. In DCR, organ perfusion may be marginally sacrificed (permissive hypotension) in the face of severe hemorrhage during the early phase of DCS. Once achieving hemostasis, attention may then be shifted to the goal of optimal organ perfusion.<sup>152,153</sup>

### ASSESSMENT OF THE HEMOSTATIC SYSTEM

Given the importance of coagulopathy in severely injured trauma patients, assessment of the hemostatic system is

<!-- PDF page 15 -->

essential in the early phases of management. This is best accomplished by collecting and integrating information from the four pillars of perioperative coagulation monitoring: (1) medical history, (2) clinical presentation, (3) standard laboratory- based coagulation tests, and (4) viscoelastic monitoring (see also Chapter 46).<sup>154</sup> Obtaining a focused patient medical history is one of the critical pieces for evaluating the individual bleeding risk. In some settings, standardized assessments and specific questionnaires can be deployed to raise sensitivity and specificity, resulting in superior detection of hemostasis issues compared to routine coagulation studies alone.<sup>155</sup> The assessment of the clinical phenotypes of bleeding is critical for the differential diagnosis. It allows the clinician to differentiate and semi- quantify the underlying coagulation disorder straightforwardly and quickly. The clinical assessment also helps put abnormal coagulation studies in context, so clinical interventions are not based solely on laboratory values. The clinical evaluation aims to differentiate whether the cause of bleeding is “surgical” or “nonsurgical.” A more diffuse pattern characterizes the latter. Standard lab coagulation tests typically include prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), and platelet count. In some institutions, lab values, such as fibrinogen levels, factor XIII, and thrombin time, may be part of a standard laboratory coagulation battery. Standard coagulation tests alone play a limited role in the initial diagnostic steps of patients with deranged hemostasis due to trauma. When used in isolation, the standard tests have significant limitations in detecting the extent and origin of a given patient’s coagulopathy. These tests allow only the determination of specific questions and are of restricted value. The key restrictions of standard coagulation tests are delayed results in a dynamic situation, lack of validation, and inability to detect both hyperfibrinolysis and hypercoagulability. A meta- analysis by Haas _et al._ concluded that standard plasma coagulation tests represent historically established parameters that are not fully supported by evidence for managing perioperative coagulopathic bleeding.<sup>156</sup> This is where viscoelastic monitoring, the fourth pillar of perioperative coagulation assessment, brings added value. Viscoelastic coagulation monitoring devices like RapidTEG ROTEM and Quantra, assess the whole coagulation process from initial thrombin generation to maximum clot formation up to clot lysis.<sup>140</sup> In contrast to the standard lab coagulation tests, TEG and ROTEM have been better validated for the diagnosis of coagulopathy in trauma patients.<sup>157,158</sup> These tests can significantly improve a patient’s coagulation assessment and management. They may reduce unnecessary administration of pro- coagulant substances such as platelets, fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and coagulation factor concentrates. The rapid turnaround for results may enable the anesthesiologist to distinguish between a surgical cause of bleeding and trauma- associated coagulopathy.

### APPROACH TO RESUSCITATION

### Direct Admissions to the Operating Room

The trauma patient arriving directly to the operating room (OR) from the emergency department (ED) or the primary scene with little or no information to OR staff presents many logistical and clinical challenges to the receiving

team members. This is a task- laden time for the entire team with high unpredictability. Communication, observation, and organization are critical. Call- out and closed- loop communication is preferred in interdisciplinary trauma team interactions but not frequently utilized.<sup>159</sup> Information must be clear and accurate in a method recognized by the entire team. When the patient arrives, all movements by the trauma anesthesiologist are purposeful and precise. The trauma anesthesiologist must become an accomplished service leader and multi- tasker. Patients intubated in the ED or the field cannot be questioned about their medical history. The trauma anesthesiologist’s observational, organizational, and communication skills must occur in parallel.

The intubated patient arriving emergently to the OR cannot respond to questions commonly asked by the medical staff; communication with the trauma surgery team is paramount. In preparation for the move to the OR table, all lines should be organized while simultaneously surveying the patient and inquiring about the mechanism of injury, interventions to date, current physiologic status, and treatments rendered before the OR (i.e., “MIST” report).<sup>52</sup> Scene time, response time, and transport time are additional factors that determine the depth of shock and resuscitation needs of the patient and, ultimately, mortality. In penetrating injuries, prehospital response time and scene time independently correlate with in- hospital mortality.<sup>160,161</sup> Abnormal lab data should be shared as well as the progress of successful resuscitation. If the surgeon packs the abdomen to control bleeding while the trauma anesthesiology team continues resuscitation, they must be made aware of the progress, or lack thereof, in resuscitation, before the surgeon removes the packing from the abdomen. Surgical events such as compressing the vena cava during exposure, lifting the heart, and compressing structures within the mediastinum must be communicated before they occur.

A rapid survey while organizing lines will give the trauma anesthesiologist a global picture of the patient’s injury severity. Losing large-bore lines or an arterial line during transport could be catastrophic and delay resuscitation. After a motor vehicle crash, a seat belt sign could signal rib fractures and a pneumothorax. A rapid pupillary exam may indicate a concomitant closed or open head injury. If placed in the ED, thoracostomy tubes must be placed on suction before placing the patient on positive-pressure ventilation. Pink frothy secretions in the ETT may indicate extensive pulmonary contusions and alter the mode of ventilation in the OR. A protuberant, tense abdomen and a positive, focused assessment with sonography in trauma (FAST) exam may indicate a severe solid organ or vascular injury. A pelvic binder placed in the ED may mean a severe pelvic injury, and blood products should be immediately available when the binder is released. A deterioration in hemodynamics will necessitate the re- tightening of the pelvic binder and further resuscitation. Open extremity fractures must be stabilized. Unstable fractures are a source of ongoing bleeding and consumption of clotting factors. Closed long-bone fractures can be a source of compartmental bleeding, further insidious blood loss, and potential compartment syndrome. Sub- xiphoid ecchymosis (London sign) caused by a handlebar of a bicycle, or electric scooter, may indicate a pancreaticoduodenal injury. A patient arriving with an ED thoracotomy and the aorta cross- clamped changes the

Questions posed to the trauma team while organizing lines and surveying the patient can quickly reaffirm information gathered during a rapid primary survey. Was the FAST exam positive or negative? If positive, what quadrant? Information from the FAST may be critical for the placement of additional lines. Questioning the patient about events immediately before the injury, as well as medical history and current medications (i.e., anticoagulants), is vital. Blunt traumatic injuries may be the result of a medical emergency. A patient may be involved in a motor vehicle crash because of chest pain, paroxysmal a- fib, or a paroxysmal episode of a- fib with a rapid ventricular response causing a syncopal episode while driving. A pedestrian struck by a motor vehicle may be a confused diabetic with hypoglycemia who wandered into traffic.

### Fluid Management

Fluid administration is the cornerstone of resuscitation (see also Chapters 43, 45, and 46). Intravascular volume is lost to hemorrhage, uptake by ischemic cells, and extravasation into the interstitial space. Administration of IV fluids will predictably increase cardiac output and arterial blood pressure in a hypovolemic trauma patient. The ATLS curriculum initially advocated rapid infusion of up to 2 L of warmed isotonic crystalloid solution in any hypotensive patient to restore adequate arterial blood pressure. More recently, this has been revised to recognize the importance of a balanced

resuscitation with elimination of the emphasis on a more aggressive approach. Current recommendations suggest initiation of resuscitation with 1 L of crystalloid and earlier use of blood and blood products for patients in shock.<sup>38,52</sup>

The change in ATLS recommendations recognizes that aggressive crystalloid fluid resuscitation during active hemorrhage may be deleterious. Dilution of red cell mass reduces O2 delivery and contributes to hypothermia and coagulopathy. Increased arterial blood pressure may lead to increased bleeding because of disruption of clots and reversal of compensatory vasoconstriction.<sup>164</sup> The result of aggressive fluid administration is often a transient increase in arterial blood pressure, followed by increased bleeding, another episode of hypotension, and the need for more volume administration. This vicious cycle has been recognized since World War I and remains a complication of resuscitation therapy today. The ATLS manual characterizes such patients as “transient responders” with active, ongoing hemorrhage.<sup>38,52</sup> Resuscitation of these patients should be considered in the following three phases (Table 62.2):

- **Phase 1** , Uncontrolled Hemorrhage: ongoing active bleeding with a focus on damage control with pragmatic resuscitation.

- **Phase 2,** Controlled Hemorrhage: major bleeding sources under control with a focus on goal- directed and tailored management of coagulopathy and resuscitation.

- **Phase 3** , Restoration of Physiology: hemorrhage has been controlled with a focus on end- organ perfusion and optimization of physiologic state.

### Phase 1, Uncontrolled Hemorrhage

During the initial phase of management, the goal in trauma patients with massive hemorrhage requiring an emergent surgical procedure in the OR is to stop the bleeding as soon as possible. In this setting, there is little opportunity to perform additional studies, await test results, or evaluate for perioperative optimization. The role of the anesthesia team is to help achieve hemostasis as quickly as possible while bridging the patient’s physiologic status to allow for surgical stabilization. In Phase 1, the general approach employs the concept of damage control resuscitation (DCR). DCR combines an empiric hemostatic resuscitation strategy in combination with permissive hypotension during surgical or angioembolization control of ongoing hemorrhage. Combined with surgical approaches incorporating damage control techniques, the initial goal of controlling hemorrhage, physiologic stabilization, and correction of coagulopathy allow for more definitive surgical interventions later.

**Permissive Hypotension.** Since World War I and II, many clinicians observed that achieving higher blood pressure with crystalloids was often harmful.<sup>165,166</sup> In 1965, Shaftan and colleagues<sup>167</sup> published the results of a study of coagulation in dogs that demonstrated that the formation of a soft extraluminal clot limits bleeding after arterial trauma. This study compared the quantity of blood lost from a standardized arterial injury under various conditions. The least blood loss occurred in hypotensive animals (whether hypotensive from hemorrhage or administration of a vasodilator), followed by the control group and then vasoconstricted animals. The most significant amount of blood was lost in animals that underwent vigorous reinfusion during the period of hemorrhage.

Laboratory data have shown the benefits of limiting intravascular fluid volumes and blood pressure in actively

hemorrhaging animals.<sup>168–171</sup> In the most sophisticated models, direct assessment of cardiac output and regional perfusion showed no difference between moderate- volume or large- volume resuscitation in terms of cardiac output, arterial blood pressure, or regional perfusion of the heart, kidneys, and intestines. Burris and co- workers<sup>172</sup> studied conventional resuscitation fluids and combinations of hypertonic saline and dextran and found that rebleeding correlated with higher mean arterial pressure (MAP) and that survival was best in groups resuscitated to lowerthan- normal MAP. The optimum target blood pressure for resuscitation varied with the composition of the fluid used. In larger animal studies, targeting higher MAP with fluid administration alone resulted in severe tissue edema and organ dysfunction.<sup>173</sup> Other preclinical data suggest that a MAP of 60 mm Hg should be evaluated in human studies based on more favorable inflammatory profiles and survival in animal studies.<sup>174</sup>

Hypotensive resuscitation has increasingly been accepted as a component of early DCR to avoid excessive fluid administration until control of active hemorrhage has been achieved.<sup>175</sup> This strategy uses less fluids and blood products during the early stages of treatment for hemorrhagic shock compared with the standard of care.<sup>176</sup> Hypotensive resuscitation has also been shown to be feasible and safe during early resuscitation of trauma patients in the prehospital and hospital settings.<sup>177–181</sup> In a meta- analysis that included 30 studies, hypotensive resuscitation was associated with significant mortality benefits (relative risk of mortality, 0.50; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.40–0.61), reduced need for blood transfusions, and lower incidence of acute respiratory distress syndrome, although a nonsignificant risk for acute kidney injury was observed.<sup>182</sup>

Initial work by Bickell and colleagues in 1994 randomized victims of penetrating torso trauma to one of two treatment groups: standard of care (up to 2 L of crystalloid infused in the prehospital setting) or delayed resuscitation (no fluid until the patient reached the OR).<sup>178</sup> This 37- month study eventually included 598 patients. Average transport times to care were 30 minutes from injury to the ED and an additional 50 minutes before reaching the OR; the fluid- restricted group received an average of about 0.8 L of fluid during this time. The immediate- resuscitation group received an average of 2.500 L of crystalloid and 130 mL of blood over this same period. Although substantially different during the study period, blood pressure on arrival at the OR was similar in both groups, which the authors took as evidence that the unresuscitated group had achieved spontaneous hemostasis. Survival to hospital discharge in the delayed- resuscitation group was significantly improved over the immediate- resuscitation group (70% vs. 62% [ _P_ &lt;.04]). No data were presented on the conduct of anesthesia after arrival at the OR but before control of hemorrhage or on the incidence of rebleeding after volume loading and induction of anesthesia in patients who had achieved hemostasis preoperatively.

A retrospective review of trauma admissions to the Los Angeles Medical Center published in 1996 supported the findings from Bickell _et al._<sup>181</sup> Patients brought to the hospital by private conveyance fared substantially better than those delivered by paramedics, even with high levels of injury severity. Further corroboration was provided by

retrospective examination of outcomes in a population of hemorrhaging trauma patients who received fluids via a commercial rapid infusion system (RIS, Haemonetics, Niles, Ill) during initial resuscitation.<sup>158</sup> The survival rate of this group was compared with that predicted by the institution’s trauma registry. When compared with case- matched controls, patients who received fluid by the rapid infusion system had a survival rate of only 56.8% vs. 71.2% for patients of similar age with similar injuries ( _P_ &lt;.001). This retrospective review was followed in 2002 by a second prospective trial of delayed resuscitation in trauma patients.<sup>179</sup> Patients with systolic blood pressure lower than 90 mm Hg and clinical evidence of blood loss were randomized to fluid resuscitation titrated to a systolic blood pressure of 100 mm Hg (normal group) or 70 mm Hg (study group) until the end of surgical interventions to control hemorrhage. As in the Bickell study, hypotension allowed spontaneous resolution of hemorrhage and autoresuscitation; blood pressure would increase without exogenous fluid administration once hemostasis was achieved. The typical patient began with a low initial pressure, followed by recovery to the vicinity of the target, overshoots and undershoots as bleeding and fluid administration continued, and an eventual rise above the target when the hemorrhage resolved, even in the absence of further fluid administration. The 93% overall survival rate in this study was higher than predicted from historical data and substantially higher than the mortality rate observed in Bickell _et al._ These findings may reflect the exclusion of patients who died in the prehospital phase or arrived at the trauma resuscitation unit in a moribund condition. It may also reflect improvements in overall care, an observation effect (i.e., patients in both groups received better care than patients not in the study), or a bias in subject recruitment. Over the first 24 hours, lactate and base deficit cleared to normal in both groups. They required similar amounts of fluid and blood products, suggesting that both groups were reaching an equivalent resuscitation end point. The authors concluded that the administration of fluids to an actively hemorrhaging patient should be titrated to specific physiologic end points, with the anesthesiologist navigating a course between the Scylla of increased hemorrhage and the Charybdis of hypoperfusion.

In a recent study, Morrison and colleagues compared a hypotensive resuscitative strategy targeting a MAP of 50 mm Hg to one targeting a MAP of 65 mm Hg with conventional resuscitation for patients requiring emergent surgery.<sup>176</sup> In a preliminary report, they found that patients in the hypotensive resuscitation group had a lower early postoperative mortality, a reduced incidence of coagulopathy, and lower mortality related to coagulopathy. Subsequently, this study was terminated early for futility and clinical equipoise with no significant survival advantage and similar rates of coagulopathy, renal failure, and infection between the low (50 mm Hg) and high (65 mm Hg) MAP target groups.<sup>180</sup> Of note, this study excluded blunt trauma patients included in the preliminary report. Despite not identifying a difference in major outcomes, the authors made several observations. First, the need for vasopressor use in the first hour of surgery was greater in the high MAP group. Although this group also received more fluid, it was not statistically significant. Second, the intraoperative MAP

was not significantly different between the low and high MAP groups (65.5 ± 11.6 mm Hg vs. 69.1 ± 13.8 mm Hg, _P_ = .07, respectively).

Considering the available clinical and experimental data regarding hypotensive resuscitation for trauma patients, the current consensus at most major trauma centers is to allow for hypotensive resuscitation in the setting of DCR, barring specific caveats discussed below. While the optimal arterial blood pressure remains controversial, a reasonable approach is to target a systolic pressure of less than 100 mm Hg with MAP between 50 to 60 mm Hg.<sup>175,183–185</sup>

While the role of vasopressor administration to acutely injured and hypotensive trauma patients is often discouraged in favor of permissive hypotension, some recent evidence has challenged this approach. Evidence from retrospective studies suggests that vasopressors are associated with increased mortality in trauma patients<sup>186–188</sup> ; however, there are numerous methodologic limitations to these investigations, most notably that patients most likely to die from acute hemorrhage were excluded, as well as patients with TBI and SCI, and that all vasopressors were considered cumulatively rather than based on the specific mechanism of action. More recent retrospective studies have attempted to address these limitations by including patients likely to die from acute blood loss and who received norepinephrine during prehospital transport or the acute resuscitation period.<sup>189</sup> There was no significant increase in 24- hour or overall hospital mortality in patients that received early norepinephrine. A plausible explanation for the utility of norepinephrine in acute hemorrhage with hypotension is the redistribution of blood volume from the vasodilated and unstressed venous system to the arterial circulation. The resulting increase in mean systemic filling pressure also contributes to an increase in preload and stroke volume in hypovolemic patients.<sup>190</sup> Additional prospective, randomized studies have demonstrated that early vasopressin infusions are associated with decreased volume of blood products transfused while not increasing complications, such as mortality, thrombosis, or multiple organ failure.<sup>191,192</sup> Mechanistically, vasopressin is unique in that it augments vascular tone by V1 receptors, and water balance and uptake in the kidney through aquaporin channels. Platelet function via release of von Willebrand factor is also enhanced. Further clinical research is warranted to support a change in clinical practice that recommends more consistent administration of vasopressors and to further clarify optimal blood pressure goals in severely injured and hemorrhaging trauma patients.

As a component of intraoperative management, the effect of anesthetic drugs on the body’s response to hemorrhage is an important difference between deliberate hypotension occurring in the elective operative setting and hemorrhagic shock occurring in the ED. Hypertensive trauma patients receive minimal doses of anesthetics, even for induction of anesthesia, because of the apparent effect of these drugs on arterial blood pressure. A hypotensive trauma patient is thus often in an initial state of profound vasoconstriction, as opposed to a patient undergoing elective intraoperative hypotension who is vasodilated by general anesthesia before any blood loss. Clinical trials of permissive hypotensive resuscitation have avoided the application of this technique to populations perceived to be at greater risk for ischemic complications,<sup>178–180</sup> including patients with known ischemic coronary disease, elderly patients, and those with injuries to the brain or spinal cord (see also Chapters 53 and 61). Absent convincing evidence in humans, permissive hypotension in older patients, or patients with brain

injury or spinal cord injury should generally be avoided. Hemodynamic goals for these types of traumatic injuries are discussed in subsequent sections of this chapter.

**Hemostatic Resuscitation.** Management of the initial coagulopathy associated with trauma must be incorporated into the overall Phase 1, Uncontrolled Hemorrhage DCR strategy – often referred to as hemostatic resuscitation. Little utility is found in targeting end points of resuscitation in the face of ongoing hemorrhage. Life- threatening coagulopathy is one of the most severe complications of patients in profound shock from massive hemorrhage and is generally predictable at an early stage.<sup>199</sup>

Severely injured trauma patients are likely to develop an early and aggressive endogenous coagulopathy separate from later loss and dilution of clotting factors compounded from hypothermia and acidosis<sup>139,200,201</sup> ; hence, the practice of hemostatic resuscitation has become commonplace in the most severely injured patients with shock and ongoing hemorrhage. Hemostatic resuscitation entails the early and aggressive use of hemostatic products combined with red blood cells (RBCs) as the primary resuscitation fluids to avoid rapid deterioration into the “bloody vicious cycle” and the classic lethal triad of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy.<sup>202</sup> Two very distinct paradigms of hemostatic resuscitation have emerged: (1) the DCR model, which uses preemptive administration of empiric ratios of blood and hemostatic products to approximate whole blood, often according to an established institutional massive transfusion protocol<sup>203–205</sup> (see Fig. 62.9); and (2) goal- directed hemostatic resuscitation approaches (also often protocol based), which generally use point- of- care viscoelastic monitoring combined with the prompt administration of hemostatic concentrates<sup>206–208</sup> (see also Chapter 46). The DCR model is typically employed during Phase 1, Uncontrolled Hemorrhage, with a transition to more goal- directed hemostatic resuscitation during Phase 2, Controlled Hemorrhage.

In addition to permissive hypotension and limited crystalloid usage, DCR relies on administering empiric ratios of blood and hemostatic products to restore blood volume. In a retrospective review of combat casualties, Borgman and colleagues<sup>209</sup> found a mortality rate of 65% in patients receiving less than 1 unit plasma for every four units of RBCs, but only 20% in those with a ratio of 1:2 or above. Evidence exists of a survivor bias because patients bleeding more rapidly were likely to die after receiving RBCs but before plasma could reach the bedside. Although the issue of survivor bias does exist, this approach has been substantiated in published reviews.<sup>210</sup> Currently, a ratio of 1:1:1 is most commonly adopted, although some experts believe that the amount of FFP can be reduced in most cases. In the only major, randomized trial comparing a 1:1:1 ratio with a 1:1:2 ratio of plasma, platelets, and RBCs, respectively, the transfusion of less plasma and platelets was not associated with a difference in mortality. However, the 1:1:1 group achieved hemostasis more rapidly and had fewer deaths due to exsanguination by 24 hours.<sup>211</sup>

### Phase 2, Controlled Hemorrhage

Once significant bleeding has been controlled, the anesthesia team can generally employ a more individualized, tailored approach to resuscitation (see Fig. 62.8). In Phase 2, there should be a continued utilization of point- of- care testing such as arterial blood gas and other laboratory test results. At this point, use of viscoelastic monitoring parameters and physiologic data should drive decision making. Assessment of oxygen delivery and adequacy of perfusion can begin to incorporate targeted resuscitation measures such as lactate and base deficit. The use of other monitoring modalities, such as transesophageal echocardiography, may help quantify and guide the need for further intravascular volume replacement.

When surgical bleeding is corrected, the depth and duration of shock in combination with multisystem trauma may still contribute to coagulopathy. This requires ongoing assessments such as serial evaluations with viscoelastic coagulation monitoring. A transition from high ratio resuscitation towards a more tailored approach can be accomplished using an algorithm (Figs. 62.9 and 62.10) to guide specific product selection and limit exposure to unnecessary blood products.

Additionally, during Phase 2, attention to other physiologic considerations should proceed. For example,

hypothermia is commonly present on initial presentation. During Phase 1, all attempts should be made to initiate active fluid and surface warming though hypothermia is often difficult to entirely correct during a massive resuscitation. During Phase 2, additional measures can be instituted as the focus shifts from the control of hemorrhage to the stabilization of all physiologic processes.

### RESUSCITATION FLUIDS

Isotonic crystalloids (normal saline, lactated Ringer solution, Plasma- Lyte) are the initial resuscitative fluids administered to trauma patients (Chapter 43). They have the advantage of being inexpensive, readily available, nonallergenic, noninfectious, and efficacious in restoring total body

fluid. They are easy to store and administer, mix well with infused medications, and can rapidly warm to body temperature. Solutions such as Plasma- Lyte and Normosol® have no calcium and a favorable strong ion difference, making these preparations potentially advantageous for patients with acidosis from hemorrhagic shock.<sup>236</sup> Disadvantages of crystalloids include their lack of O2- carrying capacity, their lack of coagulation capability, and limited intravascular half- life. Of note, data have implicated specific crystalloid solutions as immunosuppressants and triggers of cellular apoptosis.<sup>237</sup> Unlike necrosis, apoptosis is highly regulated and involves gene modulation and complex signal transduction pathways. It seems clear that apoptosis is an essential element of reperfusion injury. In a rat model of controlled hemorrhage, animals receiving lactated Ringer solution showed an immediate increase in apoptosis in the liver and small intestine after resuscitation.<sup>238</sup> Neither whole blood nor hypertonic saline increased the amount of apoptosis.

Hypertonic saline solutions, with or without the addition of polymerized dextran (HS or HSD), have been extensively studied in resuscitation from hemorrhagic shock.<sup>239,240</sup> In theory, HS will draw fluid into the vascular space from the interstitium and thereby reverse some of the non- hemorrhagic fluid loss caused by shock and ischemia. A given amount of HS will thus have an enhanced ability to restore intravascular volume in contrast to an equivalent volume of an isotonic solution. This has made HS a popular choice for fluid resuscitation under austere conditions. Multiple studies of otherwise lethal hemorrhage in animals have demonstrated improved survival after resuscitation with HSD versus either normal saline solution or the components of HSD alone.<sup>240</sup> Studies of the efficacy of HSD in trauma patients have been inconclusive<sup>241</sup> ; the most apparent benefit occurred in a subset of polytraumatized patients with both hemorrhage and TBI, in whom improved neurologic status was demonstrated in those who received HSD as a resuscitation fluid. Indeed, HS is commonly used as an osmotic agent in managing TBI with increased ICP.<sup>242</sup> In one systematic review of 13 studies with 593 patients, HS was associated with decreased all- cause mortality compared with mannitol and other concentrations of HS.<sup>243</sup> A more rigorous Cochrane review did not identify strong evidence to support the use of HS over mannitol in efficacy and safety in the long- term management of acute TBI.<sup>244</sup>

Colloids _,_ including starch solutions and albumin, have been advocated for rapid plasma intravascular volume expansion (see Chapter 43). Like crystalloids, colloids are readily available, easily stored and administered, and relatively inexpensive, although significantly more expensive when compared solely to crystalloids. As with hypertonic solutions, colloids increase intravascular volume by drawing free water back into the vascular space. When IV access is limited, colloid resuscitation restores intravascular volume more rapidly than crystalloids and at a lower volume of administered fluid. Colloids do not transport O2 or facilitate clotting; their dilutional effect is similar to that of crystalloids. Systematic reviews show no benefit of colloids over crystalloids in trauma resuscitation.<sup>245</sup> Concerns have been expressed about specific colloids such as 6% hetastarch and an adverse effect on renal function and mortality (see also Chapter 43).<sup>246–248</sup>

Many of the risks associated with aggressive intravascular fluid administration are related to the dilution of the circulating blood volume. Recognition of this fact and continued improvement in the safety of donated blood led to increased use of blood products in managing early hemorrhagic shock (see also Chapter 45). Maintaining an adequate hematocrit decreases the risk of systemic ischemia, and the potential for dilutional coagulopathy can be reduced by the early administration of plasma. The composition of resuscitation fluids may be as important as the rate and timing of administration. A 4- year retrospective review of a cohort of critically injured patients who underwent emergency surgery examined the outcomes of short- term care based on the number of units of blood transfused.<sup>249</sup> One hundred and forty- one patients received massive blood transfusions (≥20 units of RBCs) during preoperative and intraoperative resuscitation. The number of blood units did not differ between survivors (30%) and non- survivors (70%). Eleven variables were significantly different: aortic clamping for control of arterial blood pressure, use of inotropic drugs, time with systolic blood pressure higher than 90 mm Hg, time in the OR, temperature less than 34°C, urine output, pH lower than 7.0, PaO2/FiO2 ratio less than 150, PaCO2 more than 50 mm Hg, K<sup>+</sup> more than 6 mM/L, and calcium less than 2 mM/L. Of these, the presence of the first three variables and a transfusion of more than 30 units of packed RBCs was universally fatal. Total blood loss and the amount of transfused blood were less critical than the depth and duration of the shock. These concerns led to the concept of damage control surgery, and DCR, discussed earlier in this chapter, which emphasizes rapid control of active hemorrhage.<sup>250</sup> Indeed, since the inception of DCR, which aims to limit crystalloids during resuscitation in favor of appropriate blood component therapy, DCR has been associated with improved outcomes for over two decades.<sup>251</sup>

RBCs are the mainstay of treatment of hemorrhagic shock. With an average hematocrit of 50% to 60%, a unit of RBCs will predictably restore O2- carrying capacity and expand intravascular volume as well as any colloid solution. RBCs of blood type A, B, or AB carry major incompatibility antigens that may precipitate a lethal transfusion reaction if given to a patient with the opposite blood type. Because RBCs also have dozens of minor antigens that can cause reactions in susceptible patients, cross- matching is desirable when time allows (typically 1 hour from the time a sample reaches the blood bank until the RBCs reach the patient). Type- specific blood requires less time for delivery from the blood bank (usually ∼30 minutes) and may be an appropriate alternative in some situations. Type O blood— the universal donor type—can be given to patients of any blood type with little risk of a major reaction.<sup>252</sup> This is the preferred approach for patients who arrive at the ED in hemorrhagic shock. If O- positive blood is given to a Rhesusnegative woman who survives, prophylactic administration of an anti- Rh0 antibody is indicated. O-neg blood should available for transfusion of women of child bearing age.

Risks of RBC administration include transfusion reaction, the transmission of infectious agents, and hypothermia (see also Chapter 45). For example, RBCs are stored at 4°C and will decrease the patient’s temperature rapidly if not infused through a warming system during administration.

Plasma requires blood typing but not cross-matching; the delay in the availability of plasma is caused by the need

to thaw frozen units before they can be administered. Busy trauma hospitals will often maintain a supply of pre- thawed plasma (thawed fresh plasma as opposed to FFP) that can be issued quickly in response to an emergency need; in smaller hospitals, it is vital to request plasma early in resuscitation if it is likely to be needed. Bustling centers may keep 2 to 4 units of pre- thawed type AB neg (universal donor) plasma available in the trauma resuscitation unit or emergency department. Units are kept ready for two days at a time; if not used on an emergency basis, the units are returned to the blood bank and released to the next patient needing plasma. Whether this approach improves outcomes has not yet been studied, though, in one major multicenter prehospital study, early administration of FFP before trauma center arrival was associated with lower 30- day mortality (23.2% vs. 33.0%; difference, −9.8 percentage points; 95% CI, −18.6% to −1.0%; _P_ = .03).<sup>253</sup>

Platelet transfusion should generally be reserved for patients with clinical coagulopathy with a documented low serum level (&lt;50,000 per high- power field). When the patient is in shock, however, and blood loss is likely to be substantial, platelets should be empirically administered in proportion to RBCs and plasma (1:1:1), as discussed earlier for DCR. Transfused platelets have a very short serum half- life and should be issued only to patients with active coagulopathic bleeding. Platelets should not be administered through filters, warmers, or rapid infusion systems because they will bond to the inner surfaces of these devices, thereby reducing the number of platelets reaching circulation (see also Chapter 46). Platelet specific filters (170-260 micrometer pore size) should be used in order to remove large debris and fibrin clots.

Rapid transfusion of banked blood carries the risk of inducing citrate intoxication in the recipient.<sup>254</sup> Harvested blood units are treated with citrate to bind free Ca<sup>2+</sup> and thus inhibit the clotting cascade. Consecutive administration of multiple units of banked blood leads to a correspondingly large dose of citrate, which may overwhelm the body’s ability to mobilize free Ca<sup>2+</sup> and have a profound negative inotropic effect on the heart. Unrecognized hypocalcemia is a cause of hypotension in patients after massive transfusion and persists despite an adequate volume of resuscitation. In a hemorrhaging patient, ionized Ca<sup>2+</sup> levels should be measured at regular intervals, and Ca<sup>2+</sup> should be administered as needed (in a separate IV line from transfusion products) to maintain serum levels greater than 1.0 mmol/L.

### RESUSCITATION EQUIPMENT

Intravascular fluid resuscitation of any kind is impossible without IV access. Immediate placement of at least two large- bore catheters (16 gauge or larger) is recommended during the primary assessment of any trauma patient.<sup>52</sup> Practitioners should have a low threshold for placement of a large- caliber central line in patients with unsuccessful antecubital or other peripheral placement attempts. Potential sites for central line placement include the internal jugular, subclavian, and femoral veins, each of which has its benefits and potential risks. The internal jugular approach, though familiar to most anesthesiologists, will require the removal of the cervical collar and manipulation of the patient’s neck and is not recommended in the acute setting unless other options have been exhausted. The femoral vein is easily and rapidly accessed and is an appropriate choice

<!-- PDF page 26 -->

in patients without apparent pelvic or thigh trauma who require urgent drug or fluid administration. Caution should be used in patients with penetrating trauma to the abdomen because fluids infused via the femoral vein may contribute to hemorrhage from an injury to the inferior vena cava or iliac vein; these patients should have IV access placed above the diaphragm if possible. Femoral vein catheterization carries a high risk for the formation of deep venous thrombosis (DVT),<sup>255</sup> thus limiting the use of this approach in the acute setting. Femoral lines should be removed immediately after the patient’s condition stabilizes. The subclavian vein is the most common site for early and ongoing central access in a trauma patient because the subclavian region is easily visible and seldom directly traumatized. This approach carries the highest risk for the development of pneumothorax, although many patients will already have indications for tube thoracostomy in one or both chest cavities; when possible, this is the preferred side for placement of a subclavian line. Rapid infusion catheters (RIC) can be placed peripherally over a guide wire placed via in situ PIVs. They are a good alternative to central access when placement is time limited. Placement of an arterial line facilitates frequent laboratory analysis and allows close monitoring of blood pressure; this should be undertaken as soon as possible but should not impede other diagnostic or therapeutic maneuvers.

The anesthesiologist should work to maintain thermal equilibrium in any trauma patient. Deliberate hypothermia has been suggested as a management strategy for both hemorrhagic shock<sup>256</sup> and TBI,<sup>257</sup> there is insufficient evidence to support this approach. Hypothermia will potentiate dilutional coagulopathy and systemic acidosis while shivering, and vasoconstriction in response to cold will demand an additional metabolic effort that may predispose the patient to myocardial ischemia. Hypothermia also dramatically increases the subsequent risk for sepsis. Because many trauma patients arrive at the ED cold from exposure to the elements, early active warming measures are required. All IV fluids should be prewarmed or infused through a warming device. The patient should be covered with warmed blankets whenever possible, and the environment should be kept warm enough to make the patient comfortable. If hypothermia has already developed, forced hot air warming is strongly indicated to restore normothermia. Even though all these measures are routine and evident in the OR, the anesthesiologist can perform a valuable service by ensuring that they are available and applied in the ED, CT scanner, and angiography suite.

Commercial rapid- infusion devices are incredibly beneficial in trauma care, particularly in hemorrhagic shock. These machines offer benefits when large quantities of fluid resuscitation are likely (Box 62.7).

Early experience with these devices demonstrated higher patient temperature and reduced acidosis after the initial surgery,<sup>258</sup> although rapid infusers may contribute to over- infusion of fluids, inappropriately increased arterial blood pressure, and contribute to rebleeding.<sup>259</sup> Application of the principles described earlier for permissive hypotension can prevent this complication; basal fluid administration should be kept low (200 to 500 mL/h), with small fluid boluses given in response to measured systolic blood pressure less than 80 to 90 mm Hg. ## Trauma to the Central Nervous System

CNS trauma accounts for almost half of all trauma death postmortem examinations in population- based analyses<sup>260</sup> or after trauma center admission.<sup>261</sup> Although not as prevalent, acute traumatic spinal cord injury (SCI) remains a significant problem.<sup>262</sup> As with hemorrhagic shock, CNS trauma consists of the primary injury, in which tissue is disrupted by mechanical force, and secondary response, in which the body’s reaction to the injury plays an important role. Mitigation of secondary CNS injury depends on prompt diagnosis and early, goal- directed therapy. Although primary CNS injuries cannot be minimized other than with preventive strategies, secondary injury accounts for much of the death and disability that follows trauma.<sup>263</sup> The initial management of these patients can significantly affect the outcome. Attention to the ABCDE approach is paramount to successful resuscitation, and the trauma anesthesiologist should be intimately involved in this process.

Trauma produces shear forces that primarily damage neuronal cell bodies, axons, and vasculature. The pathophysiologic processes of secondary injury include metabolic failure, oxidative stress, and a cascade of biochemical and molecular events leading to delayed necrotic and apoptotic cell death.<sup>263</sup> Secondary injury is often exacerbated by tissue hypoxia or ischemia and by inflammatory responses, and many interacting factors influence outcomes from TBI. Individual drugs such as free radical scavengers, antiinflammatory agents, and ion channel blockers have been effective in animals but have had little impact or yielded disappointing results in human trials. TBI cannot predict the long- term outcome; thus, full resuscitative efforts should be initiated and continued, even in the most severely injured. In a review of the Trauma Registry of the German Society for Trauma Patients, outcome prediction was most accurate using the GCS motor score and pupil reactivity. A good outcome was still seen in patients with bilateral fixed and dilated pupils in 8% of patients.<sup>264</sup>

A retrospective review of patients with isolated TBI found a frequent incidence of subsequent organ failure: 89% developed dysfunction of at least one non- neurologic organ system. ### TRAUMATIC BRAIN INJURY AND CONCOMITANT TRAUMA

Patients with isolated head injuries can be managed with traditional ventilatory strategies. Still, those with chest trauma, aspiration, or massive resuscitation after a shock are at high risk for the development of ARDS. The classic teaching of no or low- level positive end- expiratory pressure (PEEP) to prevent increased ICP is inappropriate because hypoxemia may not be corrected. With adequate intravascular volume resuscitation, PEEP does not increase ICP or decrease cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP)<sup>278</sup> and may reduce ICP due to improved cerebral oxygenation.<sup>279</sup> Patients who develop ARDS after TBI are at risk for cerebral hypoxia.<sup>280</sup> A double- hit model has been proposed as the cause of ARDS secondary to CNS trauma – severe TBI leads to a systemic inflammatory response, which then primes the lungs to be more susceptible to injurious ventilator strategies or other mediators of lung injury. ARDS and cardiac insufficiency after TBI may lead to compromised systemic oxygenation and, therefore, a decrease in cerebral O2 delivery.<sup>281</sup> Hyperventilation therapy (e.g., at a PaCO2 of 25 mm Hg), long a mainstay in managing patients with TBI, is no longer recommended as a prophylactic treatment.<sup>197</sup> A range of PaCO2 between 30 and 35 mm Hg should be induced with hyperventilation to 30 mm Hg only for episodes of increased ICP that cannot be controlled with sedatives, drainage of cerebrospinal fluid, neuromuscular blockade, osmotic drugs, or barbiturate coma; targeting a lower PaCO2 is often a temporizing measure used as a bridge to definitive operative treatment.<sup>193</sup>

Hyperventilation during the first 24 hours after injury is of concern because of the critical reductions in perfusion during this timeframe. However, these recommendations should be taken in context and modified in the face of unstable clinical circumstances, such as an expanding mass lesion or signs of imminent herniation.<sup>282</sup>

Patients with concomitant TBI and hemorrhagic shock are at risk for worse coagulopathy before resuscitation and increased mortality.<sup>283</sup> A single episode of hypotension, defined as systolic blood pressure lower than 90 mm Hg, is associated with an increase in morbidity and doubled

### MANAGEMENT OF INTRACRANIAL PRESSURE IN THE OPERATING ROOM

Although most interventions for managing a patient with severe TBI will occur in the ICU, urgent cranial or noncranial surgery can be indicated. The previously described therapies should be continued throughout the perioperative period, including positional therapy (when possible), aggressive hemodynamic monitoring and resuscitation, administration of osmotic agents (with attention to maintaining euvolemia), and deep levels of analgesia and sedation. Appropriate anesthetic choices include opioids and low concentrations of volatile anesthetics. Pharmacologic management of TBI is the mainstay of the anesthesiologist’s role in the operating room. Intraoperative management of elevated ICP may be accomplished with osmotic diuretics or hypertonic saline. No difference was noted in longterm outcomes between mannitol and hypertonic saline on outcomes at six months. ### SPINAL CORD INJURY (SCI)

SCI after trauma affects some 13,000 Americans each year.<sup>262</sup> Blunt trauma accounts for most of SCI: 36% from motor vehicle collisions and 42% from falls, with only 4% from firearms. Cervical spine injuries occur in 1.5% to 3% of all major trauma. Over 50% of SCIs involve the cervical spine, usually between the C4 and C7 levels. Complete quadriplegia occurs in 11% of SCI cases. It is essential to realize that more than 40% of trauma patients with SCI may also have other significant injuries, including TBI. As lifespans continue to increase, more patients with SCI will survive to an older age.<sup>295,296</sup>

Most spinal injuries are in the lower cervical spine, just above the thorax, or in the upper lumbar region, just below the thorax. Blunt SCI is most common in the most flexible areas of the cord, especially at the junctions between flexible and inflexible segments. The vertebral column is divided longitudinally into three columns: anterior, middle,

and posterior; injuries to any two columns suggest biomechanical instability. These are the patients who will often require urgent surgical stabilization. Patients with unstable cervical spine injuries who meet the criteria for emergency intubation should undergo rapid sequence induction (see section above on protection of the cervical spine).<sup>269</sup> SCI at midthoracic levels is less common because of the rotational stabilization provided by the rib cage and intercostal musculature.

SCI is commonly accompanied by radiographically visible injury to the bony spine and concomitant disruption of the muscles, ligaments, and soft tissues that support it. However, clinically significant injury to the cervical spinal cord can occur without a visible skeletal injury. This entity, known as spinal cord injury without radiographic abnormality (SCIWORA), is more common in children. It is presumably the result of temporary hyperdistraction or torsion of the neck insufficient to disrupt the bony skeleton.<sup>297</sup>

Primary injury to the spinal cord sustained at the time of injury may be exacerbated by secondary factors (Fig. 62.13).

The combination of biochemical changes, vascular disruption, and electrolyte abnormalities can lead to cellular changes and worsening of SCI lesions up to 3 days after injury.<sup>298</sup> SCI includes sensory deficits, motor deficits, and both. Incomplete deficits may be worse on one side than the other and may improve rapidly in the first minutes after injury. Complete deficits—representing total spinal cord disruption at one level—are much more ominous, with generally slight improvement seen over time. Spine injuries above the level of T4–6 are accompanied by significant hypotension because of inappropriate vasodilatation, loss of cardiac inotropy, and bradycardia resulting from the denervation of the cardiac accelerator fibers (neurogenic shock). (Note the distinction between _neurogenic shock_ and the incorrect term _spinal shock,_ which refers to a loss of reflexes.) Functioning of the lower cord will gradually return, along with restoration of normal vascular tone. Diagnosing cervical spine instability can be difficult. The Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma has published guidelines regarding which patients require cervical spine radiographs, which views and studies should be obtained, and how to determine the absence of significant ligamentous injury in an obtunded patient.<sup>299</sup> For the obtunded patient, a high- quality CT scan without evidence of cervical SCI is considered adequate for clearance, although this is not universally accepted. Many institutions still require magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to rule out ligamentous injury in this patient population. The most commonly missed cervical fractures are at the C1–2 and C7–T1 levels, usually because of inadequate imaging.

Early intubation of the trachea is almost universally required for patients with cervical spine fractures and quadriplegia. Ventilatory support is almost always required for patients with a deficit above C4 because they will lack sufficient diaphragmatic function. Patients with levels from C6 to C7 may need support because of impairment in respiratory muscle function resulting from (1) loss of chest wall innervation, (2) paradoxical respiratory motion, (3) inability to clear secretions, and (4) decreased lung and chest wall compliance. ### INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT OF SPINAL CORD INJURY

A patient about to undergo surgical reduction and fixation of a spinal column fracture presents challenges for the anesthesiologist. First and foremost is the need for intubation of the trachea in a patient with a known injury to the cervical spine (see also Chapter 53). Direct laryngoscopy with in- line stabilization is appropriate in the emergency setting and in unconscious, combative, or hypoxemic patients when the spine’s status is unknown.<sup>306</sup> Video laryngoscopy and fiberoptic- assisted techniques might be associated with a higher first- attempt failure rate over controls. Still, no firm conclusions regarding the best method for tracheal intubation in patients at risk for worsening SCI can be made based on the available data.<sup>307</sup> Hence, no technique for airway management in patients with unstable SCI is superior for preventing neurologic deterioration.<sup>308,309</sup> Tracheal intubation can occur in the OR in an awake, alert, and cooperative patient by several methods known to produce less displacement of the cervical spine, presumably with less risk for worsening an unstable SCI. A common technique in current clinical practice is awake fiberoptic intubation. Although the nasal route is associated with an easier path to intubation in most patients, it can lead to an increased risk for sinusitis in the ICU if the trachea is not extubated at the end of the procedure. Oral intubation may be more challenging technically but will be of greater value if the patient remains mechanically ventilated. Blind nasal intubation and using an intubating LMA, video laryngoscopy, or any of a variety of other instrument systems for indirect laryngoscopy are acceptable. Clinicians are advised to use the equipment and techniques with which they are most

<!-- PDF page 32 -->

familiar. The critical concept is to successfully achieve tracheal intubation while minimizing the motion of the cervical spine and preserving the ability to assess neurologic function after positioning.<sup>75</sup>

A patient with a partial neurologic deficit and visible spinal canal impingement on imaging studies is considered an emergency because of the potential for regaining neurologic function after decompression. Timing for surgery suggests that earlier decompression may improve outcomes in some patients, particularly those with cervical injuries, although the exact timing remains controversial.<sup>310,311</sup> In motor complete thoracic SCI patients, a laminectomy has significantly increased the rate of successful spinal cord decompression.<sup>312</sup> Hemodynamic instability may complicate urgent and emergent spinal surgery. Hypotension from neurogenic shock is characterized by inappropriate bradycardia resulting from loss of cardiac accelerator function and unopposed parasympathetic tone. However, this situation can be difficult to distinguish from hypotension resulting from acute hemorrhage. A trial of fluid administration is still indicated, subject to the end points of resuscitation listed earlier. Once hemorrhage has been controlled or ruled out, patients who have maintenance of an increased MAP of more than 85 mm Hg for seven days after SCI may have improved functional recovery. This approach is highly controversial but remains a recommendation in treatment according to published guidelines.<sup>313</sup>

## Orthopedic and Soft Tissue Trauma

Musculoskeletal injuries are the most frequent indication of other operative management in most trauma centers (see Chapter 60). In addition to the physical handicaps and long- term morbidity that patients with orthopedic injuries may endure, long- term psychological trauma is also a prominent feature.<sup>314–316</sup> The length of many procedures, particularly in patients with multiple extremity injuries, necessitates attention to body positioning, maintenance of normothermia, fluid balance, and preservation of peripheral blood flow.

Musculoskeletal injuries represent common injury patterns seen in patients with high- energy mechanisms of injury and are one of the more common reasons for operative intervention in the trauma population.<sup>317,318</sup> The timing of operative intervention in polytrauma patients with orthopedic injuries has been a topic of considerable academic and clinical discussion. The process involves multidisciplinary communication among anesthesiologists, intensivists, orthopedic surgeons, and general trauma surgeons. Discussions should address patient physiology and specific injury patterns that include, but are not limited to, concomitant TBI, blunt cerebral vascular injury, blunt aortic injury, pulmonary contusion, and intra- abdominal trauma (i.e., hepatic, splenic injuries, and open abdomen).

Historically, injuries to the extremities in multisystem trauma patients who were too critically injured for definitive operative intervention were managed with traction and prolonged immobilization. These patients experienced high rates of pulmonary failure and prolonged mechanical ventilation, developed sepsis frequently, and had increased

mortality rates.<sup>319</sup> Specifically, pulmonary complications have been a common occurrence in this population of trauma patients, with nearly 30% of patients with multiple extremity injuries experiencing pulmonary morbidity. In one retrospective review of 582 patients with femur fractures, definitive fixation was delayed until lactate concentrations were decreased below 2.5 mmol/L<sup>320</sup> ; ARDS, death, and ICU length of stay were improved for patients who were resuscitated before definitive fixation. Therefore, the goal of fracture management in the multisystem trauma patient revolves around restoring musculoskeletal anatomy that allows for mobilization, pulmonary toilet, and adequate pain control; definitive operations are often deferred until the patient is physiologically optimized.

Multiple scoring systems and criteria have been described to identify severely injured patients (i.e., ISS >15) in whom it is safe to proceed with early definitive fixation of an extremity injury.<sup>321–323</sup> Nahm _et al._ reported that the following physiologic criteria could be used to risk- stratify patients who are appropriate for definitive fracture fixation: pH ≥7.25, lactate &lt;4, base excess ≥−5.5.<sup>321</sup> Considerable attention to ICP is also necessary for patients with TBI, such that intracranial hypertension and marked elevations in TBI that may occur with changes in position should be contraindications to early definitive fracture care.<sup>324</sup> While substantial evidence supports early fracture in severely injured trauma patients, it should be recognized that many of these studies are retrospective in design with certain limitations.<sup>325–329</sup> Nonetheless, there is a certain clinical benefit to early operative intervention with regard to fewer days of mechanical ventilation and lower complication rates.<sup>325</sup> In those severely injured patients in whom it is not feasible to perform early definitive fixation due to either altered cardiopulmonary physiology, acid–base status, and/or concomitant TBI, temporary external fixation with internal fixation occurring at a later date (i.e., days to weeks after initial injury) when appropriate physiology has been restored is a valuable option.<sup>330</sup>

### SPECIFIC ORTHOPEDIC CONDITIONS

Hip fracture is common, morbid, and costly among older adults (see Chapter 60). Frequently, patients with isolated hip fractures present to the OR for urgent repair. Prior beliefs that regional anesthesia is associated with decreased odds of inpatient mortality and pulmonary complications among patients with hip fractures in contrast to general anesthesia have since been debunked with more recent systematic reviews.<sup>331</sup> in the REGAIN (Regional versus General Anesthesia for Promoting Independence after Hip Fracture) trial, similar rates of postoperative ambulation and survival at 60 days were observed when spinal anesthesia was compared to general anesthesia for hip fracture surgery in patients over the age of 50.<sup>332</sup> A secondary analysis of this trial revealed more pain 24 hours after surgery and more postoperative prescription analgesic use in patients who received spinal anesthesia than general anesthesia.<sup>333</sup>

In patients with isolated extremity or hip fractures without polytrauma, the evidence remains clear that early definitive fracture care improves outcomes. In hip fracture patients, current United States<sup>334</sup> and Canadian<sup>335</sup> guidelines recommend surgery within 48 hours. Within

the United Kingdom, surgery within 36 hours is a quality of care indicator, although adherence to these guidelines is often inconsistent.<sup>336</sup>

Hip dislocation is common after high- energy trauma and is frequently accompanied by an acetabulum fracture. Whereas the fracture itself can be safely managed on a delayed basis or nonoperatively, the dislocation is a medical emergency that must be promptly addressed if the patient is to have a good functional outcome. Failure to promptly diagnose and reduce a dislocated hip joint is a significant risk factor for avascular necrosis of the femoral head. Reduction typically requires a very deep level of sedation, which may be facilitated by a nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockade. For this reason, the anesthesiologist is commonly involved.<sup>337</sup> Although a dislocated hip can be reduced in a spontaneously breathing sedated patient, an acutely injured patient is at high risk for aspiration of gastric contents. Any patient undergoing surgery soon (such as for an open long- bone fracture or exploratory laparotomy) can be tracheally intubated at the time of reduction and maintained with appropriate sedation and analgesia until reaching the OR. Other patients who may require intubation even for uncomplicated reductions include those who are inebriated or uncooperative, hemodynamically unstable, or suffering from pulmonary dysfunction. Additionally, successful postoperative fracture reduction is more challenging in morbidly obese patients than in those with average body mass index.<sup>338</sup> Unlike acetabular fractures, a fracture of the pelvic ring requires immediate recognition and management by the trauma team. Hemorrhage, even exsanguination, is common after a significant pelvic ring fracture and is a leading contributor to early death after motor vehicle collisions. Bleeding occurs from multiply disrupted venous beds in the posterior pelvic bowl; if the pelvis is unstable, no anatomic barrier exists to the continued expansion of this retroperitoneal bleeding. Surgical exploration by way of the peritoneum is usually unrewarding because the bleeding vessels are not easily accessed.<sup>339</sup> In the past, therapy consisted of supportive volume resuscitation, external fixation of the unstable pelvis, and angiography. Despite this multidisciplinary approach, mortality remained frequent in these patients, partially because of the inability of angioembolization to control venous bleeding from the rich venous plexuses associated with the most severe fractures. Many US trauma centers have adopted the technique of preperitoneal packing. Preperitoneal packing via laparotomy is a rapid method for controlling pelvic fracture–related hemorrhage that can supplant the need for emergent angiography and reduces transfusion requirements and mortality.<sup>340</sup> Endotracheal intubation is usually undertaken on an emergency basis in a hypotensive patient. The anesthesiologist may remain with the patient throughout the initial hours of stabilization to manage sedation, analgesia, transport, and ongoing resuscitation needs. In the absence of an orthopedic specialist, temporary stabilization and tamponade of some pelvic fractures can be accomplished with a specially made pelvic binder or a bed sheet knotted tightly around the bony pelvis.<sup>341</sup>

Mechanically unstable pelvic ring fractures are associated with increased fluid and blood resuscitation volumes, significantly more frequent presence of concomitant

injuries, increased ventilation and ICU stay, rate of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), sepsis, and mortality rate.<sup>342</sup> In some cases, patients with pelvic fractures may present for operative procedures with or without early interventions such as placement of a pelvic binder or resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA). REBOA is becoming a more commonly utilized temporary hemorrhage control technique for patients with hemodynamically significant pelvic fractures.<sup>343</sup>

Patients with pelvic fractures can initially be divided into two groups: (1) stable pelvic fracture; or (2) displaced pelvic ring fractures with hemodynamic instability or high risk for deterioration. This second group has the highest risk for complications and mortality related to their injuries. The presence of associated injuries to the head, chest, and abdomen can lead to conflicting priorities in managing these patients. For example, a CT finding of a high- grade splenic injury in the setting of a severe pelvic injury and hemodynamic instability may require an exploratory laparotomy before addressing the pelvic injury. These patients may present for operative procedures with or without early interventions such as placement of a pelvic binder or REBOA. As the unstable trauma patient is moved to the angiography suite or hybrid OR, resuscitation should maintain a sufficient perfusion pressure; survival is higher if this transition occurs within 3 hours of presentation.<sup>344</sup> Management of these interventions in the OR requires an understanding of the anatomy, physiology, and surgical considerations involved in their employment in the operative setting, as discussed above.

Open fractures should be pulse- lavaged and débrided as soon as possible after the injury to minimize the risk of infectious complications. If ongoing resuscitation or unstable TBI precludes the patient from early management in the OR, this procedure can be performed at the bedside. Despite the frequency of open fractures, their management remains one of the most significant and debated orthopedic challenges. The timing of surgical fixation in open fractures is often surgeon- specific or center- specific because little consensus exists among orthopedic surgeons as to optimal treatment.<sup>345</sup>

The advantages and disadvantages of regional and general anesthesia are summarized in Boxes 62.8 and 62.9.

Regional anesthesia has been reported to be associated with improved postoperative morbidity and mortality<sup>346–348</sup> (including cognitive measures of function). Acute compartment syndrome of the extremities is defined as a “condition in which increased pressure within a limited space compromises the circulation and function of the tissues within that space.”<sup>357</sup> The most common cause of compartment syndrome is edema secondary to muscle injury and associated hematoma formation. Although most associated with traumatic injuries, compartment syndrome can also occur because of trauma- related causes, including reperfusion injury, burns, drug overdose, and prolonged limb compression (Box 62.10). The most common fractures associated with the development of compartment syndrome are those of the tibial shaft (40%) and forearm (18%).<sup>358,359</sup> Forearm compartment syndrome requiring fasciotomy predominantly affects males and can occur after a fracture or soft tissue injury. Soft tissue injuries cause a further 23% of cases without fracture.<sup>360</sup> Although no randomized, prospective trials have been conducted, regional anesthesia does not blunt detection of compartment syndromes if there is not an associated dense motor block.<sup>361</sup>

Fasciotomy is usually indicated when compartment pressure approaches 20 to 30 mm Hg below diastolic pressure in any patient with a worsening clinical condition, a documented rising tissue pressure, significant soft tissue injury, or a history of 4 to 6 hours of total ischemia of an extremity. Compartment syndrome is suspected in the awake when pain is experienced, by the patient, beyond what is expected for the given injury. Prophylactic fasciotomy may be indicated in patients with warm ischemic time of more than 2 hours, ligation of the major veins in the popliteal region or distal part of the thigh, and crush injuries. Early or prophylactic fasciotomy decreases subsequent muscle loss.<sup>362</sup>

Crush syndrome is the general manifestation of crush injury caused by prolonged continuous pressure on one or more extremities. It is commonly found in patients who have been trapped in one position for an extended period.<sup>363,364</sup> Muscle injury secondary to ischemia causes myoglobinuria, which can lead to acute renal failure and subsequent profound electrolyte disturbances. The most critical treatment consists of crystalloid fluid resuscitation; a total body fluid deficit of 15 L may occur in severe rhabdomyolysis.<sup>365</sup> Osmotic diuresis with mannitol and alkalization of urine with sodium bicarbonate to prevent precipitation of myoglobin in the renal tubules remain controversial.<sup>366</sup>

## Other Traumatic Injuries

### HEAD AND NECK SURGERY

Except for emergency exploration of penetrating trauma to Zone II of the neck (from the clavicles up to the angle of the mandible), most surgical interventions for head and neck trauma will occur during the subacute phase after complete resuscitation and secondary diagnostic studies. Anesthetic management of these patients is not substantially different from that for similar elective procedures, although coexisting injuries may influence patient positioning, airway management, and ventilator settings.<sup>370</sup> Surgery on the mandible and maxilla will be facilitated by nasotracheal intubation. Still, a secure airway should not be jeopardized by attempting to switch from an oral to a nasal tube in a patient in whom visualization of the larynx might be difficult because of traumatic swelling or body habitus. It is safer in these cases for the surgeon to either work around an oral tube secured behind the second molar (to allow dental occlusion) or place a tracheostomy if the need for intubation and mechanical ventilation is likely to be protracted. Surgery on the zygoma and the nasal, orbital, and ethmoid bones will be possible with an oral ETT. Securing the ETT to the molar with a fine- gauge wire will help stabilize it throughout the operation. All these surgeries will lead to significant soft tissue swelling in the immediate postoperative period, often necessitating several days of continued intubation and sedation until sufficient venous drainage has occurred to allow safe extubation of the trachea. Though not definitive, the presence of an air leak when the ETT cuff is deflated suggests that the airway will remain patent once the tube is removed.

### CHEST INJURIES

### Pulmonary

Injuries to the lung parenchyma that produce pneumothorax can be managed by tube thoracostomy to relieve tension, drain accumulated blood, and apply suction to the pleural space until the air leak spontaneously resolves (see also Chapter 49). Bleeding from the low- pressure pulmonary circulation is usually self- limited. Thoracotomy is uncommon but becomes necessary when evidence exists of mediastinal injury, chest tube output exceeds 1500 mL in the first hours after injury, tracheal or bronchial injury and massive air leak are apparent, or the patient is hemodynamically unstable with evident thoracic pathology.<sup>52</sup> Blood collected from the pleural space is generally free of clotting factors and can be directly reinfused with any of several commercial systems.<sup>371</sup> However, concern has been expressed that this may be prothrombotic and potentially harmful.<sup>372</sup> Hemorrhage necessitating surgery may be from injured intercostal or internal mammary arteries or the lung parenchyma. Staple resection of injured lung or anatomic lobectomy is not uncommon, particularly after penetrating trauma.

If the resultant damage is an incomplete tear, it may heal with stenosis, subsequent atelectasis, pneumonia, pulmonary destruction, and sepsis. When surgery is

required for a delayed, incomplete tracheobronchial injury, pulmonary resection may be necessary if significant tissue destruction has occurred. In contrast, complete transection may be amenable to reconstruction preserving pulmonary tissue. The level of injury dictates the surgical approach. Cervical injuries are approached through a transverse neck incision, left bronchial injuries via a left thoracotomy, and tracheal or right main bronchial injuries via a right thoracotomy. In the cervical region, it is sometimes possible to access a longitudinal tear of the posterior membranous trachea by opening the anterior trachea and operating around the ETT.

### Traumatic Aortic Injury

Traumatic aortic injury (TAI) must be ruled out in any patient with a high- energy injury, such as occurs with a motor vehicle accident or a fall from height. The incidence of TAI has decreased in recent years because of the increased presence of airbags in motor vehicles; side- impact collisions have caused most cases in the past decade.<sup>380</sup> Aortic injury occurs most commonly just distal to the left subclavian artery. It results from sheer forces between the mobile heart, aortic arch, and the immobile descending thoracic aorta. TAI is manifested as a continuum of injury from a small intimal flap to free transection contained by the surrounding mediastinum and pleura. The diagnosis is most commonly made by a CT scan of the chest with IV contrast which has a sensitivity comparable to aortography.<sup>381</sup> Surgical or endovascular repair is indicated for most patients with TAI because of the high risk for rupture in the hours and days after injury, with the endovascular repair being the preferred option in the absence of contraindication. Selective nonoperative management of high- risk patients with TAI has been described.<sup>382,383</sup> Treatment is similar to that in patients with uncomplicated type B aortic dissection and consists of β- adrenergic blockade to minimize the cardiac rate–pressure product (shearing force).

### Rib Fractures

Rib fractures are the most common injury resulting from blunt chest trauma. The fracture generally requires no specific treatment and will heal spontaneously over several weeks. Therapy aims to minimize pulmonary complications secondary to these fractures, such as pain, splinting, atelectasis, hypoxemia, and pneumonia. Rib fractures in older adults (older than 55) are of particular concern. Elderly patients with rib fractures have twice the mortality and thoracic morbidity of younger patients with similar injuries.<sup>384</sup> Epidural anesthesia is used liberally in some patients with severe pain, older adults, and patients with preexisting compromised pulmonary function. Pain relief is superior with epidural analgesia; however, a meta- analysis failed to identify a reduction in mortality.<sup>385</sup> Nonetheless, current guidelines for pain management in patients with blunt thoracic trauma recommend epidural analgesia over nonregional pain control modalities.<sup>386</sup> Epidural analgesia may minimize or avoid complications of splinting and pain, such as hypoxemia, hypoventilation, the need for tracheal intubation, and the possibility of pneumonia. Various non- neuraxial blocks are also available, depending on the location of the rib fractures. These blocks include serratus blocks, paravertebral blocks, and erector spinae blocks, all

<!-- PDF page 37 -->

of which are becoming more prevalent due to the increased use of ultrasound for regional anesthesia.<sup>387</sup> Endotracheal intubation is reserved for patients who are unable to oxygenate or ventilate or who require protection of the airway.

Fractures of multiple neighboring ribs will result in the flail chest syndrome, characterized by paradoxical chest wall motion during spontaneous ventilation. Not all patients with a flail chest require positive- pressure ventilation. Patients who are not initially intubated should be observed closely in the ICU for signs of worsening respiratory function. Increasing cases of noninvasive positive- pressure ventilation (NIPPV) for lung injury secondary to trauma have been reported.<sup>388</sup> For patients who subsequently require intubation for a surgical procedure, the anesthesiologist will need to determine the safety of extubation postoperatively. NIPPV may be associated with fewer cases of pneumonia, which may lead to fewer tracheostomies and decreased ICU length of stay. A successful technique has been to proceed with extubation, transitioning to a mask capable of delivering continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP).<sup>389</sup>

Concomitant pulmonary injury, especially lung contusion, is commonly associated with a flail chest. Pulmonary contusion will cause shunting, which will lead to hypoxemia. This syndrome may progress rapidly in the hours and days after injury. An initially clear chest radiograph does not exclude the possibility of a pulmonary contusion, and close observation is warranted if signs of significant chest wall trauma are present. As with all patients after traumatic injury, a high degree of suspicion and a continuous search for missed injuries is warranted. No specific therapy exists for a pulmonary contusion; treatment is directed at the associated injuries or resultant hypoxemia. Early and aggressive implementation of a lung- protective strategy may have a role in patients with a significant pulmonary contusion to minimize progression to ARDS.

### Cardiac Injury

Blunt cardiac injury is a rare and poorly understood phenomenon that must be considered in any patient who has sustained a frontal impact to the chest. Bruising or edema of the myocardium is functionally indistinguishable from myocardial ischemia. It may be causally related because the pathophysiology of cardiac contusion may involve forcible dislodgement of unstable atherosclerotic plaque. Blunt cardiac injury can be safely excluded if the patient is hemodynamically stable and the ECG does not demonstrate conduction disturbances or tachyarrhythmias.<sup>390</sup> If either a new tachyarrhythmia or conduction disturbance subsequently develops or the patient has unexplained hypotension, other causes (hypovolemia, renal failure) should be ruled out first. If the workup is negative, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) should be performed. Right ventricular dysfunction resulting in hypotension may be overlooked, while more common causes of hypotension in the trauma patient are being investigated. TEE is superior to TTE in obese patients or those with chest wall injuries, making it challenging to obtain adequate acoustic windows. Still, it will generally require intubation and deep sedation to perform. Once diagnosed, the blunt cardiac injury should be managed as ischemic cardiac injury, with the completion of resuscitation and careful control of fluid volumes,

administration of coronary vasodilators, and monitoring and symptomatic treatment of rhythm disturbances. Anticoagulation with aspirin or heparin should be approached on a case- by- case basis as determined by the patient’s other injuries. Cardiology consultation is appropriate if the patient may benefit from coronary angiography followed by angioplasty or stenting of stenotic vessels.

Patients with penetrating cardiac trauma and blunt trauma causing rupture of one or more chambers (usually the atria) are rarely seen by the trauma center because of the frequent rate of prehospital mortality.<sup>391</sup> Those who do not die immediately of free exsanguination into the thoracic cavity will have pericardial tamponade and can be extremely unstable in the first minutes after admission. This condition is diagnosed by clinical suspicion, focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST), and direct inspection during ED thoracotomy. Relief of the tamponade and clamp or suture control of the cardiac injury may allow restoration of spontaneous circulation and necessitate an urgent transition to the OR for definitive hemostasis and chest closure. Cardiopulmonary bypass may be required for support during the repair of cardiac injuries. Shock characterized by acidosis, but not hypotension, is the most common presentation in patients needing OR thoracotomy after penetrating thoracic trauma. Survival rates are excellent unless a pneumonectomy or damage control thoracotomy is required.<sup>392</sup>

### ABDOMINAL INJURY

The need for exploratory laparotomy, once the mainstay of the trauma surgeon, has declined because FAST and high- resolution CT have reduced the incidence of negative abdominal explorations. In contrast, angiographic embolization of hemorrhaging vessels in the liver and spleen has reduced the need for open procedures. When it does occur, urgent celiotomy will typically follow the principles of damage control, as described previously in this chapter.<sup>393</sup> The abdomen is opened and packed tightly in all four quadrants. Systematic exploration is undertaken in each quadrant, with the time taken only to control hemorrhage and rapid staple closure of open gastrointestinal injuries. The abdomen is packed open at the end of the procedure, a sterile drape is used to cover exposed viscera, and the patient is moved to the ICU to complete resuscitation (Damge Control Surgery). Definitive treatment of nonlethal injuries and restoration of bowel continuity is deferred until a second operation 24 to 48 hours later.

Anesthetic management of emergency celiotomy should follow the principles of early resuscitation outlined earlier. Adequate IV access is required, as well as continuous arterial pressure monitoring. The use of cell salvage devices can reduce the patient’s exposure to banked blood, although reinfusion is generally deferred if there has been significant contamination by bowel contents. A rapid infusion system is advantageous for preserving intravascular volume and normothermia during periods of heavy bleeding. Subsequent abdominal surgeries will be needed in hemodynamically stable patients and should not present unusual anesthetic challenges. Subsequent reconstructive procedures can become technically complex as scarring and adhesions develop; the anesthesiologist should be prepared

## Selected Patient Populations

### TRAUMA AND PREGNANCY

Trauma to pregnant patients is associated with a frequent risk for spontaneous abortion, preterm labor, or premature delivery, depending on the location and magnitude of the mother’s injury (see also Chapter 58).<sup>394</sup> Trauma is the leading nonobstetric cause of death in pregnant women, with nearly double the mortality rate.<sup>395</sup> Early consultation with an obstetrician is desirable for any pregnant trauma patient for immediate management and long- term follow- up. The best treatment for the developing fetus consists of rapid and complete resuscitation of the mother. Trauma patients in the first trimester of gestation may not realize they are pregnant; therefore, human chorionic gonadotropin testing is part of the initial laboratory studies for any injured woman of childbearing age. Severe trauma occurring during fetal organogenesis may induce birth defects or miscarriage because of the effects of medications, pelvic irradiation, or hemorrhage leading to placental ischemia. Indicated radiologic tests should not be deferred, but shielding of the pelvis should be provided whenever possible.<sup>396</sup> Patients who do not spontaneously miscarry should be advised of the potential risks for birth defects and be referred for counseling if desired.<sup>348</sup> Dilatation plus curettage of the uterus is advisable after miscarriage to avoid toxicity arising from retained products of conception.<sup>397</sup>

Trauma occurring in the second or third trimester of pregnancy necessitates an early ultrasonographic examination to determine fetal age, size, and viability. Monitoring the fetal heart rate is indicated if the pregnancy is sufficiently advanced and the fetus would be viable if delivered. Preterm labor is very common in this population and should be treated with β- adrenergic agonist drugs or magnesium at the direction of the obstetrician. Delivery should be delayed if the fetus is not an unacceptable metabolic stress on the mother. Delivery by cesarean section is indicated if the mother is _in extremis_ , if the uterus is hemorrhaging, or if the gravid uterus is impairing surgical control of abdominal or pelvic hemorrhage. Placental abruption can occur in response to substance abuse or abdominal trauma and can precipitate life- threatening uterine hemorrhage. Emergency cesarean section is indicated in these cases. The Kleihauer–Betke blood test can be used to determine if fetal blood has leaked into the maternal circulation<sup>398</sup> ; if positive, administration of anti- Rh0 immune globulin is recommended for any Rh- negative mother carrying an Rh- positive fetus. By the third trimester, the uterus is sufficiently enlarged to compress the inferior vena cava when the patient is positioned supine, thereby impairing venous return to the heart and contributing to hypotension. Left lateral uterine displacement is indicated to treat this problem. If the patient is immobilized on a long spine board because of concern for a thoracic or lumbar spinal fracture, the whole board can be tipped to the left. While the overall proportion of pediatric trauma patients compromises a minority of trauma center admissions, traumatic injuries remain the leading cause of death for children aged 1 to 14.<sup>399</sup> Motor vehicle crashes, drowning, falls, and burns constitute most traumatic injury mechanisms.<sup>400</sup> A disproportionate number of pediatric trauma patients (∼80%) have an associated traumatic brain injury; such injuries are responsible for more than half of all deaths.<sup>400,401</sup> The initial priorities of trauma assessment and management are the same for children and adults; nonetheless, several particularities must be considered, including differences in airway anatomy, the normal range of physiologic variables, and other anatomic differences (see also Chapter 72).<sup>52,401</sup>

Successful management of the pediatric airway constitutes the foundation and initial step in pediatric trauma resuscitation because hypoxia and respiratory compromise are the most common causes of cardiac arrest.<sup>401,402</sup> Preceding airway management, a high degree of caution must be exercised to immobilize the cervical spine in patients with dangerous mechanisms that increase the risk of spinal injuries. Some of these mechanisms include: falls from a height greater than one meter or five stairs, axial loading to the head, high- speed and/or rollover motor vehicle crashes, ejection from a motor vehicle, or accidents involving motorized recreational vehicles and bicycle collisions.<sup>403</sup> Spinal cord injury without radiologic abnormality (SCIWORA) may be present in up to 35% of children with high- risk mechanisms. This increased risk precludes cervical spine clearance in the same manner as adults.<sup>403,404</sup> Children have a high oxygen consumption rate and react to hypoxia with bradycardia, sometimes even with very brief periods of apnea.<sup>405</sup> The anesthesiologist should be prepared to encounter the following anatomic differences in pediatric patients when managing the airway: a prominent, protuberant occiput, a small oral cavity, a large tongue, the presence of large adenoids and tonsils, and a short and anteriorly displaced larynx.<sup>401,405</sup> These anatomic features can predispose to obstruction, bleeding, and a difficult airway. Pediatric patients should always be considered to have a full stomach, and rapid sequence induction and intubation are the gold standard for airway management.<sup>405</sup> In cases where intubation and mask ventilation are impossible, surgical cricothyroidotomy is considered unsafe in children younger than 12 years of age due to the small size of the cricothyroid membrane and proximity to vocal cords.<sup>52</sup> Needle cricothyroidotomy is preferred as a temporizing solution until other preparations are made. Emergent venous access in children can be challenging; intraosseous access is recommended if unable to obtain peripheral access.<sup>52</sup> Determination of weight is mandatory to dose medications appropriately and guide fluid and blood component resuscitation. If weight is unknown, a length- based resuscitation tape or specialty stretcher with an integrated scale may be used. Throughout the perioperative period, vigilance for hypothermia should be maintained because pediatric trauma patients are prone to developing hypothermia due

to a large surface- to- weight ratio, immature thermoregulation mechanisms, and the effects of general anesthetics.<sup>406</sup> Hypothermia can be particularly detrimental for pediatric trauma patients because up to a 10% decrease in clotting factor activity has been observed for every degree of Celsius below normothermia.<sup>406,407a</sup>

There are several unique aspects of pediatric trauma resuscitation that anesthesiologists should be familiar with. Due to differences in pediatric coagulation profiles (i.e., decreased levels of procoagulation protein and fewer coagulation inhibitors), thromboelastography has an unclear role in diagnosing and treating coagulation disorders.<sup>406</sup> There are no validated scoring systems to determine the need for massive transfusion in children, and a damage control resuscitation strategy (i.e., transfusion of packed red blood cells to fresh frozen plasma to platelets in a 1:1:1 ratio) has not been studied adequately in pediatric patients. The use of whole blood resuscitation was associated with improved survival in the pediatric population.<sup>407b</sup> Hypotensive resuscitation has not been studied in children and may be hazardous because baseline blood pressures in children closely approximate the lower limits of cerebral autoregulation.<sup>406,408,409</sup> The use of adjuncts such as tranexamic acid recombinant factor VIIa may have utility in limiting bleeding, but large studies in pediatric patients are presently lacking.<sup>408,410</sup>

### ELDERLY TRAUMA PATIENTS

As the world population continues to increase in age, elderly trauma patients become a growing part of the overall trauma population.<sup>411</sup> Overall mortality among elderly trauma patients is higher than among younger adults; injured patients over 74 years are at considerably higher risk for mortality.<sup>412</sup> Elderly trauma patients frequently present with lower Injury Severity Score but more often sustain severe head and lower extremities injuries.<sup>413</sup>

When managing elderly patients with trauma, the effects of aging on physiologic function and the impact of preexisting conditions and medications must be carefully considered. Equivalent traumatic injuries will have a markedly more serious outcome in the elderly than younger victims (see Chapter 61).<sup>412,414</sup> Elderly patients may be taking multiple medications, such as anticoagulants and beta blockers, which may worsen traumatic shock by causing severe bleeding and blunting of compensatory responses, respectively. Elderly patients may not have teeth or dentures to help maintain airway patency; edentulous patients are often difficult to mask ventilate, and dentures may loosen and obstruct the airway. Many older adults have decreased chest wall, and pulmonary compliance, diminished functional residual capacity, and decreased mucus clearance – all of which have contributed to a mortality double that found in younger patients.<sup>384</sup> In elderly patients with blunt thoracic trauma, each additional rib fracture increases mortality and the risk for pneumonia by over 20%. Degenerative spine changes and kyphoscoliosis may complicate intubation. The patient may have a pacemaker or automatic implantable cardioverter–defibrillator in place and artificial cardiac valves or stents. Patients of this age group are commonly anticoagulated pre injury. Older adults have thinner skin and are more prone to hypothermia and early development of pressure ulcers. Great care must be taken

during intraoperative positioning to avoid secondary pressure injuries. Potential causes of an altered mental status may include delirium, dementia, pain medication, sedatives, increased intracranial pressure, and decreased cerebral perfusion pressure, the latter two of which are the deadliest.

A tailored evaluation and management of elderly trauma patients is crucial for improving outcomes.<sup>415</sup> A higher hematocrit, achieved by tighter control of administered fluid, is generally recommended to maintain maximized tissue oxygen delivery. Posttraumatic myocardial dysfunction is a significant risk, particularly if the heart rate is elevated secondary to blood loss, pain, or anxiety. Complex operations or large blood loss should encourage the use of TEE, invasive arterial monitoring, and minimally-invasive monitors of cardiac output and volume status to guide fluid and inotropic therapy.<sup>198</sup> Older patients will have diminished requirements for postoperative analgesia and may respond to sedative medications with inappropriate agitation.

### JEHOVAH’S WITNESS PATIENTS

A trauma patient who refuses blood products requires special management. Early identification and control of hemorrhage are essential (see Chapter 45). Deliberate hypotension to limit bleeding may be helpful. Preoperative and intraoperative phlebotomy should be minimized. The use of salvaged red cells (from the intraoperative collection or a chest drainage system) should be discussed with the patient because some Jehovah’s Witness patients will allow the use of salvaged blood, provided that the entire system remains in continuity with the vasculature.<sup>416</sup> The use of albumin and other substances derived from circulating proteins should be addressed if possible. Early hemodynamic monitoring is indicated to help determine the role of colloid therapy, pressors, and inotropes in maintaining tissue O2 delivery at the highest possible level. Case reports have described the successful use of rFVIIa and hemoglobinbased O2 carriers in Jehovah’s Witness patients to rapidly correct bleeding and support O2 delivery. Still, these agents are not approved for these uses, and large case series are unavailable.<sup>240,417</sup> In the post- acute phase, using erythropoietin to promote red cell growth may shorten the period of relative anemia, although there is limited evidence.<sup>418</sup>

### ACUTE PAIN MANAGEMENT

Trauma patients present significant pain management challenges to the clinician because of multiple sites of injury, protracted care episodes, complicated psychological and emotional issues, and prior or ongoing substance abuse (see also Chapter 77). As with pain management practice in other diseases, trauma patients are frequently undertreated, thus creating a significant source of dissatisfaction. Because trauma patients run the gamut of physiology, from healthy young athletes to debilitated elderly adults, anesthesiologists providing pain management for trauma patients must be prepared for a wide range of needs.

Individual trauma patients will have widely varying requirements for pain medication, so induction of analgesia must be titrated carefully, ideally in a closely monitored environment such as the PACU. Administration of rapidly acting IV agents in small doses at frequent intervals

until pain relief is achieved is recommended. This allows the practitioner to determine the patient’s basal requirements before starting long- acting medications or patientcontrolled analgesia. Hypotension in response to analgesics is most commonly indicative of hypovolemia and should prompt a search for occult hemorrhage while further resuscitation occurs.

The need for analgesic medication and the duration of the requirement for analgesics will be minimized if a comprehensive emotional support system is available to the patient. Because of its unexpected nature, trauma carries with it a strong negative psychological overlay that can profoundly affect how anatomically based pain is perceived by the brain.<sup>419</sup> and how the patient reacts. After an injury, the patient may have legal, financial, and family- based concerns without the ability to immediately address them. The availability of religious, financial, or legal counselors who can help the patient and family with these issues is of enormous benefit. The anesthesiologist can help by clearly communicating to the patient the injuries, the probable time required for recovery, and the plan for managing pain throughout the course. The anesthesiologist should refer the patient to counseling services as needed and should be alert to the potential for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in any traumatized patient.<sup>420</sup> Referral to an experienced psychiatrist or psychologist is appropriate if PTSD hinders the patient’s recovery.

The need for analgesic medication is also influenced by the schedule of physical therapy prescribed for the patient. The more active a patient can be after a traumatic injury, the lower the risk for pulmonary complications, venous thrombosis, and decubitus ulcers. Though painful in the short term, the sooner the patient is mobilized, the lower the analgesic requirements in the long term. Early mobilization demonstrates to the patient the path to recovery and contributes to an improved emotional state. One of the goals of analgesia, therefore, is the provision of adequate medication to facilitate physical therapy without sedating the patient so that participation is impossible.

Neuropathic pain arises when a direct injury occurs to a major sensory nerve and is common after spinal cord trauma, traumatic amputations, and major crush injuries. Neuropathic pain is characterized by burning, intermittent electrical shocks, and dysesthesia in the affected dermatomal distribution. Neuropathic pain must be identified because it responds poorly to the analgesics used for somatic pain. This diagnosis should be considered whenever pain control is poor, or the patient has a rising requirement for medications unexplained by anatomic injuries. First- line therapy for neuropathic pain has been revolutionized by the widespread use of gabapentin, an antiepileptic drug with very strong specificity for this problem.<sup>421</sup> Gabapentin therapy is typically initiated at a dose of 200 mg three times daily, with daily titration upward to a maximum of 2 to 3 g/ day. If neuropathic pain persists, selective regional anesthesia or analgesia may be indicated to break the cycle of spinal cord receptor recruitment.<sup>422</sup>

Regional analgesia (see also Chapters 41 and 42) provided through an epidural or brachial catheter should be considered for any trauma patient with the potential to benefit from this approach because it will spare the use of systemic opioids and facilitate early mobilization.<sup>423</sup> Epidural

Regional techniques are less practical when the patient has multiple sites of injury or when fractures or open wounds make placement difficult. Although epidural placement in anesthetized patients is relatively contraindicated because of the potential for occult SCI, the risk- to- benefit ratio in many trauma patients favors placement during surgery when general anesthesia facilitates appropriate positioning and patient cooperation.

## Summary

Trauma spans all ages and classes of patients, from young and vigorous to older and frail. Because of its significant prevalence, practicing anesthesiologists will encounter trauma patients throughout their careers. Increased public recognition of the consequences of injury has sparked interest in trauma research and education, with the result that diagnostic and therapeutic techniques have evolved rapidly in recent years. As a perioperative physician, the anesthesiologist is in the ideal position to understand and apply these new techniques.
