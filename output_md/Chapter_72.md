## Physiological Considerations

Developmental changes in anatomy and physiology especially occurring at birth and during the first year of life have a major impact on the perioperative management of infants and children.

### FETAL DEVELOPMENT

The prenatal period is characterized by increased vulnerability to external factors which can impact development of several organ systems (Table 72.1) and is typically divided into three phases; germinal, embryonic, and fetal stage. The germinal stage starts with conception and ends approximately two weeks later with the implantation of the embryo into the uterine wall. One key feature of this period is the formation of the placenta. The embryonic

stage comprises the period between the third and eighth weeks of pregnancy and is characterized by rapid cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation leading to the establishment of all major organs. Maternal exposure to potential teratogens during this period can lead to developmental defects. The fetal stage lasts from the 9th week of pregnancy to birth and is characterized by the growth and functional differentiation of organs. Exogenous factors, such as environmental toxins, ionizing radiation, infections, and drugs can interfere with the physiological patterns of organ development throughout the fetal period which, in turn, will result in organ dysfunction of variable severity. Careful evaluation of prenatal history is therefore an important part of preoperative assessment and can guide further investigations.

### NEONATAL AND INFANT PHYSIOLOGY

The transition from intra- to extrauterine life is rapid and involves a complex and well- orchestrated series of events aimed to ensure neonatal viability.<sup>4</sup> A clinically useful measure to assess the condition of the newborn infant immediately after birth is the Apgar score at 1 minute and 5 minutes after birth (Table 72.2). Apgar scores between 7 and 10 are reassuring, a score of 4 to 6 is considered abnormal, while scores 3 and below are indicative of poor outcome. Despite the historic value of the Apgar scores, it has its limitations and cannot be used independently to diagnose perinatal asphyxia.<sup>5</sup>

### Cardiovascular System

The cardiovascular system undergoes anatomical and physiological changes during the first year of life.<sup>6</sup> _In utero_ , most of the cardiac output flows from the placenta across the foramen ovale into the ascending aorta (oxygenated blood), whereas superior vena cava blood (deoxygenated) is directed to both the pulmonary artery and the ductus arteriosus (see also Chapter 73). At birth, lung expansion and oxygenation results in rapid decreases in pulmonary vascular resistance and increases in peripheral vascular resistance. As portal blood pressure falls, the ductus venosus and ductus arteriosus close. Pulmonary vascular resistance continues to decline as the architecture of the pulmonary vessels changes. Increasing pressure on the left side of the heart (caused by the increase in peripheral vascular resistance) leads to functional closure of the foramen ovale. Closure of the ductus arteriosus is primarily triggered by an increase in arterial oxygen concentration. Preterm infants frequently have patent ductus arteriosus. True mechanical closure by fibrosis does not occur until 2 to 3 weeks of age. The immature myocardium has poorly developed myofibrils, sarcoplasmic reticuli, and T- tubules resulting in less efficient contractility and less compliant ventricles. Therefore, cardiac output is relatively more dependent on the heart rate. Bradycardia is poorly tolerated while tachycardia supports cardiac output without any significant decrease in stroke volume. The most frequently encountered arrhythmia in pediatric populations is bradycardia, induced by hypoxia that can progress to asystole. Ventricular fibrillation is extremely rare in infants and children. Myocardial calcium stores are reduced because of the immaturity of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, leading to a greater dependence on exogenous (blood- ionized) calcium and probably increased susceptibility to myocardial depression by volatile anesthetics that have calcium channel–blocking activity.

### Pulmonary System

The lung bud rises from the foregut during the first trimester and the gas-exchanging portions of the airway are formed during the second trimester. Alveolar ductal development starts at 24 weeks while the septation of the air sacs begins around the 36th gestational week.<sup>8</sup> Alveoli then increase in number and size until the child is approximately 8 years old. Further growth is exhibited as an increase in size of the alveoli and airways. At term, complete development of surface- active proteins helps maintain patency of the airways. If a child is born prematurely and these proteins are insufficient, then respiratory failure (respiratory distress syndrome) may follow.

Respiration is less efficient in infants. The small diameter of the airways increases resistance to airflow. The neonatal and infant tracheobronchial tree and chest wall are highly compliant, which can lead to airway obstruction and atelectasis. Thus, functional airway closure may accompany each breath. Dead space ventilation is proportionally like adults. However, oxygen consumption is two to three times higher. In preterm infants, the work of breathing is approximately three times that in adults and can be significantly increased by cold stress (due to increased metabolic demand for oxygen) or any degree of airway obstruction. ### Renal System

Renal function is diminished in neonates and even more in preterm infants because of relatively low perfusion pressures and immature glomerular and tubular function (Table 72.3; Fig. 72.4).<sup>16,17</sup> Nearly complete maturation of glomerular filtration and tubular function occurs by approximately 20 weeks after birth, although delayed in preterm infants. Complete maturation of renal function occurs at approximately 2 years of age. Thus, the ability to excrete free water and solute loads may be impaired in neonates, and the half- life of medications excreted by means of glomerular filtration will be prolonged (e.g., antibiotics that therefore require longer intervals between doses in neonates).

### Hepatic System

Functional maturity of the liver is incomplete in full- term neonates.<sup>18,19</sup> Most enzyme systems for drug metabolism are developed but not fully activated at birth. As the infant grows, the ability to metabolize medications rapidly increases as hepatic blood flow increases and enzyme systems are functionally induced. The cytochrome P450 system and phase I drug metabolism of lipophilic compounds is 50% of adult values at birth. CYP3A (cytochrome P450, family 3, subfamily A) is generally present at adult values at birth, whereas other cytochromes are absent or reduced. Phase II reactions, which conjugate drugs able to facilitate renal excretion, are often impaired in neonates and result in jaundice (decreased bilirubin breakdown) and long drug (and their active metabolites) half- lives. Some of these reactions do not achieve adult activity until after 1 year of age. A preterm infant’s liver has minimal glycogen stores and is unable to manage large protein loads. This accounts for the neonate’s tendency toward hypoglycemia and acidemia and for the failure to gain weight when the diet contains too much protein. Additionally, plasma levels of albumin and other proteins necessary for the binding of drugs are lower in full- term newborns (and are even lower in preterm infants) (Fig. 72.5). In addition, the binding of some drugs to albumin may be altered in the presence of hyperbilirubinemia in the neonatal period.

### Gastrointestinal System

Gastric pH is alkalotic at birth but is physiologically neutral by the second day of life. The ability to coordinate swallowing with respiration does not fully mature until infants are 4 to 5 months of age, thus resulting in a high incidence of gastroesophageal reflux, particularly in preterm newborns. Congenital anomalies of the gastrointestinal system will present with signs and symptoms within 24 to 36 hours of life. Upper intestinal abnormalities are exhibited as vomiting and regurgitation, whereas lower intestinal abnormalities produce abdominal distention and a failure to pass meconium.

### Central Nervous System

The neural tube is formed between the third and fourth week of pregnancy and this event is followed by an active phase of cell proliferation and migration during the second trimester. Extensive brain development occurs between the beginning of the third trimester of pregnancy and the first few years of postnatal life. During this brain growth spurt period, the nervous system undergoes important differentiation, including the formation of myriads of synaptic contacts between neurons. Neural activity plays a preponderant role in these events especially during critical periods of development when the nervous system is particularly sensitive to and relies on external stimuli to drive differentiation of neuronal networks. Pharmacological interference with physiological activity patterns during this period may lead to impaired brain development.

Premature and term newborns exhibit physiologic and reflex responses to noxious stimulation that are diffuse and nonspecific when compared to older children and adults. The first functional and reflex responses to tactile and noxious stimuli are aimed at guarding against tissue damage and can trigger a range of physiological responses. The time of onset of pain awareness or “feelings” in humans remains undefined and largely debated. Nevertheless, there is evidence that early painful experiences, even if nonconscious, might alter subsequent central nervous system (CNS) function and that adequate pain relief can improve outcome.<sup>24,25</sup>

### Thermoregulation

Infants are especially vulnerable to hypothermia because of the large ratio of body surface area to weight, the thinness of the skin, and a limited ability to counter cold stress.<sup>26</sup> Cold stress causes increased oxygen consumption and metabolic acidosis, particularly in preterm infants because of thin skin and limited fat stores. The infant compensates by shivering and nonshivering (cellular) thermogenesis (metabolism of brown fat). However, limited shivering during the first 3 months of life makes cellular thermogenesis the principal method of heat production. Placing the baby on a warming

mattress and warming the surgical unit (26°C or warmer) will reduce heat lost by conduction. Keeping the infant in an incubator and covered with blankets minimizes heat lost through convection. Heat lost through evaporation is lessened by humidification of inspired gases, use of plastic wraps, and warm skin disinfectant solutions. Although forced hot air blankets are the most effective means of warming, overheating can occur especially in neonates. Anesthetics alter many thermoregulatory mechanisms, particularly non- shivering thermogenesis in neonates.

## Pharmacology

Drug dosing in children differs from adults because of both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic factors.<sup>27–30</sup> Pharmacokinetics in infants and children varies with body composition and renal and hepatic function. Renal and hepatic function evolves as relative blood flow and organ maturity change with age. The changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics are most pronounced in neonates. Evidence- based therapeutics is limited in pediatrics and many anesthesia drugs are used “off label” in small children.<sup>31</sup>

### Protein Binding

Neonates have reduced total plasma protein levels, including lower levels of albumin (which binds acidic drugs) and alpha- 1 acid glycoprotein (which binds neutral and basic drugs). Protein concentrations reach adult levels by the first year of life. As a result of reduced protein levels in the neonate, highly bound drugs will have a higher free fraction and greater drug effect. However, it is important to note that this is only clinically relevant for drugs that have a very high degree of protein binding, a high extraction ratio, and a narrow therapeutic index. Some drugs, such as caffeine and ceftriaxone may also displace bilirubin from plasma proteins, increasing the risk of kernicterus in sick neonates.<sup>33</sup>

### Clearance

Clearance changes with age in a complex manner.<sup>34</sup> Clearance, expressed as L/h/kg, is greater in a toddler than in an older child. Increased clearance is a result of the nonlinear relationship between many aspects of organ function and size. This nonlinear relationship is not related to organ maturity and is surprisingly constant across different aspects of organ function, age, and species. Many drugs are independent of clearance of the liver or kidney due to nonspecific esterases that are mature at birth.<sup>35</sup> The clearances for these drugs do not require an adjustment for maturation and can be predicted largely through allometry alone. In neonates and infants, drugs that require hepatic or renal clearance will have a lower clearance, resulting in longer elimination half times. These drugs require infrequent dosing and lower infusion rates to achieve a steady state. In older children, elimination half times may appear to be shorter. However, this difference tends to disappear with allometric scaling. Additional perturbations in drug pharmacokinetic effects occur with factors such as sepsis, congestive heart failure, and increased intra- abdominal pressure.

### PHARMACODYNAMICS

In older children, pharmacodynamic properties of most anesthetic agents are probably similar to adults, albeit with some notable exceptions such as anticoagulants.<sup>36,37</sup> Less is known about the pharmacodynamics of anesthetic agents in neonates and infants. Functional anesthetic end points such as pain, amnesia, and unconsciousness can be difficult to assess in infants. Surrogate measures of anesthesia effect, such as the raw and processed electroencephalogram (EEG), are also unreliable in infants.<sup>38</sup> Our increasing understanding of the developmental neurobiology or pain and consciousness suggest there are likely to be clinically significant pharmacodynamic differences in infants and children.<sup>39</sup>

## Anesthetic Drugs

### INHALED ANESTHETICS

### Uptake and Distribution of Inhaled Anesthetics

The rate of rise of inhalational anesthetic concentration depends on rate of delivery, which is determined by the inspired concentration, minute ventilation, and ratio of minute ventilation to residual functional capacity and blood/gas and tissue/blood partition coefficients<sup>40</sup> (see Chapter 18).

Attainment of steady state, where the inspired, alveolar, and tissue (brain) fractions equilibrate, is faster in children than adults. This is traditionally due to a higher ratio of minute ventilation to functional residual capacity, which hastens equilibration of inspired and alveolar fractions. However, tissue/blood solubility also plays a role. The effect of age is greater with more soluble agents such as isoflurane and less for sevoflurane and desflurane.

The rapid attainment of steady state in neonates increases the risk of overdose, especially if an overpressure maneuver (using a high inspired concentration) is used. This risk may be greater for agents for which greater MAC multiples can be delivered by the vaporizer. For instance, a halothane vaporizer can deliver up to 5.75 MAC multiples versus 2.42 MAC multiples for a sevoflurane vaporizer (Table 72.4).

### Potency of Inhaled Anesthetics

The expired minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) of an inhaled anesthetic required in children changes with age<sup>41–48</sup> (Fig. 72.8). MAC is lowest in the preterm neonate, increases by 30% from birth, and peaks between 1 to 6 months of age. Infants have a higher MAC than that of older children or adults; the reasons for these age- related differences in MAC are not known. Compared to adults, older children have a similar relationship between MAC and other measures of anesthetic effect. The ratio of MAC to MACawake, MACintubation, MACLMA insertion, MACextubation, and MACBAR are similar in children to adults.<sup>49–52</sup> At the same time, the relationship between MAC and the electroencephalogram (EEG), and hence most anesthesia depth monitors, is not as consistent in children as in adults. Children have a higher bispectral index (BIS) for a specific fraction of MAC.<sup>53,54</sup> In infants and neonates there are no data to determine how MAC relates to other aspects of anesthetic

### Sevoflurane

Sevoflurane has a low blood solubility, facilitating a relatively rapid inhalational induction. Sevoflurane is less pungent than isoflurane and desflurane and hence has become the agent of choice for inhalational induction in children. Unlike other inhalational agents the MAC is similar between neonates and infants, but like other agents becomes lower with age after infancy: 3.3% for neonates, 3.2% for infants 1 to 6 months old, and 2.5% for children older than 6 months.<sup>41,42</sup>

Sevoflurane is associated with a greater incidence of emergence delirium compared to halothane and is reported to cause epileptiform changes in the EEG when delivered at high concentrations in children. The etiology of these findings is unknown.<sup>58</sup>

### Isoflurane

Isoflurane has a blood solubility between halothane and sevoflurane. Like sevoflurane it has a relatively lower MAC in neonates, peaks in infancy, and then declines with age. It is more potent than sevoflurane but has a relatively more noxious smell, which makes an inhalational induction with it unacceptable for most children.

### Desflurane

Desflurane has a blood solubility lower than isoflurane or sevoflurane and MAC is lower in neonates, peaks in infancy, and declines with age.<sup>44,59</sup> However, it is not suitable for inhalational induction in children due to a pungent odor and is not recommended for maintenance in children who are not intubated due to an unacceptable incidence of laryngospasm.<sup>60</sup>

### Nitrous Oxide

Nitrous oxide is an odorless gas that has a low solubility in blood but is relatively non- potent. The MAC of nitrous oxide has not been accurately determined in children. If used together with other inhalational anesthetics, it reduces the MAC of more potent agents. It may speed the uptake of more potent agents but the underlying theory behind this “second gas” effect has been challenged and it is almost certainly of minimal clinical relevance. Nitrous oxide is a weak analgesic and is often used in children alone, or in conjunction with other agents for procedural sedation and analgesia.<sup>61–63</sup> Since it is odorless, it is also commonly used as an adjuvant for induction of general anesthesia. Breathing high concentrations for a short period can provide sedation before sevoflurane is added. When compared to adults, there is less evidence that nitrous oxide increases postoperative nausea and vomiting in children.<sup>64,65</sup>

### INTRAVENOUS GENERAL ANESTHETICS

### Hypnotic and Sedative Drugs

**Propofol.** In children the volume of distribution of propofol is greater than in adults due to rapid redistribution. Clearance is similar in children and adults, but longer in preterm neonates. The dose for induction increases with decreasing age. The median effective dose (ED50) for loss of eyelash reflex is 3 mg/kg in infants aged 1 to 6 months, and 1.3 to 1.6 mg/kg in children aged 1 to 12 years. Use of propofol has been associated with profound hypotension in neonates.<sup>66</sup> A potential drawback to propofol is pain on injection. The most effective is the administration through a large vein or adding lidocaine (0.5–1.0 mg/kg) to the propofol, or injecting lidocaine before the propofol. Propofol is not contraindicated in children with egg allergy.<sup>67</sup>

Prolonged administration of propofol for children can lead to propofol infusion syndrome, which is characterized by lipemia, metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and hyperkalemia followed by refractory cardiovascular collapse.<sup>68</sup> It has been reported after high- dose infusions for an extended period and its onset may be subtle and unnoticed followed by rapid demise. The mechanism underlying propofol infusion syndrome remains unclear. It may be related to alterations in mitochondrial lipid metabolism<sup>69</sup> (see Chapter 21).

**Ketamine.** Ketamine has a similar volume of distribution in children compared to adults, but the clearance is reduced in infants. It may be used for induction of anesthesia (1–3 mg/ kg intravenously or 5–10 mg/kg intramuscularly). The intramuscular route is well described for uncooperative patients where other premedication or intravenous and inhalational induction are not tolerated. The administration of a benzodiazepine when using an intramuscular dose of ketamine may reduce the risk of hallucinations and an anticholinergic agent may be required to reduce the risk of hypersecretion. If used for a relatively brief procedure, a large intramuscular dose of ketamine can lead to significantly delayed emergence.

Ketamine causes relatively little cardiovascular or respiratory depression, is less likely to result in airway obstruction, and is utilized as a safer agent in resourcepoor settings. It is also frequently used in the pediatric

emergency department for brief, painful procedures. Given its cardiovascular stability it may be the optimal premedication for children with congenital heart disease. Ketamine is increasingly used as an adjunct to other agents to optimize postoperative analgesia.

**Etomidate.** Clearance of etomidate is similar in children and adults. However, the volume of distribution is larger in children and hence a larger initial bolus is required. Concerns regarding anaphylactoid reactions and suppression of adrenal function have limited widespread use of this anesthetic in children. As with propofol, the incidence of pain on intravenous administration is frequent. Etomidate has minimal cardiovascular suppression and thus it is useful in children who are critically ill and those with a head injury.

### Alpha- 2 Receptor Agonists

Alpha- 2 receptor agonists are being used more commonly in pediatric anesthesia. Uses of these agents include sedation, premedication, analgesia, adjunct for general anesthesia, prevention of emergence delirium, and prolongation of regional nerve blockade. Alpha- 2 receptor agonists cause a dose- dependent reduction in heart rate and blood pressure. However, this hemodynamic effect is rarely clinically relevant in the doses commonly described.

**Clonidine.** Clonidine is increasingly used for premedication. There is some evidence that clonidine is superior to midazolam as a premedicant in terms of sedation, postoperative agitation, and postoperative pain.<sup>70</sup> To be optimally effective, 4 mcg/kg clonidine should be given orally 45–60 minutes prior to induction. Like many other drugs, the clearance of clonidine is reduced in neonates, however, it rises to 82% of adult levels by 1 year of age.<sup>71</sup>

**Dexmedetomidine.** Dexmedetomidine has greater selectivity for the α2 receptor than clonidine, and hence produces less hypotension and bradycardia compared to clonidine. It also has minimal respiratory depressive effects. The sedation provided by dexmedetomidine is similar to natural sleep. Children may be more easily aroused from dexmedetomidine sedation compared to other sedative agents. Dexmedetomidine has been widely used as a sedative in the intensive care setting. It has also been used as a sole sedative for medical imaging procedures in children. The loading dose of 1–2 mcg/kg is typically given over 10 minutes followed by an infusion of 0.5 to 1 mcg/kg/h.<sup>72</sup> Clearance is reduced in neonates.<sup>73</sup>

Dexmedetomidine is increasingly used intranasally for premedication, where it has a bioavailability of 80%. A dose of 1 to 2 mcg/kg is used for intranasal premedication, allowing for 30–40 minutes for its peak effect. Several studies have found intranasal dexmedetomidine to be superior to midazolam for premedication in children.<sup>74,75</sup>

### Benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepines are primarily administered as an anxiolytic and amnestic in pediatric patients. Artificially flavored oral preparations of midazolam are commonly administered to premedicate an anxious toddler prior to induction of general anesthesia. It can also be injected intramuscularly with ketamine to partially induce anesthesia in uncooperative

### Opioids

**Morphine.** Morphine is frequently used for postoperative analgesia. There is also a large variability in pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic profile and thus doses should be titrated to effect. Morphine is metabolized by both glucuronidation and sulfation. In contrast to adults, sulfation is the dominant pathway of metabolism in infants. Clearance is low in neonates but reaches adult levels at 6 to 12 months of age. Neonates are more susceptible to respiratory depression, perhaps due to an immature blood–brain barrier and immature metabolism of the parent drug. There is some evidence that human neonates are also more susceptible to the respiratory depressive effects of morphine, however, the mechanism remains unclear. Nevertheless, it should be used cautiously in infants, particularly in preterm infants.

**Codeine.** Codeine is a morphine- like opioid with approximately 10% of the potency of morphine. It has rapid oral absorption and 90% bioavailability. These features resulted in it previously being used extensively as an oral analgesic (1 mg/kg). Approximately 10% of codeine is metabolized to morphine and thus a considerable amount of its analgesic action is through this mechanism of metabolism. There is considerable variation in this metabolism with poor, rapid, and ultra- rapid metabolizers. Ultra- rapid metabolizers are at risk of increased clinical response including profound respiratory depression that has been associated with death in children.<sup>76</sup> For this reason, codeine is now rarely used for children. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recently issued a “black box” warning against its use in children after tonsillectomy.<sup>77</sup>

**Meperidine.** The use of meperidine is declining in all clinical settings due to concerns over the accumulation of the metabolite normeperidine, which may cause seizures, particularly after multiple doses are administered. Meperidine has a potency of approximately one-tenth that of morphine and a shorter time to peak effect. The elimination of meperidine is reduced in neonates.

**Fentanyl.** Fentanyl is commonly used in pediatric anesthesia for intraoperative analgesia. It has greater hemodynamic stability than morphine. High doses of 10–100 mcg/ kg can be used to maintain cardiovascular stability. The clearance is markedly reduced in preterm infants but rises to 80% of adult values by term. Adult levels of clearance are achieved within the first few weeks post term. The volume of distribution is greatest in neonates (5.9–L/kg) and steadily declines with age to 1.6 L/kg in adults.

**Alfentanil.** The clearance of alfentanil in preterm neonates is markedly reduced and the volume of distribution is greater than older infants. Pharmacokinetic data are generally sparse and somewhat contradictory, but volume of distribution and elimination half- life are similar between infants aged 3–12 months and older children.<sup>78</sup>

**Sufentanil.** Sufentanil is used mainly for pediatric cardiac, complex neurosurgical, and craniofacial surgery. Its

shorter and predictable context- sensitive half- life, make it an ideal opioid for long surgical procedures that require rapid emergence from anesthesia. Like other similar agents, in neonates sufentanil has a greater volume of distribution, reduced clearance, and longer elimination half- life.

**Remifentanil.** The main advantage of remifentanil is its extremely brief half- life. The elimination half- life is 3–6 minutes and is independent of dose and duration. Remifentanil is degraded by nonspecific plasma and tissue esterases, and metabolism is not affected by butyrylcholinesterase deficiency. Since renal and hepatic function is minimal, it has great utility in infants with hepatic or renal failure. The differences in remifentanil’s half- life among neonates, infants, and adults are minimal. There are age- related differences in the volume of distribution and clearance but not in half- life, with volume of distribution being greater in infants compared to older children (Fig. 72.9).<sup>79</sup> Contrary to the pharmacokinetics of most drugs, neonates clear the drug more rapidly than older children. Of further interest is the very small patient- to- patient variability in pharmacokinetic parameters when compared with similar studies examining other opioids, particularly in infants and neonates. The particularly favorable pharmacokinetics in neonates allows the provision of a deep opioid- induced plane of anesthesia while avoiding cardiovascular depression and the need for postoperative ventilation.

A loading dose of remifentanil may be required prior to infusion; however, rapid large bolus may produce hypotension and bradycardia. A bolus of remifentanil 3 mcg/ kg with propofol 3–4 mg/kg has been described as an alternative to succinylcholine to facilitate endotracheal intubation.<sup>80,81</sup> Acute tolerance to remifentanil has been described in children. If postoperative pain is anticipated, adequate long- acting analgesia should be given well before the remifentanil infusion is discontinued.

**Tramadol.** Tramadol is a relatively weak opioid with less respiratory depressant effects. Two enantiomers provide analgesia; one is an opioid mu receptor agonist and the other inhibits uptake of serotonin and norepinephrine. Clearance is less in premature neonates but in children is similar to adults if standard allometric scaling is applied. Tramadol is metabolized by various pathways including via CYP2D6 to O- desmethyltramadol. This metabolite has mu- receptor affinity approximately 200 times greater than tramadol.<sup>30</sup> The genetic polymorphism of CYP2D6 produces fast and slow metabolizers which may produce a variable response amongst children. The FDA has warned against using tramadol after tonsillectomy in children with obstructive sleep apnea.<sup>82</sup> One oral formulation of tramadol for children has a concentration of 100 mg/mL and is administered in drops. This may increase the risk of errors in administration if milliliters are mistakenly given rather than drops, resulting in a potentially 10- fold overdose. This formulation has been replaced in some countries by an elixir of 10 mg/mL.

Although the initial dose per kilogram needed for neuromuscular blockade is often similar for children of all ages, the greater volume of distribution and reduced renal or hepatic function of neonates result in a slower rate of excretion and hence a prolongation of effect.

The choice of nondepolarizing muscle relaxant depends on the side effects and the duration of the desired muscle relaxation. Vecuronium, atracurium, rocuronium, and cisatracurium are useful for shorter procedures in infants and children; they may also be administered as a constant infusion. The method of excretion of atracurium and cisatracurium (Hofmann elimination and ester hydrolysis) makes these relaxants particularly useful in newborns and children with immature or abnormal hepatic or renal function. Vecuronium is valuable because no histamine is released; however, its duration of action is prolonged in newborns.

Rocuronium has a clinical profile similar to that of vecuronium, cisatracurium, and atracurium. Acceptable conditions for intubation are produced by rocuronium within 3 to 4 minutes after 1 mg/kg intramuscularly in infants and 1.8 mg/kg intramuscularly in children older than 1 year of age; these effects were more dependable with deltoid than with quadriceps muscle injection.<sup>85</sup> Table 72.5 provides commonly recommended guidelines for doses. ### TOTAL INTRAVENOUS ANESTHESIA IN CHILDREN

Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) is increasingly being utilized in pediatric anesthesia.<sup>89–91</sup> Propofol and remifentanil are the main agents in these TIVA protocols. One limitation of TIVA in children is the need for specific and well- validated pediatric algorithms. Adult target- controlled infusion models are not best suited for children.<sup>92</sup> However, pediatric- specific algorithms have been applied to pediatric patients but are primarily investigational.<sup>93</sup>

### ANESTHESIA AND THE DEVELOPING BRAIN

In 2016, the FDA issued a warning that general anesthetics and sedatives may have harmful effects on the child’s brain.<sup>94,95</sup> The warning emphasizes that the risk is greater with prolonged and repeated anesthetics in children under the age of 3 years. This controversial warning is based on animal data and a limited number of human studies.

### Animal Data

There is substantial preclinical evidence that many general anesthetics have the capacity to cause morphological and functional changes to the developing brain.<sup>96–98</sup> This has been demonstrated in a wide variety of species, ranging from the nematode to the nonhuman primate.<sup>99,100</sup> Histological manifestations of this phenomenon include neuronal and glial apoptosis and altered dendritic morphology and arborization.<sup>101–103</sup> Although the effects are age, dose, and time dependent, it is difficult to identify the upper limit

of duration of exposure which has no effect.<sup>97</sup> In general, the effects are greater in the more immature brain, which translate to the late trimester of pregnancy or early infancy in humans, but some effects are also seen in older animals. Effects are greatest with γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) agonists and _N_ -methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) antagonists and hence have been seen with propofol, benzodiazepines, volatile anesthetics, and ketamine. There is contradictory evidence for an effect with alpha2- agonists. Deficits in learning and altered behavior have also been reported in laboratory animals, including nonhuman primates, exposed to anesthesia. However, not all experiments have demonstrated consistent functional deficits and it is unclear if they are a function of the morphological changes.

**Translating the Animal Data to Humans.** There are considerable problems translating the animal data to humans.<sup>104,105</sup> Comparable developmental lifespan stage between species is not an exact science. Homeostasis may be deranged during anesthesia and concurrent surgery. Human brains are complex and develop and compensate over a longer period. Human brains can be extraordinarily vulnerable to injuries but have considerable plasticity and recovery. Importantly, genetic and environmental factors have a huge impact on resilience and recovery or vulnerability.

Human studies on anesthesia and the developing brain can be broadly grouped according to their design and subsequent outcomes.<sup>106</sup>

The design of observational studies broadly consists of:

- _Data linkage population- based studies._ These use existing data sets which can be linked. They are inherently retrospective and limited by the outcomes and exposure variables that have already been collected. They can,

however, be very large, examining whole of country or whole of state populations. The outcomes commonly used are preschool tests for school readiness, or school grades.

- _Use of existing birth cohorts or longitudinal studies._ These studies use existing data from longitudinal cohorts that have usually been set up for other investigations. They often include access to more detailed outcome from psychometric outcomes and diagnoses of disability and school grades. The details of anesthetic exposure may be limited but identify other factors that may contribute to outcome.

- _Purpose- built cohort studies._ These studies recruit children that have been exposed to anesthesia and match them to those that have not, and test for a range of psychometric outcomes. These studies enable researchers to focus on neurodevelopmental areas of greatest interest but logistically it is difficult to recruit large numbers of children. Children may be recruited in various ways including from existing longitudinal studies.

Various outcome measures have also been used<sup>107</sup> :

- _School grades or school readiness tests._ These are relevant to families, but they are course measures of neurodevelopment. A deficit may exist in one aspect of neurodevelopment which is not reflected in school grades. Conversely, many other factors influence school grades, diluting any possible “injurious” effect. Their advantage is not only their importance to families, but also that they are easy to obtain in very large numbers.

- _Diagnosis of a learning disorder or specific neurodevelopmental disorder._ These are also of great importance to the families and the community. A major problem is that the diagnosis may not always be clear. Definitions of disorders vary between jurisdictions and over time. Another potential disadvantage is that these disorders are still not common enough to be able to produce precise results except in very large studies.

- _Psychometric testing._ Many tests are available to test a wide range of neurodevelopmental domains. Apical tests, such as intelligence quotient (IQ), are composite scores which pool results from several domains. Apical tests have the best value as far as predicting future function but using apical tests may miss deficits in some subdomains. Looking at multiple domains raises the problem of type 1 error (finding a “significant” association due to multiple testing). The psychology literature abounds with studies that are not replicable – partly because of this issue. If a “deficit” is found in only one or two of many subdomains tested, then the results must be interpreted very cautiously until they are replicated in following studies. Performing psychometric testing is labor intensive and must be done to a high standard to be useful.

- _Imaging._ Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies are usually small due to logistic problems. Like psychometric testing, there are usually many outcomes measured, increasing the risk of type 1 error. While our understanding of MRI is rapidly increasing, there is still a degree of disconnect between what is seen on MRI and functional measures.

In all these studies it is important to consider the age of testing. Some aspects of neurodevelopment such as higher

executive function cannot be tested until the child is older. Furthermore, children will usually “grow into their deficit”. An injury in a particular aspect of brain function will become more apparent as the child develops and lacks that function. This is contrary to the idea that the brain will always have plasticity and recovery.

**Confounding Variables.** Confounding occurs when an observed association between A and B is not direct or causal but due to another factor, C. There are significant issues with confounding in all the observational studies looking at anesthesia and developmental outcome. Children have anesthesia because they are having surgery or a diagnostic procedure. The surgery or procedure may itself cause injury by inducing the stress response from surgery or poorly managed pain. Furthermore, the condition that warrants surgery may be associated with increased risk of poor neurodevelopmental outcome. This may be obvious in the case of genetic abnormalities or major illnesses, but it can also be more subtle. Children having ear tube insertion may be showing signs of delay and the pediatrician wants to optimize their hearing. A child requiring dental extractions may come from an environment where health care is suboptimal or have subtle behavioral problems that make dental care without out anesthesia or sedation more problematic.

It is important to note that confounding by indication could explain why the outcomes appear worse with multiple as opposed to single exposures.

Confounding can be reduced by careful sample selection, matching, or statistical adjustments in the analysis, but these measures are never mathematically perfect and thus cannot completely remove the influence of confounding. Importantly they can only reduce the influence of known confounding factors. The problem of confounding means that, by itself, any association seen in an observational study cannot ever be regarded as anything better than weak evidence for causation. Randomized controlled trials are by far the best way to reduce confounding.

**Results from Population-Based Clinical Studies.** Most, but not all, large population- based studies looking at school grades or readiness for school found evidence for a small difference in school grade or readiness in children that have had anesthesia in early childhood.<sup>108–113</sup> The increase in risk is much smaller than the risk associated with gender or month of birth. Interestingly the studies do not indicate that exposure at 0–2 years is worse than 2–4 years, and some find the opposite. As far as frequency of exposure, some show weak evidence for a greater risk with multiple exposures, but most have insufficient power to determine if having multiple exposures poses any greater risk than single exposure. Several found that surgery increased the risk of not getting to school or not being able to sit the test. This may be consistent with an increased risk of developing a specific developmental disorder.

**Results from Studies that Look at Specific Developmental Disorders.** Most studies that have diagnosis of a learning or developmental disorder as their outcome found evidence for an association between anesthesia in early childhood and an increased risk of a diagnosis of a

<!-- PDF page 14 -->

behavioral disorder or learning disability.<sup>114–120</sup> Most of these studies do find good evidence that the association is greater with multiple exposures.

**Results from Studies that Use Psychometric Testing for Outcomes.** Several reports demonstrate an association between anesthesia in early childhood and specific domains of psychometric testing. These domains include language, reading, abstract reasoning, executive function, some aspects of memory, processing speed, fine motor abilities, and some aspects of behavior.<sup>121–128</sup> Some but not all find an association with a small reduction in IQ.<sup>110</sup>

Some robust studies such as PANDA found no evidence for an association with any deficit.<sup>129</sup> The only randomized controlled trial, the GAS study, found no evidence for a difference in IQ and a range of other psychometric tests in children tested at age 2 or age 5 after being randomized to either general or awake regional anesthesia for hernia repair.<sup>112</sup>

**Summary and Recommendations.** Despite strong animal evidence, there is inconsistent human evidence for an association between anesthesia in early childhood and a range of later neurodevelopmental outcomes. A causal relationship cannot be ruled out, although evidence for a causal relationship in humans is weak. These associations could be simply explained by confounding variables. Clinical decisions need to be made based on the current preclinical and clinical data. Most pediatric anesthesia societies currently recommend that surgery not be delayed or modify the anesthetic technique. Some recommend delaying nonurgent procedures; however, very few purely elective procedures are done in children and delaying procedures is nearly always associated with an increased risk inherent in not treating the condition that warranted the procedure. Finally, if surgery is delayed, there are no data on a “safe” period for anesthesia. While not all agree that neurotoxicity should be included as part of informed consent, anesthesiologists should, however, be prepared to discuss the risk with parents and caretakers if they are asked about the risk or express concerns about proceeding with anesthesia and surgery.

**Ongoing Uncertainties.** There is some evidence that repeated exposure to anesthesia is potentially harmful, but it is unclear if this represents a cumulative effect or is a result of confounding.<sup>130</sup> Two recent studies have identified the range of duration of anesthesia in children in the United States and the majority of children have anesthesia which is an hour or less in duration.<sup>106,131</sup>

Apart from the issue of anesthesia neurotoxicity, there is growing evidence that neonates having major surgery have a substantially higher risk of neurologic injury and poor neurologic outcome.<sup>132,133</sup> This observation may be completely unrelated to exposure to anesthesia and be partly due to other factors in the perioperative period such as comorbidities, changes in cerebral perfusion, with or without hypotension, inflammation, hypoxia, hypercarbia, stress, and pain.<sup>134</sup> Neonates have vulnerable brains and much more work needs to be done to identify optimal perioperative management strategies to protect the brain.

## Anesthetic Management

### PERIOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

### Preoperative Evaluation

Preoperative evaluation is an essential component of perioperative management. Patients with significant congenital anomalies and other medical conditions should be evaluated well in advance of elective surgery to allow sufficient time for testing, planning, and any optimization of medical conditions to decrease perioperative risk. The designation of the pediatric American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) status provides the child and family with detailed and individualized information on perioperative management and decreases both perioperative communication and anxiety. Conducting this interview several days before anesthesia is a legal obligation in several countries to fulfill requirements for informed consent.

Corroborating the past medical history with the child’s pediatrician is very helpful when hospital chart reviews are not available. Physical examination should include a thorough assessment of the airway, cardiovascular, respiratory, and nervous systems along with the hydration state of the child. The anesthesiologist should order specific preoperative investigations related to suspected conditions (e.g., the presence of congenital heart disease), which may modify perioperative management and outcome or underlying clinical conditions (e.g., asthma) in which introduction of a specific treatment could decrease perioperative risks.<sup>135,136</sup> Although a routine preoperative electrocardiogram (ECG) is not required for healthy children, there is an ongoing controversy about the value of routine neonatal screening for long QT syndrome (LQTS), which is the leading suspected cause of sudden infant death and mortality.<sup>137–139</sup> Since both perioperative stress and a number of anesthetics can lengthen QT interval, performing ECG in neonates and infants under 6 months of age may be an option.<sup>140</sup> The routine use of preoperative chest radiographs should be abandoned, especially in view of the harmful effects of ionizing radiation.<sup>136,140</sup> In contrast, a pregnancy test may be recommended in any patient capable of childbearing after proper consent.

### Upper Respiratory Tract Infection

Upper respiratory tract infections (URIs) are common in childhood, with an annual incidence as frequent as 6–8 episodes in infants and preschool children.<sup>141</sup> The infection usually lasts for 7–10 days, but symptoms may persist for up to 3 weeks. More than 200 viruses have been shown to be associated with URI.<sup>142</sup> The viral invasion of the respiratory mucosa leads to an inflammatory response that results in airway edema and increased secretions.<sup>143</sup> Bronchial hyperreactivity, resulting primarily from the impact of the viral infection on the autonomic nervous system, may persist for up to 6 weeks or longer, well beyond the disappearance of clinical symptoms.<sup>141</sup> Therefore a child with a URI is a major challenge for pediatric anesthesia.<sup>141,144</sup> The most common perioperative respiratory adverse events (PRAE) associated with a recent URI include laryngospasm, bronchospasm, breath-holding, atelectasis, arterial oxygen desaturation, bacterial pneumonia, and unplanned hospital admission.<sup>145</sup> Fortunately, the incidence of serious of PRAE in children with “common cold” is low.<sup>141,146</sup>

<!-- PDF page 15 -->

Children presenting with signs of a significant URI (fever, productive cough, purulent rhinorrhea, or otitis media) are at increased risk for PRAE.<sup>147</sup> Infants with a respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection present a particularly high- risk group during perioperative assessment and management.<sup>148</sup> Primary pulmonary disease, such as bronchial asthma, prematurity, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, cystic fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, and smoke exposure, will increase respiratory morbidity. Instrumentation of the airway (bronchoscopy and endotracheal intubation) are relevant surgical and anesthetic risk factors.<sup>141,147</sup> Otorhinolaryngologic, ophthalmologic, upper abdominal, and thoracic surgeries also increase the risk of PRAE.

The decision to postpone an anesthetic for a child with URI is evolving and should be considered in the context of the patient’s medical history, frequency of URIs, timing of the surgery or procedure, and logistical factors (Fig. 72.10). Given the prevalence of URI in children, recent expert consensus statements note that it is not necessary to postpone surgery for 6 weeks after a URI in children and suggest an approximately 2- week- long time lag between the resolution of clinical symptoms and anesthesia.<sup>141,144</sup> Premedication with inhaled salbutamol has been shown

to be effective in both the prevention and treatment of perioperative bronchospasm in children with bronchial hyperreactivity.<sup>149</sup> While intravenous lidocaine (1 mg/kg) has also been proposed to decrease the incidence of PRAE, current evidence does not support this approach.<sup>141</sup> Induction of anesthesia through an intravenous approach with propofol results in a lower incidence of PRAE in children with URI when compared to inhalational induction.<sup>147</sup> Endotracheal intubation in patients with bronchial hyperreactivity has been shown to be associated with a higher incidence of PRAE.<sup>147</sup> Finally, several lines of investigation point to the anesthesiologist’s experience as an important factor to prevent PRAE.<sup>141</sup> Since 2019, the COVID- 19 pandemic has significantly impacted perioperative risk assessment and anesthetic management due to the infectious nature of this virus and its sequalae.<sup>150</sup> Given the transmission of the virus through aerosolization, modifications to resuscitation, airway management, tracheal intubation, and aerosol- generating procedures have been proposed.<sup>151–153</sup> This includes clarifying the appropriateness and timing of any intervention, identifying oxygenation and airway management techniques to minimal aerosolization risk, and ensuring availability of equipment

### Managing Perioperative Anxiety in Children

Children experience significant anxiety and stress before anesthesia.<sup>156</sup> The pre- anesthetic visit provides an opportunity to identify the contributory factors and to provide interventions aimed to decrease anxiety levels.<sup>157,158</sup> Planning of these interventions must consider the agespecific developmental differences in how children react to the stress of anesthesia and surgery. Infants up to 9 months of age are less prone to separation anxiety and will most probably accept parental surrogates (including soothing voices, gentle rocking, and being held).<sup>159</sup> Separation anxiety is the greatest problem in children between 1 and 3 years of age. Some, but not all, of these children may respond to distraction techniques such as toys and stories. Although parental presence at anesthesia induction has been a standard of care in many pediatric centers, recent studies do not support routine parental presence as the optimal means of reducing anxiety.<sup>160</sup> Children between 3 and 6 years of age have concerns about body mutilation and may require reassurance. Preoperative play therapy is especially useful in this age group. Children between 7 and 12 years of age usually require more explanation and wish to actively participate in their perioperative course. The use of virtual reality programs as distraction techniques has been shown to reduce the Modified Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale scores in children.<sup>161</sup> Videos, leaflets, and interactive computer applications are very helpful in this population. Assessing anxiety in adolescents is particularly difficult. Despite their outwardly calm appearance, teenagers can experience high anxiety, which may steadily escalate on their way from the preoperative holding area to the operating room. Risk factors to predict higher anxiety in this group include increased baseline anxiety, depression, somatization, and a fearful temperament.<sup>162</sup>

Prehospitalization programs, including tours of the hospital and the operating room, videos, leaflets, and other interactive books, should be implemented several days before surgery to achieve the desired effects.<sup>159,163</sup> On the day of intervention, several distraction techniques have been shown to be equivalent or even superior to pharmacological premedication in several studies.<sup>164,165</sup> Hypnosis, music, and non- aggressive lighting can also be used to provide a calm and soothing environment for the child upon arrival to the operating room.

Sedative and hypnotic drugs are useful for managing preoperative anxiety in children.<sup>166</sup> In the absence of an intravenous line, the most used routes are the oral, nasal, and rectal routes in decreasing order of acceptability by most children. Midazolam is the most utilized benzodiazepine for premedication, with an appealing profile of safety and efficacy. It is usually administered orally at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg (up to a maximum of 15 mg) after which sedation and anxiolysis are achieved within approximately 20 minutes. It can also be administered by the intravenous route (0.05–0.1 mg/kg), the intranasal route (0.3 mg/kg), and the rectal route (0.5 mg/kg).<sup>157</sup> Potential limitations

include prolonged effect and paradoxical reactions. Alpha2 adrenergic agonists are increasingly used for premedication for their sedative, analgesic, and opioid- sparing properties. Clonidine can be administered both orally (4 mcg/kg) or intranasally (4 mcg/kg) and, although it has a relatively long onset time (45 minutes), is very advantageous. Dexmedetomidine has a shorter onset and duration of action when compared with clonidine due to its low bioavailability when given orally (approx. 15%) but may be more effective, particularly when given intranasally.<sup>162</sup> Ketamine is highly lipid soluble and is rapidly absorbed after either oral, intranasal, intramuscular, or intravenous administration. Onset of sedation occurs after 15–20 minutes following oral intake (5–8 mg/kg).<sup>158</sup> Intramuscular administration of ketamine (4–5 mg/kg) is especially useful in uncooperative and combative children when the procedure cannot be delayed or rescheduled. Premedication with ketamine, however, can be associated with sialorrhea, hyperventilation, hallucinations, and emergence delirium.<sup>166</sup> Fentanyl is rapidly absorbed by the transmucosal route and can be used for premedication as a pleasant- tasting lollipop. The bioavailability by this route is 33% but is reduced if the lollipop is chewed or swallowed.<sup>166</sup>

### Preoperative Fasting

Preoperative fasting is aimed to minimize the risk of pulmonary aspiration of gastric contents during anesthesia. Traditional guidelines recommend the “6- 4- 2 rule,” which is a minimum of 6- hour fast for solid foods, 4- hour fast for breast milk, and a 2- hour fast for clear fluids. These guidelines do not make any distinction between adults and children and are primarily based on expert opinion and are not backed by solid clinical evidence.<sup>167</sup> Adherence to these guidelines in children may pose several problems. Fasting times often end up being much longer, and it is not uncommon to see young children fasting for clear liquids up to 12 hours or more prior to anesthesia induction.<sup>168–170</sup> In addition to the obvious discomfort related to hunger and thirst, these prolonged fasting times may result in hypoglycemia, metabolic acidosis, dehydration, and cardiovascular instability. The incidence of pulmonary aspiration in otherwise healthy children is very low (1–2/10<sup>4</sup> ), and increasing evidence indicates that liberal clear fluid intake does not result in increased residual gastric volume or increase the incidence of pulmonary aspiration. The new European consensus statement on fasting in children recommends a onehour- long fasting after clear fluids.<sup>171</sup> The ASA updated fasting guidelines to acknowledge the general trend toward one-hour fasting for clear liquids but stopped short of making a specific recommendation. However, they advocate the need to encourage drinking clear liquids as close to 2 hours before surgery as possible.<sup>172</sup>

### INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

### Induction of General Anesthesia

Induction of general anesthesia should be guided by the medical condition of the child, the surgical procedure, the level of anxiety of the child, the child’s ability to cooperate and communicate, and the risk of aspiration pneumonitis. A recent evidence- based review of the existing

<!-- PDF page 17 -->

literature suggests that neither the child’s nor the parent’s anxiety is alleviated by parental presence upon anesthesia induction.<sup>173</sup> Nevertheless, parental presence may be important with children presenting with behavioral problems or developmental delay as well as in children scheduled for multiple procedures. Some parents believe that they have an obligation to participate during induction of anesthesia and require counseling on the process to reduce anxiety.

Both the parents and the operating room staff should be involved in the perioperative plan and management, particularly for combative or overly stressed children. Reviewing previous anesthetics with the parents can be extremely useful to identify what works best for the child. In the absence of an existing intravenous line, intramuscular administration of ketamine (4–5 mg/kg) or inhalational induction using high concentrations of sevoflurane can be a helpful option to induce anesthesia. These latter approaches may necessitate physical restraint that raises ethical, legal, and practical issues. Relative contraindications to the use of restraints include lack of consent/concurrence by parents or staff, failure to exhaust all other techniques, and when the restraint- induced stress could significantly worsen the child’s state.<sup>174</sup> The option of postponing elective surgery in these children should always be considered, though may not eliminate the challenges of managing these situations in the future.

The two most common anesthesia induction techniques in children are inhalational and intravenous. A retrospective study and a randomized trial suggested a benefit of intravenous induction of anesthesia in reducing respiratory complications.<sup>147,173</sup> It is, nevertheless, important to note that these studies were focused only on adverse respiratory events in a group of at- risk children. Therefore, when deciding on the induction technique, care should be taken to weigh all the relevant factors including whether intravenous access is possible.<sup>175</sup> The use of a RSI technique to prevent pulmonary aspiration has modifications due to the anatomical and physiological differences in pediatric patients.<sup>176,177</sup> While the classic RSI relies on adequate preoxygenation, this often cannot be achieved in uncooperative children. Given the relatively lower functional residual capacity, preoxygenation in infants and children creates minimal oxygen reserve. Brief periods of apnea can lead to profound hypoxemia and related bradycardia. Cricoid pressure can easily compress the trachea in infants and toddlers, rendering difficulty in visualizing glottic structures and insertion of the endotracheal tube. Recent studies question the value and need for cricoid pressure.<sup>178</sup> Altogether, these factors may lead to a higher incidence of forced mask ventilation and unsuccessful intubation attempts. Therefore, to balance the risk of pulmonary aspiration with the much more prevalent risk of hypoxemia, controlled forms of the RSI technique have become increasingly popular amongst pediatric anesthesiologists. The direct laryngoscopy under light anesthesia and incomplete muscle paralysis can induce regurgitation and vomiting with pulmonary aspiration.<sup>179–181</sup> While the possibility of mask induction should always be evaluated, intravenous access must be considered mandatory in “at- risk” children. Intraosseous access is a suitable

alternative in this population where intravenous access cannot be established.<sup>182</sup>

### Airway Management and Ventilation

**Airway Management.** A wide range of syndromic and genetic conditions and congenital malformations are associated with potential airway problems, especially those involving facial dysmorphias.<sup>183,184</sup> History of congenital anomalies, obstructive sleep apnea, head and neck trauma, previous surgery, and previous airway management must also be considered. During the physical examination, the anesthesiologist should check for facial dysmorphias, signs of stridor, dysphonia, swallowing disorders, difficulty in breathing, difficulty in speaking, and hoarseness.<sup>184</sup> There are many difficult airway predictors in children, but their applicability, sensitivity, and specificity vary in the clinical setting. Among them, mandibular protrusion, Mallampati classification, movement of atlanto- occipital joint, reduced mandibular space, and increased tongue thickness have all been shown to be good predictors of airway problems.<sup>185,186</sup> Other reported risk factors are age less than 1 year, ASA II– IV status, obesity, and maxillofacial and cardiac surgery.<sup>187</sup>

Mask ventilation is an essential component of pediatric airway management. Anesthetized children are particularly prone to upper airway collapse, and this can be easily relieved by a combination of moderate head tilt, chin lift, jaw thrust, and the application of continuous positive airway pressure.<sup>188,189</sup> Additionally, both oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airway devices can be used during spontaneous or positive- pressure mask ventilation to relieve airway obstruction caused by the posterior displacement of the tongue in anesthetized children.<sup>190,191</sup>

Variations of the supraglottic airway devices are now commonly used in pediatric anesthesia. The two most popular among these are the classic laryngeal mask airway (LMA) and the modified LMA with a gastric port (Pro- seal, Teleflex, Westmeath, Ireland).<sup>189</sup> Both devices have comparable safety and efficacy profiles in pediatric populations.<sup>192</sup> Increasing evidence suggests the use of the LMA in children is associated with a decreased incidence of perioperative respiratory complications when compared to endotracheal intubation.<sup>193</sup>

Direct laryngoscopy is still the most common technique for intubation in children. Given the cephalad location of the larynx, straight blades may be useful to optimize exposure of the vocal cords in neonates and infants. Children can be managed with either curved or straight blades. Other devices have been developed over the past decade to facilitate endotracheal intubation.<sup>194,195</sup> Video laryngoscopy enables better and faster glottic visualization and reduces the time of intubation, the number of attempts, and risk of dental trauma. Utilization of video laryngoscopy in infants has been shown to increase first- attempt success rates and reduce complications. It is important to note that each type of video laryngoscope device varies considerably.<sup>194</sup> Video laryngoscopes may be technically superior and more intuitive than fiberoptic scopes.<sup>195</sup> Furthermore, video laryngoscopy with standard blades rather than curved blades was associated with greater success rates.<sup>196</sup>

While awake fiberoptic intubation is generally considered the gold standard for the known or anticipated difficult airway in adults, this option is usually not feasible in

<!-- PDF page 18 -->

children due to the lack of cooperation. Most pediatric anesthesiologists respond best to an inhaled induction of general anesthesia and flexible fiberoptic- aided intubation under spontaneous ventilation.<sup>197</sup> A comparison of sedation versus general anesthesia for tracheal intubation in pediatric patients with predicted difficult airways revealed similar first- attempt success rates. However, 27.6% of the sedated patients were converted to general anesthesia prior to intubation of the trachea.<sup>198</sup>

The use of cuffed endotracheal tubes is increasingly common in neonates, infants, and young children.<sup>189</sup> Historically, uncuffed endotracheal tubes were recommended in children younger than 8 years of age, because it was thought that the narrowest part of the airway was the cricoid ring and to minimize potential cuff- induced damage of the tracheal mucosa and allow reduction of air flow resistance by inserting a larger size tube.<sup>199</sup> Airway trauma can also occur, when using an uncuffed tube with acceptable leak pressures. Moreover, a higher incidence of laryngospasm with the use of uncuffed tubes has also been reported, and there is no data documenting increased subglottic airway trauma when cuffed versus uncuffed tubes are used.<sup>184</sup> The use of a cuffed tube may provide a more accurate sampling of end- tidal CO2 and avoid pollution of the operating room. Finally, when using an uncuffed endotracheal tube, there may be the need for repeated manipulation of the

airway to ensure the correct tube size without too large a leak, resulting in more manipulation of the airway. Repeat laryngoscopy can be avoided by using a smaller cuffed tube. However, when using a smaller tube, there can be increased risk of kinking of the tube or accumulation of secretions with the smaller lumen.

International guidelines for the management of the unanticipated difficult airway in pediatric practice are based on a Delphi panel expert discussion focusing on airway management in children between 1 and 8 years of age.<sup>200</sup> Three scenarios are identified: difficult mask ventilation, difficult tracheal intubation, and cannot intubate and cannot ventilate in anesthetized children with neuromuscular blockade. ### Equipment and Monitoring

There have been considerable advancements in the equipment and monitoring available for children over the past

decade.<sup>194,211,212</sup> Pediatric patients require an anesthesiologist with pediatric anesthesia experience and ageappropriate equipment. A resuscitation cart for pediatric patients of all ages, including pediatric defibrillation paddles and resuscitation drugs, is mandatory. The drugs should be available in appropriate concentrations and with clear pediatric dose schedules. Pediatric- specific cognitive aids for the diagnosis/treatment of the most common emergencies and critical conditions should be immediately available both in the operating room and in the postanesthesia care unit. Age- specific airway equipment and airway cart for the management of the difficult pediatric airway should also be available. Ultrasound- guided peripheral and central venous access in children has consequently become a standard of care.<sup>213,214</sup> When confronting challenging venous access options in an awake child, tablets and smartphones can be used as distraction techniques to reduce anxiety.<sup>215</sup>

Assessment of cardiac function has also become increasingly important in the care of infants and children undergoing complex surgical procedures. Transesophageal Doppler probes and Fick and dye dilution techniques can be used for children as small as 3 kg.<sup>216</sup> Changes in thoracic electrical bioimpedance because of changes in blood volume and flow, and blood cell orientation can be used for continuous noninvasive monitoring of cardiac output and other cardiac parameters. The ICON monitor (Cardiotronic/Osypka Medical Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA), a portable noninvasive cardiac output monitor that is based on the principle of electrical velocimetry, has been validated against the Fick equation, thermodilution, and echocardiography in pediatric populations ranging from neonates to adolescents.<sup>216</sup> Pulse oximetry–based noninvasive hemoglobin determination is also available and in clinical use; while a valuable new technology, it still requires validation in pediatric populations.<sup>212</sup>

Near- infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) monitoring can be used to monitor oxygenation and, thus indirectly, the blood flow and supply–demand relationship in the brain, liver, kidney, intestines, and muscles. Evidence that NIRS improves outcomes is lacking, as it is for many of the monitoring devices, but there is certainly potential for its use in goal- directed interventions to reduce organ hypoxemia and ischemia.<sup>212,217,218</sup>

### Safety Issues

Perioperative mortality in neonates and infants is severalfold higher when compared to older children.<sup>221</sup> It is important to realize that anesthesia- related death rarely occurs in infants and children without significant medical comorbidities. Nevertheless, critical events that could potentially lead to mortality or significant and lasting morbidity can be a direct consequence of anesthesia management. There are major approaches that can identify adverse outcomes in pediatric perioperative care, such as institutional audits, closed claims analysis, and large- scale registries of anesthesia- related cardiac arrest. The critical events are most often cardiac and/or respiratory. According to the Pediatric Perioperative Cardiac Arrest (POCA) Registry, the most common causes of perioperative cardiac arrest in children are cardiac in origin, including, in decreasing order of frequency, hypovolemia, myocardial ischemia, hyperkalemia, and sudden arrhythmias.<sup>222</sup> Respiratory causes include laryngospasm, inadequate oxygenation, and difficult intubation. Based on closed claims analyses, critical events leading to serious respiratory morbidity have decreased since the introduction of minimal monitoring standards.<sup>223</sup> These findings have been corroborated by large European pediatric anesthesia centers.<sup>224,225</sup>

There is circumstantial evidence suggesting an association between the level of expertise in pediatric anesthesia and perioperative morbidity. The incidence of respiratory complications has been repeatedly reported to be dependent on the anesthesiologist’s experience.<sup>147,224,226</sup> There is also a relationship between perioperative complications and the number of pediatric anesthetics provided by the anesthesiologist.<sup>227,228</sup> Specific training for pediatric anesthesia and maintaining a suitable volume of experience of pediatric anesthesia has therefore been recommended for those providing anesthesia care to children.<sup>229,230</sup>

### Fluids and Blood Component Management

Perioperative fluid management raises important challenges in pediatric populations. Hypovolemia is the most common cause of perioperative cardiac arrest in children.<sup>231</sup> Age- specific changes in metabolism, extracellular fluid volume, and fluid turnover do not allow the direct extrapolation of adult guidelines to infants and children.

The three major goals of perioperative fluid management are to meet maintenance requirements, replace preoperative deficits, and compensate for ongoing losses occurring during the perioperative period. The most commonly used basic fluid maintenance formula in pediatric anesthesia

practice is the “4- 2- 1” rule, which is a weight- based formula stating that the hourly fluid requirements of children are 4 mL/kg for the first 10 kg of body weight followed by 2 mL/kg between 10 and 20 kg and 1 mL for each additional kg of body weight above 20 kg.<sup>232,233</sup> However, there is an ongoing debate on how to compensate for preoperative fluid deficits.<sup>234</sup> One of the simplest options to decrease preoperative fluid deficits is to decrease preoperative fasting times for clear liquids.<sup>168</sup> Replacing the preoperative fasting deficit using the 4- 2- 1 rule, or a bolus of 25 mL/kg for children younger than 3 years of age and 25 mL/kg over 3 years of age, was the traditional practice.<sup>235</sup> The German guidelines state that “the background infusion may be initiated with an initial infusion rate of 10 mL/kg/h and be adjusted to the actual requirement during the further course.”<sup>236</sup> Moreover, in case of apparent clinical signs of dehydration, these guidelines recommend 10 mL/kg fluid bolus for every percent of estimated dehydration. The volume of fluids needed for ongoing losses during the perioperative period largely depends on the type of procedure as well as on the pathological state of the child. It can easily be as high as 10–15 mL/kg/h and, in some specific cases such as burns or extensive neonatal abdominal surgery, can exceed 50 mL/kg/h.<sup>234</sup>

The amount of insensible water loss is inversely proportional to gestational age. Premature neonates have increased skin permeability and ratio of body surface area to weight. The fact that the neonatal kidney is unable to excrete large amounts of excess water or electrolytes must also be considered. The extracellular fluid volume in a newborn is relatively large. Therefore term newborns have reduced fluid requirements for the first week of life. The daily fluid requirements for a term newborn in the days after birth are estimated to be 70 mL/kg on day 1, 80 mL/kg on day 3, 90 mL/kg on day 5, and 120 mL/kg on day 7. Daily fluid requirements would be slightly higher for preterm infants. The choice of type of fluid is at least as important as the amount administered. Based on the electrolyte composition of either human or cow milk, the original recommendation from Holliday and Segar defined the daily electrolyte requirements as 2 mEq/100 kcal/day for both potassium and chloride and 3 mEq/100 kcal/day for sodium.<sup>234</sup> This electrolyte administration regimen, along with the 4- 2- 1 rule, resulted in the frequent use of excessive quantities of hypotonic fluid solution which, in turn, led to hyponatremia, seizures, cerebral edema, and death. Children are particularly susceptible to cerebral edema with acute hyponatremia. Hospital- acquired hyponatremia may occur rapidly in children and may present initially as vomiting and drowsiness.<sup>237</sup> A recent meta- analysis that included large, randomized trials convincingly demonstrates that isotonic intravenous maintenance fluids with sodium concentrations similar to that of plasma reduce the risk of hyponatremia when compared with hypotonic intravenous fluids.<sup>238</sup> Therefore isotonic crystalloid solutions should be administered to maintain homeostasis during the perioperative period.<sup>234,236</sup> It is important to note that both hyponatremia and hypernatremia can still develop in children receiving isotonic saline solutions, and routine serial checking of serum sodium concentrations in children undergoing prolonged or extensive interventions is advised.<sup>237</sup>

<!-- PDF page 23 -->

The use of dextrose- containing intravenous fluids administered to pediatric surgical patients has evolved.<sup>234</sup> It is well established that the incidence of preoperative hypoglycemia is less than 2.5% and is usually associated with fasting periods exceeding current recommended fasting guidelines.<sup>239</sup> Conversely, the use of 5% dextrose- containing fluid during surgery in pediatric patients has invariably produced hyperglycemia.<sup>239,240</sup> Therefore a growing consensus is that routine administration of dextrose is not necessary in otherwise healthy children receiving anesthesia.<sup>234</sup> Patients receiving hyperalimentation and having endocrine/metabolic diseases (diabetes mellitus) are at highest risk of hypoglycemia. In these individuals, adjusting the rate of glucose infusion guided by glucose monitoring is recommended: 1%–2.5% dextrose- containing isotonic solutions administered in neonates and infants appear to be the best option, while serial blood glucose measurements are needed to maintain blood glucose levels in the optimal range.<sup>240</sup>

In the absence of well- controlled studies with clearly defined end points, there is currently no evidence supporting the role of crystalloids versus colloids in perioperative fluid loss replacement and volume expansion in neonatal and pediatric populations.<sup>234,241</sup> Historically, albumin has been considered the gold standard for maintenance of colloid osmotic pressure in neonates and infants and is still the most frequently used plasma expander in these populations. Nevertheless, data supporting the continued use of albumin for general fluid resuscitation in children are lacking. Nonprotein synthetic colloids such as hydroxyethyl starch (HES), gelatin, and dextran have also been used as plasma expanders in children. While a prospective observational drug safety trial using a third-generation HES revealed no serious adverse drug reactions, such as anaphylaxis, renal failure, or clotting disorder, in children with normal renal and clotting function, further studies are needed before presuming safety in patients with renal failure or those at increased risk of bleeding.<sup>234,242</sup> Both the efficacy, in terms of intravascular volume expansion, and the safety profile of HES have been reported comparable to albumin in the setting of pediatric cardiac surgery.<sup>243</sup> Gelatins are polypeptides produced by degradation of bovine collagen. Although the initial formulations of this product induced a high incidence of hypersensitivity reactions, a more recent trial was unable to show evidence of any short- or long- term adverse effects.<sup>244</sup> Dextrans are watersoluble glucose polymers. Although these molecules have excellent colloid oncotic powers, they probably should not be used because of their negative coagulation effects and high anaphylactic potential.

Transfusion guidelines for non- neonatal pediatric patients are similar to those for adults.<sup>245</sup> Some precautions are necessary, especially in case of massive transfusion.<sup>246</sup> In calculating need for blood replacement, the calculation should consider the maximal allowable blood loss (MABL) that will be tolerated to maintain an acceptable hematocrit. MABL considers the effect of patient age, weight, and starting hematocrit on blood volume. In general, blood volume is approximately 100 to 120 mL/kg for a preterm infant, 90 mL/kg for a full- term infant, 70 to 80 mL/kg for a child 3 to 12 months old, and 70 mL/kg for a child older than 1 year of age. These volumes are merely estimates of blood volume. The individual child’s blood volume is calculated by simple proportion by multiplying the child’s weight by

the estimated blood volume (EBV) per kilogram. Although several formulas are available, a simple relationship is easiest to remember.

The indications and contraindication for the transfusion of fresh frozen plasma (FFP) are essentially the same for children as for adults (also see Chapters 45 and 46). Children with known clotting factor deficits, such as those with massive thermal injury or coagulopathy, may require transfusion of FFP. In contrast, healthy children who do not have coagulation factor deficits at the beginning of surgery do not need FFP until blood loss exceeds 1 blood volume.<sup>247,248</sup> Despite the loss of 1 blood volume, prolongation of the prothrombin time (PT) and the partial thromboplastin time (PTT) will only be minor. This generalization applies to children given PRBCs; children given whole blood will not need FFP, even when blood loss exceeds several blood volumes.

If the clotting factor deficit is associated with abnormal oozing, then a PT exceeding 15 seconds (international normalized ratio [INR] >1.4) or a PTT greater than 60 seconds (>1.5 times baseline) warrants close observation. Usually, an INR &lt;2 does not warrant correction. If these abnormalities exist but oozing does not occur and the surgical field is relatively safe from the complications of hematoma formation, as in orthopedic surgery versus neurosurgery, then it seems appropriate to observe the child and withhold the transfusion of FFP.

### Regional Anesthesia and Analgesia (see Chapter 74)

Most regional anesthesia techniques used in adults can be safely administered to children if strict attention is paid to the dose of the local anesthetic and use of proper equipment. Perhaps the greatest advance in regional pediatric anesthesia has been the development of methods for postoperative analgesia (also see Chapters 41, 42, and 76). Recent advances in ultrasound equipment and techniques have further improved the accuracy of nerve blocks and reduced the dose of drug needed to provide a successful block.

Regional nerve blocks and direct local infiltration of surgical wounds with long- acting local anesthetics are simple yet very effective and safe methods of providing pain relief for all children.<sup>256,257</sup> This practice has been especially helpful in the outpatient population; parents are encouraged to start analgesics when they observe their child becoming irritable but before the complete dissipation of the block.

## Anesthetic Management for Specific Pediatric Conditions and Procedures

### NEONATAL ANESTHESIA

The neonate has a higher incidence of complications than older children.<sup>231,265–270</sup> These complications primarily relate to oxygenation, ventilation, airway management, and response to anesthetic medications. Congenital lesions such as congenital heart disease and lesions of the pulmonary and central nervous system can complicate the perioperative course. A room air oxygen saturation should prompt an echocardiogram if low saturation values are detected. Neonates generally have limited cardiovascular and respiratory reserve, resulting in a narrow margin for error and the need for meticulous attention to detail in all

aspects of anesthesia care. Neonates are more likely to have a sudden deterioration provoked by persistent fetal circulation, undiagnosed congenital malformations, or genetic conditions during anesthesia and surgery.

Optimal neonatal anesthesia care requires thorough preoperative evaluation, optimization, and input from the neonatologists and surgeons, which will guide appropriate monitoring, ventilatory and hemodynamic support, and personnel both in the operating room and in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) or neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Access to the neonate may be difficult once the sterile drapes are placed and surgery commences. Thus, the airway, intravenous access, and all monitoring should be checked and secured before surgery commences.

The anesthesiologist must devote particular attention to the calculation of drug dose and concentration. Air must also be removed from all intravenous devices and syringes before use to prevent air embolization, which can be catastrophic in a neonate. Intravenous fluids should be administered with volume- limiting devices; infusion pumps are particularly helpful in preventing overadministration of intravenous fluids.

Every effort must be made to maintain the neonate’s temperature to minimize thermal stress. The surgical environment should be warm, and exposure of the neonate kept to a minimum. Forced air warming is particularly useful for maintaining temperature.

Monitoring end- tidal carbon dioxide is essential to prevent hypo- or hypercapnia and significant changes in cerebral perfusion. However, it is not always accurate in prematurity and necessitates the confirmation by concurrent transcutaneous CO2 monitoring.

The optimal oxygen saturation for neonates is controversial. High oxygen saturations in premature neonates increase the risk of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), however, some randomized trials have found that aiming for low (85%–89%) saturations in extremely preterm neonates may increase the risk of other neurologic morbidity.<sup>271</sup> During stable operative conditions it is reasonable to aim for an oxygen saturation between 93% and 95% in preterm neonates. Given the relative inaccuracies of these monitors, the anesthesiologist must be extremely vigilant and prepared to respond rapidly to changes in oxygen saturation. Hypoxia or acidosis in the newborn can lead to significant pulmonary vasoconstriction, hypoxia, worsening pulmonary hypertension, acidosis, hypoxia, and eventual cardiovascular collapse.

The neonatal lung is fragile and particularly prone to injury from excessive tidal volumes. PEEP should be used. Even brief disconnection of the airway circuit or mechanical ventilator can lead to significant alveolar collapse. Conventional anesthesia machines equipped with ventilators optimized for volume- targeted modes of ventilation may not be suitable for premature and low- birthweight neonates. Therefore, neonatal ventilators with monitoring equipment that can accurately measure tidal volumes and pressures may provide optimal ventilation for these vulnerable populations. It is also increasingly recognized that intubated neonates should be transported to and from the operating room with neonatal ventilator equipment rather than simply a bag and T- piece. Transferring a neonate that is on an oscillator is particularly challenging. For an unstable neonate it may be safer to perform the surgery in the NICU.

The optimal blood pressure during neonatal anesthesia is unknown. Recent studies have suggested that the neonatal brain may be particularly susceptible to hypotension.<sup>272</sup> Pain management in newborns is particularly challenging. Neonates, even extremely preterm neonates, can feel pain and will respond to painful stimuli; indeed, preterm neonates may be even more sensitive to painful stimuli.<sup>273</sup> Adequate anesthesia and analgesia in the neonate is unclear.<sup>274,275</sup>

Propofol and inhaled anesthetics can result in profound cardiovascular depression in the neonate. In contrast, the synthetic opioids (e.g., fentanyl, sufentanil, alfentanil, remifentanil) are usually well tolerated, even by infants who are critically ill. These potent opioids must still be carefully titrated to a defined response, and anesthesiologists must remain cautious of opioid- induced bradycardia and its consequences on cardiac output. Low concentrations of potent inhaled anesthetics can be used with opioids to provide a means of controlling hemodynamic responses without significantly depressing the myocardium. The relative merits of one anesthetic technique over another are not well defined. Regional anesthesia can be highly effective in neonates. Caudal and spinal anesthesia are relatively straightforward, however, the safe placement of a lumbar or thoracic epidural block requires considerable skill. Epidural local anesthetic infusions can result in systemic toxicity due to immature metabolism.

### Meningomyelocele

Meningomyelocele, the protrusion of a part of the meninges and spinal cord through a defect in the vertebral column, is becoming less common in developed countries due to improvements in maternal folic acid intake and antenatal screening. Specific management issues include, special positioning for tracheal intubation (i.e., the defect is placed on a “doughnut” and towels are placed under the head), underestimating insensible fluid and blood loss, association with hydrocephalus, cranial nerve (vocal cord) palsy, resulting in inspiratory stridor, and potential for brainstem herniation. Latex allergy precautions should be used with these children for their first and subsequent anesthetics.

### Tracheoesophageal Fistula

A tracheoesophageal fistula can have five or more configurations, most of which are diagnosed after an inability to swallow because of an associated esophageal atresia (esophageal pouch). In these cases, the characteristic diagnostic test is an inability to pass a suction catheter into the stomach. Neonates may develop aspiration pneumonitis from a distal fistula connecting the stomach to the trachea through the esophagus or from a proximal connection of the esophagus with the trachea. Neonates with the rarer H- type fistulae have a fistula between esophagus and trachea, however, the esophagus is patent with no atresia. These children present later, typically with respiratory distress and chest infections.

The tracheoesophageal fistula may be part of constellation of congenital anomalies known as the VATER association ( **V** , vertebral; **A** , anal; **TE** , tracheoesophageal; **R** , renal) or the VACTERL association (VATER and **C** , cardiac; and **L** , limb). Any child with a tracheoesophageal fistula or esophageal atresia should be suspected of having the other anomalies. An echocardiogram to examine for a right- sided aortic arch and the presence of congenital heart disease should be performed before anesthesia.<sup>276</sup> Abdominal ultrasound should also be performed to detect major renal abnormalities.

The major anesthetic issues include compromised respiratory function due to aspiration pneumonia, overdistention of the stomach from entry of air directly into the

stomach through the fistula from positive- pressure ventilation by mask, inability to ventilate the child’s lungs because of the large size of the fistula, and coexisting congenital heart disease.<sup>277</sup> Prior to anesthesia, the neonate’s feedings are withheld, a catheter is placed in the esophagus, and the neonate is placed prone in a head- up position.

A major aim of anesthesia is to minimize positive- pressure ventilation until an endotracheal tube is placed distal to the fistula and/or the fistula is occluded or ligated. Positivepressure ventilation may inflate the stomach through the fistula, leading to distention of the stomach and reduction of functional residual capacity of the lungs. This risk is greater if the fistula is large, or the lung compliance is poor. Several different strategies for anesthesia have been described.<sup>278</sup> An inhalational induction is generally preferred, and spontaneous ventilation is maintained until the fistula is ligated. This is not always possible. Bronchoscopy is usually performed after induction to assess the size and location of the fistula. At bronchoscopy, a balloon embolectomy catheter or similar device may be placed directly in the fistula to occlude it. The endotracheal tube is ideally placed in the trachea distal to the origin of the fistula. This may be done blindly by advancing the tube into a main bronchus and then carefully pulling it back until equal air entry is heard. The endotracheal tube may be inadvertently placed into the fistula, resulting in rapid gastric distention and arterial oxygen desaturation. If this occurs the tube should be withdrawn. Urgent transcutaneous gastric decompression or intra- abdominal clamping of the distal esophagus through an abdominal incision may be needed.

Most repairs are now done thoracoscopically. Invasive blood pressure monitoring is recommended as intraoperative arterial desaturation or hypotension may occur with manipulation of mediastinal structures. A preductal and postductal pulse oximeter can facilitate diagnosis of an intracardiac shunting. Extubation of tracheal and postoperative respiratory management should be coordinated with the surgeon. Postoperative pain may be managed with wound infiltration local anesthetic, caudal catheter threaded up to the thoracic level, or a paravertebral catheter.

### ANESTHESIA FOR SPECIFIC SURGICAL PROCEDURES IN INFANTS

### Pyloric Stenosis

Pyloric stenosis is diagnosed from 1 week to 3 months of life, typically after persistent projectile vomiting. The child may be severely dehydrated with a profound hypochloremic, hypokalemic, metabolic alkalosis. This procedure is never a surgical emergency. The infant should be carefully evaluated, and any dehydration or metabolic imbalance be corrected before surgery. The metabolic imbalance and dehydration can be corrected slowly, provided there is no cardiovascular instability and hypovolemic shock. A persistent alkalosis increases the risk of postoperative apnea, and it should be noted that correction of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pH may lag several hours after normalization of plasma pH.

Increasingly, the myotomy is performed laparoscopically. Nasogastric tubes are not always used preoperatively as they may worsen the electrolyte imbalance. Aspiration of gastric contents during induction is a primary concern. Even if the child arrives with a nasogastric tube in place, the stomach should still be immediately suctioned with a wide- bore vented catheter in the supine and the right and left lateral positions immediately before induction of anesthesia. This suctioning technique will remove 98% of the gastric contents.<sup>282</sup> A wide variety of techniques have been described for the induction of children with pyloric stenosis.

Awake intubation was previously in vogue but is becoming less common because it can be traumatic to the infant. The use of an induction agent and muscle relaxants resulted in fewer attempts and time for successful intubation of the trachea.<sup>283</sup> The classic rapid-sequence technique (preoxygenation, cricoid pressure, and avoiding mask ventilation) may not be appropriate for an infant with pyloric stenosis. Cricoid pressure can easily distort the anatomy, making laryngoscopy and insertion of the endotracheal tube difficult. It may also not be possible to avoid mask ventilation. Infants rapidly desaturate with apnea, so it is often necessary to gently ventilate the child with 100% oxygen prior to laryngoscopy to avoid hypoxemia and bradycardia. A variety of different intravenous anesthetic agents and neuromuscular blocking agents have been described in this situation. Atropine (0.02 mg/kg) should be given to prevent bradycardia if succinylcholine (2 mg/kg) is used. Postoperative analgesia is generally provided by local infiltration of the skin incision and acetaminophen. Rectus sheath and transversus abdominis plane (TAP) blocks have also been described in this setting.

Reports of apnea after pyloromyotomy have been reported.<sup>284</sup> These children should be observed with apnea monitors and pulse oximetry postoperatively.

### Inguinal Hernia Repair

Inguinal hernia repair is one of the most common surgeries in infants. Inguinal hernia is often bilateral and is more common in males and premature infants. The optimal timing of surgery is controversial. Waiting until the child is older may reduce anesthesia risk. However, an unrepaired asymptomatic hernia is still at risk of incarceration, which may be a life- threatening complication. The repair may be performed laparoscopically or open. Laparoscopic hernia repairs require general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation, while open procedures can be accomplished with a regional technique or general anesthesia. The regional technique is usually with a spinal anesthetic. Caudal anesthesia has been described but usually requires substantial additional sedation. Spinal anesthesia has fewer complications in PACU and shorter PACU stay but fails in approximately 10%–20% of cases.<sup>106,285,286</sup> This may be due to inability to find the subarachnoid space or the child failing to adequately settle even with an effective block. The spinal anesthetic usually provides 60–90 minutes of anesthesia and thus may not be appropriate for hernia repair that is anticipated to be complex. The spinal block may be augmented with a caudal local anesthetic block. A caudal local anesthetic block, ilioinguinal block, or local infiltration of local anesthetic by the surgeon can all provide adequate analgesia and obviate the need for opioids.

The major anesthesic concern around infant inguinal hernia repair is the risk of postoperative apnea, particularly for preterm infants. Most infants with postoperative apnea are former preterm infants (gestational age less than 37 completed weeks) or 44 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA). However, postoperative apnea can occur up to 60 weeks PMA.<sup>287</sup> Studies examining postoperative apnea are difficult to interpret or compare due to widely varying definitions of apnea and methods to detect apnea. In one combined analysis the risk of postoperative apnea increases with lower PMA and lower gestational age.<sup>288</sup> Anemia

(hematocrit &lt;30%) was also found to be an independent risk factor associated with apnea in former preterm infants. Apnea still occurs even with sevoflurane or desflurane.<sup>289</sup> The risk of early apnea is less with awake- regional anesthesia, although the risk of late apnea may be similar.<sup>290,291</sup> There is also some evidence that sedation with spinal anesthesia has increased risk of apnea compared to those that had no sedation.<sup>292</sup>

Prophylactic methylxanthine (caffeine) may reduce the risk of postoperative apnea but does not modify the risk of late apnea.<sup>293</sup> In general, all former preterm infants younger than 56–60 weeks’ PMA should be admitted after inguinal hernia repair for apnea monitoring.<sup>288,294,295</sup> While apnea in term infants is poorly defined, they should be considered vulnerable up to 44 weeks’ PMA.

### Cleft Lip and Palate

Cleft lip and palate are relatively common congenital malformations. Approximately a third are associated with a wide range of other syndromes, so careful and thorough preoperative assessment is required. Cleft lip is usually repaired at 3 to 6 months of age, while the palate is repaired at 9 to 12 months of age. Difficult airway may occur in a subset with retrognathia. In children with large or bilateral clefts the tongue may impinge on the cleft, obstructing the airway, or the laryngoscope blade may be lodged in the cleft. Correction of the lip is usually a straightforward procedure. Cleft palate repair may be complicated by postoperative airway obstruction. The child should be extubated awake. Postoperative analgesia is based on regular acetaminophen and the judicious use of opioids. Infraorbital nerve blocks also provide effective analgesia for cleft lip repair.<sup>296</sup>

### ANESTHESIA FOR SPECIFIC PROCEDURES AND CONDITIONS IN OLDER CHILDREN

### Foreign Body Ingestion and Aspiration

Foreign bodies of the airway and gastrointestinal tract are a major source of morbidity and mortality in infants and young children.<sup>297</sup> Airway obstruction may be acute and may cause significant respiratory distress. Foreign bodies in the upper gastrointestinal tract can erode the gastrointestinal lumen, dislodge, and obstruct the airway. Partial obstruction may be undiagnosed for days or even weeks. The acute choking episode may not have been witnessed and the child may present late with signs of pneumonia. The urgency of removal depends on the degree of respiratory symptoms and the likely location of the obstruction. Several anesthetic techniques have been described. Clear, effective communication with the surgeon throughout the procedure is paramount. Anticholinergic agents are frequently administered prior to induction of anesthesia to reduce secretions and steroids given to reduce airway swelling. Ideally spontaneous ventilation is maintained to reduce the risk of positive ventilation moving the object distally, making extraction more technically challenging. Once the child is deep enough, local anesthetic should be directly applied to the glottis and trachea. Anesthesia may be maintained with volatile agents through the bronchoscope or with a propofol infusion, with the latter providing more reliable drug delivery and reducing waste gas exposure to the operating

room personnel. Remifentanil infusion may also be used to help obtund airway reflexes but should be used judiciously to avoid apnea. Muscle relaxation may be required in some situations, but muscle relaxants should never be given before establishing positive- pressure ventilation.

### Tonsillectomy and Adenoidectomy

Tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy are some of the most common surgical procedures in children. Common indications are recurrent infection and airway obstruction. A wide variety of techniques have been described for tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy. Either a volatile or intravenous- based regimen can be used for maintenance of general anesthesia. A flexible LMA or a preformed Ring–Adair–Elwyn (RAE) endotracheal tube may be used for the airway. Kinking or displacement of the LMA or RAE tube is not uncommon when the surgeon inserts the mouth gag. Children may be extubated awake or deep. Deep extubation may avoid bleeding associated with coughing during emergence, however, deep extubation is associated with an increased risk of airway obstruction after the airway device is removed. If children are extubated while still anesthetized, it is essential that they are subsequently managed in an environment that can rapidly and effectively detect and manage any airway obstruction.

Nausea and vomiting are common after this surgery; prophylactic antiemetics should be administered. Dexamethasone is often given to reduce swelling and antiemesis, however, dexamethasone should never be given if lymphoma is a likely cause of tonsillar hypertrophy as dexamethasone may produce lethal hyperkalemia from tumor lysis. Effective analgesia is a challenge after tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy. Acetaminophen alone is insufficient. Nonsteroidal anti- inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are used in many centers; however, some surgeons avoid their use due to concerns over bleeding even though there is good evidence that bleeding is not increased.<sup>298</sup> Local anesthetic infiltration to the tonsillar bed may provide some analgesia and some surgeons infiltrate with epinephrine to reduce bleeding. Such infiltration must be done cautiously as injection into the major vessels beneath the tonsillar bed may result in seizures or cerebral infarction. Tonsillectomy is associated with significant pain for up to a week.<sup>299</sup> Opioids are often required for tonsillectomy, both in the immediate recovery and early days after the child goes home. They should be used carefully in the presence of obstructive sleep apnea. Bleeding after tonsillectomy may occur immediately postoperatively or in the early days after discharge. Minor bleeding may be managed conservatively but active ongoing bleeding requires anesthesia for surgical management. Anesthesia for a bleeding tonsil requires consideration of acute hypovolemia, full stomach, and difficult laryngoscopy due to active bleeding and swelling.

Children scheduled for tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy frequently have preoperative obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), which is a “disorder of breathing during sleep characterized by prolonged upper airway obstruction and/or intermittent complete obstruction (obstructive apnea) that disrupts normal ventilation during sleep.” These children also have an altered response to CO2.<sup>300</sup> The gold standard to diagnose severity of OSA is the polysomnogram.<sup>301</sup> The

<!-- PDF page 30 -->

apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) and the respiratory disturbance index (RDI) are measurements of the presence and severity of OSA syndrome. The AHI is the number of discrete obstructive events per hour while the RDI includes central and obstructive sleep apnea. OSA is regarded as severe if the AHI is greater than 10 and the oxygen saturation nadir is less than 80%. Most children will have surgery for obstructive symptoms without having had a formal polysomnogram. A variety of other scores based on overnight oximetry, child factors, and degree of symptoms have been developed to assess degree of obstruction and hence perioperative risk.<sup>302</sup>

Given the risk of disordered breathing in children with OSA, management decisions related to timing of surgery, perioperative management, and postoperative disposition strategies are challenging. There is considerable variation in practice surrounding postoperative management of children with and without OSA.<sup>303</sup> The ASA has published a set of guidelines.<sup>304</sup> Certain day- surgery centers routinely send non- OSA children home after tonsillectomy. Others observe the children for at least 4 hours (the interval during which bleeding or respiratory compromise will most likely occur), and then an additional 7 hours if there have been any obstructive events. Children with known or suspected severe OSA should be kept overnight. Children with comorbidities such as Down syndrome, craniofacial abnormalities, younger age (less than 3 years), or obesity should also be considered for overnight admission.

The use of opioids for analgesia should be tempered in patients with OSA. Some centers completely avoid opioids and administer acetaminophen with NSAIDs. If opioids are used, some suggest that children with OSA should have reduced doses. Codeine is no longer recommended for analgesia after tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy as there have been reports of deaths after tonsillectomy related to altered conversion of the prodrug codeine to morphine.<sup>76,305</sup> A small proportion of children have genetically determined rapid conversion to morphine.<sup>306</sup> Thus, the combination of OSA- induced opioid sensitivity and rapid metabolism can lead to fatal outcomes. The FDA has issued a warning strongly recommending that codeine not be administered to children for post- tonsillectomy analgesia, particularly to those with OSA.

### Anterior Mediastinal Mass

Anterior mediastinal masses are commonly due to lymphomas, with acute lymphoblastic leukemia and germ cell tumors. Early tissue diagnosis is essential in the management of these tumors and requires general anesthesia or sedation to facilitate a surgical biopsy.<sup>307</sup> These lesions may cause airway and/or vascular compression, which in an awake patient may be asymptomatic or result in dyspnea, orthopnea, pain, cough, or superior vena cava syndrome. These children frequently present for a biopsy of the lesion or other lymph node which, for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management, must be obtained prior to any chemotherapy or radiotherapy. The major anesthetic concern is profound cardiorespiratory collapse on induction of anesthesia. Such collapse can occur in a child that is asymptomatic. The exact etiology of this collapse is uncertain but probably relates to increasing compression of major vessels, heart and/or the tracheobronchial

tree. Preoperative assessment should include a careful history and examination to determine if positioning has any impact on symptoms and chest computed tomography (CT) and echocardiography. Occlusion of greater than 50% of the airway on CT is associated with increased risk of significant obstruction under anesthesia. Echocardiography will immediately verify if there is obstruction of the great vessels and tracheobronchial tree. The collapse may be triggered by neuromuscular blockade or positive- pressure ventilation; both advocate for maintenance of spontaneous ventilation. If hypotension or desaturation occurs on induction, repositioning the patient to lateral or prone may be lifesaving. In severe cases, every effort should be made to obtain the biopsy with mild sedation in the spontaneously ventilating patient.

### Scoliosis

Lateral and rotational derangements in the spine and vertebra angles, respectively, appear primarily in school- aged children. While the majority are idiopathic (70%–80%), more severe forms are attributable to congenital anomalies or neuromuscular disease. Corrective surgery often involves distraction and fusion of multiple levels of the thoracic and lumbar spine and is commonly associated with significant intraoperative problems including major blood loss, venous air embolism, and spinal cord injury (usually from spinal cord ischemia during straightening of the spine).

Hemodynamic stability and integrity of the spinal cord are goals of anesthesia during scoliosis surgery (see Chapter 35). Wide surgical exposure, paravertebral dissection, and vertebral distraction can precipitate massive blood loss and venous air embolus, leading to cardiovascular collapse. Therefore, normovolemia should be maintained throughout the procedure. Patients with neuromuscular scoliosis are three times more likely to have intraoperative cardiac arrests when compared with patients with idiopathic scoliosis. Somatosensory- evoked potentials (SSEPs) monitor the posterior columns of the spinal cord via signals in the somatosensory cortex generated in response to stimulation of ulnar, median, and posterior tibial nerves. Motor- evoked potentials (MEPs) assess the motor pathway by stimulating primary motor cortex and monitoring distal muscle group contraction. The motor pathway is not fully mature until about 13 years of age, so younger children often require a higher delivered cortical stimulation compared with adults to obtain reliable MEPs. MEPs are more sensitive to hypotension or vascular injury. Optimal positioning, avoidance of nondepolarizing muscle relaxants and anesthetics that depress SSEP and MEP monitoring and blood conservation techniques (blood salvaging and antifibrinolysis) are essential anesthetic goals for scoliosis surgery.

### Myopathies and Other Neurodegenerative Conditions

Children may require a muscle biopsy to assist the diagnosis of a myopathy or other neurodegenerative condition. There are a broad range of myopathies that present a range of issues for anesthesia that are associated with compromised cardiac or respiratory function, developmental delay, poor nutritional status, malignant hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, and propofol infusion syndrome. Thorough preoperative evaluation is essential, including an assessment

<!-- PDF page 31 -->

of respiratory and cardiac function.<sup>308</sup> There must also be clear communication with the referring physician to identify and discuss the most likely diagnosis, and which tests will be performed on the biopsy.<sup>309</sup>

Awake- regional anesthesia would be the ideal option for cooperative older children but is rarely feasible in younger children.<sup>310</sup> Succinylcholine is contraindicated in children with muscular dystrophy due to risk of acute rhabdomyolysis.

Volatile anesthetics have been associated with rhabdomyolysis with muscular dystrophy, particularly Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy, and children with an elevated creatinine kinase.<sup>311,312</sup> Some children with mitochondrial disorders may be more likely to be susceptible to adverse metabolic and cardiac events after propofol.<sup>313,314</sup> This may be linked to propofol infusion syndrome.

The impact of anesthesia on the biopsy specimen also needs consideration. A “non- triggering” anesthetic is required for a muscle biopsy for a contracture test. Care should also be taken that no local anesthetic contaminates the specimen. Some metabolic clinicians prefer propofol to be avoided if a mitochondrial enzyme analysis is planned.

There is no optimal anesthetic for children having a muscle biopsy. Choice of agents requires a clear understanding of the likely diagnosis and hence balance of risks, any anesthesia- related requirements stipulated by the laboratory analyzing the sample, and tailoring the plan to the child’s particular cardiorespiratory status and ability to tolerate an awake procedure.

### MANAGEMENT OF NEURODIVERGENT CHILDREN

Neurodivergent children are more likely to require anesthesia for surgery and a range of other procedures. They are, however, often neglected in terms of systematic research. For example, most studies looking at premedication exclude children with developmental disability, although they may need it, and benefit from it more than other children.

A key aspect in the perioperative care of these children is to appreciate that they are heterogeneous with a range of challenges and requirements. These children often have multiple diagnoses and several comorbidities. Even those with a single diagnosis, such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), may present with a wide range of behavioral issues. No two children with ASD are the same and care must be tailored to each child.<sup>315,316</sup> Another key element is to engage the parents or caregivers early in planning. They are best placed to give advice on what aspects of management will be challenging and what strategies are likely to work best.

Children with ASD often have fixed routines. Similarly, children with ASD may find particular actions, environments, noises, or smells distressing. Knowing and accommodating these will reduce distress. Children with ASD also may find communication easier if things are explained with pictures.

Cerebral palsy describes a broad spectrum of movement and posture disorders with varying severity. Anesthesia challenges include poor nutrition, concurrent poor

respiratory status, poor cough reflex, reflux of gastric contents, difficulty with positioning, susceptibility to pressure injuries, hypothermia, and difficult venous access.<sup>317,318</sup> Muscle spasm after surgery may result in significant pain. Spasm may be effectively reduced with regional analgesia techniques and/or diazepam. Opioids are often required and must be used carefully to avoid respiratory depression. Titrating analgesia to effect may be difficult in children with cognitive impairment. Often parents or caregivers are the best judges of whether their child is in pain.

### PEDIATRIC SEDATION

Since most infants, children, and neurodivergent patients are unable to cooperate for brief noninvasive diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, sedation with intravenous hypnotic drugs is a less-invasive alternative to general anesthesia. Procedural sedation extends beyond the safe confines of the operating room suites where standardized monitoring and equipment are managed with trained personnel. Since the procedure and sedative agents can impede respiration, maintenance of a patent airway and spontaneous ventilation is paramount and should be closely monitored and managed by trained anesthesiologists and anesthetists. The American Academy of Pediatrics and ASA published separate guidelines for providing sedation. Both documents provide best practice periprocedural guidelines for systemic approach to sedating pediatric patients. Both emphasize preprocedural evaluation and preparation, continuous monitoring of cardiopulmonary function by trained personnel, postprocedural care, and quality improvement programs.<sup>319</sup>

### NON–OPERATING ROOM ANESTHESIA

Advances in diagnostic and interventional procedures in pediatric patients beyond the traditional operating room suites have steadily posed additional challenges (see Chapter 69). In contrast to adults, most pediatric patients require sedation or general anesthesia for these procedures.

### Diagnostic and Interventional Radiologic Procedures

Most diagnostic and interventional radiological procedures require patients to be motionless for a prolonged period. A variety of nonanesthetic techniques such as feed and wrap, video gaming, play therapy, and distraction techniques have been advocated. When these techniques fail, sedation or general anesthesia is required to render the patient safely motionless during the procedure. Most diagnostic imaging procedures should be painless and require sedation and spontaneous ventilation with a natural airway. These include CT and MRI. However, therapeutic and interventional radiological procedures can be painful and require periods of apnea, which are best managed with general anesthesia and endotracheal intubation.<sup>320</sup> All caregivers are at risk for radiation exposure and should be equipped with lead aprons and have the ability to monitor the patient remotely.

### Gastrointestinal Endoscopy

Infants and children require deep levels of sedation and even general anesthesia for esophagogastroduodenoscopy. In some cases, the large caliber of the gastroscope may compromise the patient’s upper airway and require tracheal intubation. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography requires significant insufflation of air or CO2, which is painful and requires general anesthesia.

### Radiation Therapy

Anesthesia for patients undergoing radiation therapy is to deliver therapeutic doses of radiation to the tumor while limiting damage to the surrounding healthy tissue. These sessions have two components. Three- dimensional X- ray imaging assures proper positioning of the patient and target lesion. This is followed by three- dimensional conformational radiation therapy and intensity- modulated radiation therapy, which focuses the energy beam on the tumor while reducing radiation to the normal surrounding tissue. Infants and young children must be immobile and may have their head and neck fixed in an immobilization cast. Given the health hazards of ionizing radiation exposure to the health care providers, the patient should be remotely monitored from a shielded area during the imaging and treatment segments. A course of treatments consists of five sessions a week for several weeks. Since most of these patients have implantable venous access systems (Port- A- Cath, ICU Medical, San Celementi, CA), deep sedation can be achieved with propofol infusion.
