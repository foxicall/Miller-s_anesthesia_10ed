第 78 章 圍手術期神經認知障礙：重點精華總整理 ​

Perioperative Neurocognitive Disorders

主題索引： 術後譫妄、術後認知功能障礙、神經認知

本摘要由 Gemini 依本章原文整理，尚未經臨床專家審閱。引用頁碼為各章 PDF 頁序；用藥與處置請對照原文及院內現行規範。

1. 核心觀念精要 (Core Concepts) ​

  • 圍手術期神經認知障礙 (PNDs) 是老年患者最常見的圍手術期併發症，其主要危險因子為高齡與未經診斷的既有認知功能障礙。（原文第 1 頁）
  • 新的 PND 命名法與 DSM-5 診斷標準接軌，要求患者除客觀神經心理測試結果外，還需具備主觀認知抱怨及日常生活功能評估。（原文第 1 頁、原文第 2 頁）
  • 術後譫妄 (POD) 定義為術後一週內或出院前發生的急性認知障礙，特徵為注意力不集中、意識波動與思考混亂，目前尚無有效治療，僅能採取預防措施。（原文第 2 頁）
  • 目前關於麻醉藥物選擇或腦波監測 (EEG) 導引麻醉是否能降低 PND 發生率的證據仍有爭議，且臨床試驗結果不一致。（原文第 4 頁、原文第 5 頁）

2. 臨床指引與處置決策 (Clinical Guidelines & Management) ​

術前評估與禁忌症 ​

  • 所有 65 歲以上患者應在術前評估時進行客觀認知功能篩檢，若有既有認知功能障礙風險者亦同。（原文第 5 頁）
  • 應告知 65 歲以上患者術後可能出現混亂、注意力不集中與記憶力問題等 PND 風險。（原文第 5 頁）
  • 術前應識別並優化高風險患者的共病症，並考慮進行基線神經認知評估以利術後追蹤。（原文第 5 頁）

術中監測與藥物劑量建議 (Dosing / Monitoring) ​

  • 術中應避免使用 Beers 清單藥物，包括中樞作用型抗膽鹼藥物、苯二氮平類藥物 (benzodiazepines)、Meperidine 及抗精神病藥物。（原文第 5 頁）
  • 術中應監測年齡調整後的呼氣末 MAC 分數，並維持病患的正常體溫。（原文第 5 頁）
  • 應避免相對於每位患者個別血壓範圍的相對低血壓。（原文第 5 頁）
  • 可考慮使用基於 EEG 的術中腦部監測，以評估譫妄風險並調整麻醉劑量，目標包括限制術中爆發抑制 (burst suppression) 並促進額葉 Alpha 波功率。（原文第 5 頁）

關鍵介入步驟 (Step-by-step Interventions) ​

  1. 術後應定期使用標準篩檢工具（如 CAM）監測譫妄發生。（原文第 5 頁）
  2. 住院期間應採用多組分非藥物介入計畫，如 Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) 或 ABCDEF 組合式照護，以預防譫妄。（原文第 5 頁）
  3. 術後應優化疼痛控制，並優先使用多模式非鴉片類藥物。（原文第 5 頁）

3. 重要併發症與危機處理 (Complications & Crisis Management) ​

  • 本章未提供可直接套用的併發症急救流程，請依相關臨床章節與院內規範核對。

4. 臨床重點口訣 / 高頻考點 (Key Takeaways / Board Exam Focus) ​

  • PND 包含術後譫妄 (POD)、延遲神經認知恢復 (dNCR) 及術後神經認知障礙 (術後 NCD)，診斷需結合主觀抱怨、日常生活功能評估與客觀測試。（原文第 2 頁、原文第 3 頁）
  • 術後 30 天內的認知功能下降建議稱為延遲神經認知恢復 (dNCR)，以強調其可能為暫時性的恢復過程。（原文第 3 頁）
  • 全身性發炎與神經發炎在 PND 中扮演主導角色，手術引發的周邊發炎可增加血腦屏障通透性並活化小膠質細胞，而全身麻醉藥物對 PND 的因果貢獻仍較不明確。（原文第 6 頁、原文第 8 頁、原文第 9 頁）
  • 臨床研究顯示，術前認知功能障礙是預測術後 PND 最強的危險因子，而非單純由麻醉藥物所致。（原文第 1 頁、原文第 5 頁）

MILES BERGER, BEVERLEY A. ORSER, and LISBETH EVERED

KEY POINTSᑏConcerns about postoperative cognitive function have been voiced by older patients,
their families, and their caregivers for more than a century and, more recently, have been
documented with objective testing.
ᑏPerioperative neurocognitive disorders (PNDs) are the most common perioperative
complications in older adults, and the strongest risk factors for PNDs are advanced age and
preexisting cognitive impairment, which is usually undiagnosed.
ᑏThe new nomenclature for PNDs requires the presence of subjective cognitive complaints in
addition to objective neuropsychological testing and assessment of daily function.
ᑏThe new PND nomenclature aligns with the diagnosis of cognitive disorders in the Diagnostic
and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) and thus has broader clinical relevance.
ᑏObjective testing can take many forms and can be variously analyzed to establish whether
cognitive decline has occurred.
ᑏFurther evidence is required to determine if targeting light anesthesia using
electroencephalography may reduce/prevent postoperative delirium and subsequent long-
term decline.
ᑏMany mechanisms for PND have been proposed and tested in preclinical models.
Neuroinflammation induced by surgery and disease in the setting of a vulnerable brain is the
mechanistic hypothesis that has the most support at this time.
ᑏBlood and EEG biomarkers may offer a unique method to identify those at risk and may prove
to be useful targets for prevention of delirium and other PNDs.

Introduction ​

The association between anesthesia and surgery and cognitive changes in older adults has been recognized for more than a century.1 However, anecdotal reports of cognitive changes did not become a major research focus until the International Study of Post- Operative Cognitive Dysfunction was published in the late 1990s.2 Subsequently, in 2001, Newman and colleagues reported that early cognitive dysfunction after cardiac surgery was associated with increased risk of cognitive decline 5 years later.3 Since then, there has been intense interest and investigation in all aspects of both postoperative delirium (POD, which is characterized by fluctuating levels of inattention and consciousness) and longer- term cognitive decline following anesthesia and surgery. This chapter reviews the topic of perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PNDs) for both clinicians and researchers. First, we discuss the new nomenclature for PNDs that was published in 2018.4 This new classification system uses both subjective and objective metrics of cognitive decline, as well as metrics for activities

of daily living (ADLs), and thus accords with other neuropsychiatric diagnoses included in the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM5).5 Second, we summarize the pre- and intraoperative risk factors associated with various PNDs.6 Third, we review and discuss potential mechanisms underlying postoperative neurocognitive disorders (postoperative NCDs), including those related to anesthesia, surgery, patient comorbidities, and other aspects of perioperative care. Fourth, we discuss methods to prevent and to treat these disorders.

Clinical Overview ​

In 1998 the International Study of Post- Operative Cognitive Dysfunction (ISPOCD) group coined the term postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD), which reflected an objectively measured decline in cognitive function that typically persisted beyond the period expected for normal recovery from the physiologic and pharmacologic effects of anesthesia and surgery.2 Previously, cognitive impairment

identified at baseline in patients presenting for anesthesia and surgery was referred to as preexisting cognitive impairment (PreCI).7

During the hospital admission the most common complication experienced by older individuals following anesthesia and surgery is postoperative delirium (POD).8 POD can present as hypoactive, hyperactive (agitated), or mixed. POD refers to a form of acute cognitive disruption characterized by inattention, a fluctuating course, and cognitive disturbance and is distressing for the patients themselves, as well as family, caregivers, and staff. POD is strongly associated with increased risk of mortality, morbidity, and dementia and has significant long- term detrimental psychosocial and mood outcomes.9 At present, the only effective treatment is prevention.

During the first 30 days postoperatively it is difficult to interpret the relative significance of any observed cognitive or functional decline to long- term recovery, since this may simply be part of normal physiologic and pharmacologic recovery, pain, and ongoing medications.4 In view of this, the consensus recommendations suggest this be identified as delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR).

Cognitive and functional decline has been observed from 1 day to 7.5 years after anesthesia and surgery, although it is unclear to what extent, if any, anesthesia/surgery play a causal role in longer term cognitive decline (ie such as 7.5 years after surgery).10 As discussed below, consensus recommendations suggest a temporal association between cognitive disorders and anesthesia and surgery from day 1 to 12 months postoperatively.

Nomenclature and Assessment of Perioperative Neurocognitive Disorders ​

In 2018, the International Nomenclature Consensus Working Group proposed new nomenclature to standardize the identification and classification of perioperative cognitive changes falling under the umbrella of PND.4 This new nomenclature encompasses the overlapping and heterogeneous definitions of previously used terms, specifically preexisting cognitive impairment (pre-CI), and postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD). In particular, the previously used term POCD reflected an objectively measured decline in cognitive function that persisted beyond the period expected for normal recovery from the physiologic and pharmacologic effects of anesthesia and surgery in older individuals.3

By restructuring the criteria for PND by incorporating the DSM- 5 definitions of neurocognitive disorders (NCDs), the working group sought to align perioperative cognitive changes with NCD in the general population, to standardize assessments and identification of PND, and to promote communication across medical and surgical specialties. These fundamental changes were also necessary to further research and improve clinical care in this area. Specifically, the DSM- 5 criteria for NCD include (1) a decline in cognition, defined as a decline in at least one cognitive domain, together with a subjective deficit or cognitive complaint (expressed by the patient, a family member, or observed by

a clinician); and (2) an assessment of daily functional ability, based on an evaluation of the patient’s instrumental ADLs. Additionally, the diagnosis of NCD requires that the observed cognitive changes cannot be better explained by a medical condition (e.g., Alzheimer disease, cerebral vascular disease) or a psychiatric disorder.

The term POD refers to a form of acute cognitive disruption characterized by inattention, a fluctuating course, disorganized thinking, and changes in the level of consciousness. POD is diagnosed according to the criteria for delirium presented in the DSM- 5.5 Delirium occurring in the community or in hospital inpatients is diagnosed according to the same definitions and criteria. Previously, POCD and preexisting cognitive impairment were confined to the field of perioperative medicine research, where they have been historically defined by objective criteria without consideration for subjective cognitive complaints or functional deficits. In contrast, cognitive impairment and decline are typically diagnosed in the general medical community according to either DSM- 5 criteria or the definitions of the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer’s Association (NIA- AA), which require both a subjective cognitive complaint and an assessment of ADLs or instrumental ADLs. The NIA- AA terms mild cognitive impairment11 and dementia12 are far more familiar to most clinicians than the corresponding DSM- 5 terms ( mild and major NCD , respectively), but the definitions and criteria map closely onto each other.

DELIRIUM ​

POD is classified as a specific category consistent with DSM- 5 terminology when the patient is in the immediate postoperative period and other specific causes for symptoms have been excluded. The reported incidence of POD in elderly patients is highly dependent on how frequently assessments are performed and the diagnostic tool used for the assessment. A widely used and validated tool for detecting postoperative delirium is the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM).13,14 The term postoperative refers to a specific and known temporal association with anesthesia and surgery. POD is defined as delirium when it meets DSM- 5 diagnostic criteria and occurs in hospital up to one week postprocedure or until discharge. To date, most studies have investigated delirium as simply having occurred or not (a binary outcome). However, in the future, consideration should also be given to delirium severity15 and psychosocial outcomes.9

Delirium severity has been linked to pathophysiologic processes associated with neurodegenerative disease,15 while one or more episodes of delirium following cardiac surgery have recently been shown to be associated with long- term depression, trauma, and other poor psychosocial outcomes.9

POSTOPERATIVE NEUROCOGNITIVE DISORDERS (NCDs) ​

Postoperative NCD has replaced the previously used term and definition of POCD.3,10,16 Notably, POCD did not require a subjective complaint or evidence of functional impairment, whereas the DSM- 5 definition of NCD requires both. Thus, the main differences between POCD and

postoperative NCD are the requirement for a subjective cognitive complaint and evidence of impairment in ADLs (for major postoperative NCD).

SUBJECTIVE COGNITIVE CONCERN ​

A subjective cognitive complaint can be reported by the individual, a family member, a caregiver, or a clinician. It is unlikely that a patient or an informant would be able to accurately assess subtle cognitive decline in the early postoperative period. Therefore, although assessment for NCD after discharge but before full recovery may be technically possible, the clinical relevance of its attribution remains unclear. Therefore, the term delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR) is recommended for cognitive issues occurring up to 30 days after the procedure, when most patients will have recovered from surgery and hospitalization. The diagnostic criteria for NCD should be applied, but if cognition is impaired, the problem would be referred to as dNCR, to highlight the possible transience of the impairment. It is possible that high- functioning individuals may report cognitive concerns without objective evidence of decline. This might still be considered dNCR in the context of clinical relevance for a specific individual.4

ACTIVITIES OF DAILY LIVING ASSESSMENT ​

Assessment of daily function is an essential element of classifying patients as having postoperative mild or major NCD. This assessment is performed using a tool appropriate for measuring ADLs, which refer to everyday personal care activities that are fundamental to caring for oneself and maintaining independence. To detect more subtle declines in function, the instrumental ADLs (IADLs) are used; these include activities like shopping, driving, and managing finances. In cases of mild NCD (i.e., mild cognitive impairment), the ADLs and instrumental ADLs are maintained, whereas for major NCD (dementia), a decline in daily function (i.e., in ADLs or IADLs) is an essential criterion for attribution of the disorder.

OBJECTIVE TESTING ​

According to the DSM- 55 and the recommended PND terms, postoperative mild NCD requires a decline in the results of cognitive testing, relative to controls or norms, of one to two standard deviations, and postoperative major NCD requires a decline of two or more standard deviations on at least one cognitive domain (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual–motor, or social cognition)5 using an appropriate neuropsychological assessment battery. Neither the DSM- 5 nor the NIA- AA criteria for objective testing specify the individual neuropsychological tests to be used or the number of tests required in a battery. Importantly, this diagnostic criterion refers to psychometric assessments that allow objective assessment of specific cognitive domains, not to the use of screening tools such as the Mini- Mental State Examination (MMSE) or the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Although useful for preoperative risk assessment, these brief screening tools do not have the sensitivity to assess subtle cognitive change or changes in specific cognitive domains.

Beyond 30 days after surgery, the term postoperative mild NCD (equivalent to postoperative mild cognitive impairment) or postoperative major NCD (equivalent to postoperative dementia) is used instead of dNCR. The “postoperative” specifier should be used when the disorder is initially diagnosed within the first 12 months after the procedure and for as long as the NCD criteria are met. If the disorder is first noticed or diagnosed more than 12 months after surgery, the specifier “postoperative” should not be used. POD, dNCR, and postoperative NCD all fall under the umbrella term perioperative neurocognitive disorders, although delirium is also defined separately in the DSM- 5.

Measurement and Diagnosis of Perioperative Neurocognitive Disorders ​

Historically, a diagnosis of POCD relied only on an objective assessment of cognitive decline using a battery of neuropsychological tests covering several cognitive domains. In many studies, very conservative cut points were applied (e.g., 1.96 standard deviations below controls on 2 or more tests out of a battery of 8–10 tests). These research applications contrast with the objective criteria required for postoperative mild NCD or postoperative major NCD, as described above.

ASSUMPTIONS UNDERLYING ASSESSMENT OF PERIOPERATIVE NEUROCOGNITIVE DISORDER ​

Prospective studies investigating PND have typically assessed baseline cognition at a single time point only, often within days of the procedure. This approach is based on three important but not necessarily valid assumptions. First, it assumes normal preoperative cognitive function despite many studies identifying a high incidence of preoperative cognitive impairment among older adults.7,17,18 Second, it assumes stable baseline cognition. Third, it assumes that results are reproducible, even without an intervention. Yet even if patients can be assumed to have stable cognition, it is unlikely that two consecutive assessments would yield the same results, due to a variety of intrinsic and extrinsic factors.

OUTCOME CRITERIA FOR COGNITIVE DECLINE ​

Reference to an appropriate control group or use of normative data should be used to define objective changes on neuropsychological testing following surgery, which has the advantage of controlling for cognitive changes over a similar time period, largely to account for practice and time effects.19 One method for comparing the extent of cognitive change in surgical patients to that observed in controls is the reliable change index (RCI)20 which uses either a control group or normative data, and as such it satisfies the DSM- 5 criteria for NCD.

Sensitivity ​

The number of tests used in a neuropsychological test battery directly affects the sensitivity of the measure. For

assessment of the cognitive component of NCD, the DSM- 5 requires decline in at least one cognitive domain (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual–motor, or social cognition), but it does not recommend specific neuropsychological tests or a specific number of tests. Sensitivity can be improved by considering small declines across several tests or a single, large decline in the reliable change index or other measures that aggregate scores on individual tests into overall cognitive domain scores.20

While seemingly esoteric, the various methods of analysis can markedly affect the diagnosis of POCD, as highlighted by Keizer et al .,21 who reported POCD incidences of 10.5%, 31%, and 7.7% using the same test results with different methods of analysis (incidence defined on the basis of 1 standard deviation decline, 20% decline, and RCI, respectively).

Clearly, standardization will be required to interpret results across many studies.

More recently, computerized cognitive assessment batteries have been developed that avoid practice effects and offer easier, more rapid, and standardized administration. Computer- based approaches also have the potential to overcome cultural and language difficulties. To date, however, computerized test batteries have received limited attention in POCD/PND research, mainly due to a lack of relevant validation studies.

Group Change versus Individual Change ​

Studies investigating PND have variously investigated either individual change or group change.22,23 Individual change usually refers to a dichotomous outcome of “decline” versus “no decline.” In contrast, group analysis considers differences between groups on a continuous scale, such as the difference in a continuous cognitive measure between groups of control and treated patients in a randomized controlled trial. Clinical trials typically use statistics to compare an a priori primary outcome between two groups of patients to test whether any observed difference is due to chance. Unfortunately, the group approach misses important information from the individuals within each group. Those with the greatest cognitive decline may represent a small proportion of the group, but arguably are the most critical to consider. Some individuals may even demonstrate cognitive improvement, through the correction of underlying pathology, relief of pain, and improved function (e.g., improved mobility, improved ADLs and IADLs). Statistics that simply average the results of individuals across the group will miss these important individual changes, which may reflect important, identifiable, and perhaps modifiable factors.

ISSUES ASSOCIATED WITH REPEAT TESTING ​

Serial neuropsychological testing is important to reliably assess the cognitive trajectory of an individual over time; however, repeat testing may introduce several sources of error, such as reduced reliability, floor or ceiling effects, and practice effects. It is unclear whether practice effects can be eliminated, as these can be observed as far out as 2.5 years from the procedure. Individual factors (e.g., age, sex, culture, language, education, comorbidities, baseline cognitive function) and perioperative factors (e.g.,

anxiety, medications, pain) may also modulate the effects of repeated testing. Other strategies to improve reliability include parallel test versions and the use of control groups. The main concern with control groups is the need to select a well- matched group. It is generally desirable to match comorbid conditions to more accurately evaluate the effect of the surgery itself. However, the extent to which this is achievable is unclear, since in the absence of randomization there remains the possibility of unmeasured differences and confounders between groups.

Risk Factors and Perioperative Management ​

PRE- AND INTRAOPERATIVE RISK FACTORS FOR PERIOPERATIVE NEUROCOGNITIVE DISORDERS ​

A large body of work has examined pre- and intraoperative risk factors for PNDs. In general, preoperative impairment of cognitive function, older age, and lower education/IQ have been found to predict increased risk for both PNDs and POCD23 (reviewed in references 6 and 24). Whether preoperative impairment of cognitive function also contributes to the risk of dNCR after surgery is unclear from the existing literature.25

One overarching theory is that PNDs arise from a mixture of predisposing and precipitating factors.26,27 Predisposing factors consist mainly of preoperative characteristics, such as advanced age and preexisting cognitive impairment. Precipitating factors include a wide variety of intra- and perioperative risk factors, such as longer- duration surgery, greater intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative pain and sleep deprivation. In general, the literature is mixed as to the risks and benefits of specific drugs. Although some studies have shown protective effects of certain drugs, including propofol, ketamine, and dexmedetomidine, other studies have shown no beneficial effect of these drugs.28–33 Similarly, there is extensive but conflicting literature on whether using electroencephalography (EEG) guidance to reduce anesthetic dosage (or simply lowering anesthetic dose independent of EEG monitoring) results in lower rates of PND, particularly POD34 (see below, under General Anesthetic Drugs).

At least three reasons may account for the conflicting literature regarding pharmacologic and management strategies to reduce PNDs. First, it is difficult to design studies to compare equipotent doses of intravenous and inhaled anesthetics (e.g., propofol vs. isoflurane). These drugs produce their clinical effects by interacting with different molecular targets35 and thus, they differentially modulate brain activity and network connectivity.36 While evidence is available to both support and refute a cognitive benefit of total intravenous anesthesia over inhalational anesthesia, such differences in outcome may partly reflect differences in dosage regimens between clinical trials, rather than differences in the intrinsic cognitive- sparing properties of propofol versus the inhaled anesthetics. Second, a general issue with perioperative clinical trials that are designed to reduce major complications such as PND is the likelihood that the trial involved three distinct groups of participants: those who will never experience the complication, regardless of

whether they receive the intervention or not; those who will always experience the complication, regardless of whether they receive the intervention or not; and those whose risk of the complication may be altered by the intervention. The third group is the only one where the intervention has any chance of reducing the complication rate. However, researchers usually cannot determine the group to which individual patients belong, and thus, at the start of a clinical trial cannot identify the patients who will benefit most (i.e., those in the third group). If the proportion of patients in the third group varies among different clinical trials, the power to detect and compare reductions in PNDs or other outcomes across the studies will be seriously compromised. Finally, it is possible that neither the choice of anesthetic drugs nor the use of EEG- guided anesthetic titration has any effect on the rate of PNDs, and the positive results reported in some studies are simply the result of type I statistical errors. This possibility is plausible, given that, at least in the case of POD, much of the risk may be due to preoperative (and thus predisposing) patient factors, including impairment of baseline cognition, rather than intraoperative management choices.37 Nonetheless, large clinical trials are currently underway that may help to resolve some of this controversy38 and determine whether anesthetic drug choice (and dosage) will alter PND rates in specific subgroups of patients.

Other risk factors identified more recently for PNDs include frailty, which is associated with numerous poor outcomes, including longer hospital stays, increased mortality,39 and an increased risk of postoperative NCD at 3 and 12 months after noncardiac surgery.40

Intraoperatively, cerebral oximetry and EEG are key biomarkers for identifying oxidative stress and neuronal dysfunction and can be used to tailor anesthetic regimens to preserve and optimize neural function.8,41,42

PRECLINICAL STUDY DESIGNS ​

Preclinical studies of PNDs typically involve treating laboratory animals (most often rodents) with one or more general anesthetic drugs, with or without a surgical intervention. A wide variety of rodent models have been studied, including those that mimic aging, genetic variants, metabolic syndrome, or neurodegenerative disorders in humans. Commonly used preclinical surgery models include laparotomy, intramedullary fixation of fractured tibia, partial hepatectomy, splenectomy, and ligation of the cecum. Cognition in laboratory animals is often probed in the early recovery period by means of learning- and memory- related behavioral assays. For example, frequently studied cognitive end points are spatial memory (assessed with the Morris water maze or the Barnes maze) and recognition memory (assessed with the novel object recognition task). Such behavioral studies are often coupled with in vitro biochemical assays and electrophysiologic recordings.

It is important to interpret the results of preclinical studies while considering the limitations of the experimental models. Any inconsistencies between clinical and preclinical studies may depend on differences in both the species and the experimental design. For example, delirium is

characterized by alterations in attention, confusion, and disorganized thinking in humans, but these core features are difficult to model and measure experimentally in laboratory animals. Thus, other cognitive domains, such as learning and memory, are typically assessed. As an example, impaired spatial working memory has been frequently reported after exposure to general anesthesia in laboratory animals,47 yet spatial working memory is rarely studied in patients after surgery. Thus, subtle memory deficits that are identified in preclinical studies may remain undetected in patients. Also, the timescales of preclinical PND studies typically range from days to weeks, whereas PNDs in patients occur from a few days to one year after surgery.

Finally, PNDs in patients likely arise from a combination of factors, including patient characteristics, inflammation, and exposure to certain drugs, rather than single factors alone, yet combinations of multiple factors are rarely studied systematically in laboratory animal PND models. Overall, caution is needed when translating the results of studies of surrogate end points in laboratory animals to outcomes in patients.47

Despite the limitations, preclinical studies have provided mechanistic insights into factors that likely contribute to PNDs. The results of these studies suggest that multiple receptors and cell- signaling pathways are involved. Thus, it is unlikely that PNDs can be prevented or treated by a single mitigation strategy. However, the adoption of multimodal approaches may be effective in reducing the incidence and severity of PNDs,48–50 as is the case for the modern treatment of pain.

From these animal model studies, key factors that have emerged as contributing to PNDs include inflammation, advanced age, and preexisting neurocognitive impairment, which collectively contribute to what is loosely referred to as “brain fragility.” The most current and popular model of PND proposes that systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation play dominant roles. A possible causal contribution of general anesthetic drugs to PNDs remains less clear, but it remains important to determine whether anesthetic drugs are involved since anesthesiologists can control which drugs, at what doses, are administered to patients. Other factors contributing to PNDs are not unique to surgical patients; rather, they are associated with various neurologic disorders, including traumatic brain injury, sepsis, long COVID- 19, and neurodegenerative diseases. A partial list of causal factors that have been implicated in PNDs is provided here and discussed in the subsections below:

  • Systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation

  • Disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB)

  • Modulation of microglia and astrocytes

  • General anesthetic drugs

  • Altered inhibitory and excitatory neurotransmission

  • Neurodegenerative disease-related pathology

At the same time, inflammation fundamentally alters the response of brain neurons to anesthetic drugs by increasing the number of anesthetic- sensitive receptors.53 The role of inflammation and neuroinflammation in PNDs is briefly summarized below and has been extensively reviewed by Subramaniyan and Terrando,54 Safavynia et al. ,6 and others.6,24,53,55

Surgical trauma, oxidative stress, ischemia, and other forms of sterile tissue injury stimulate cells to release damage- associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). The resulting circulating DAMPs include molecules such as

high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) protein56 and S100 calcium- binding protein β (S100β). In addition, exogenous pathogens, including bacteria and viruses, foreign nucleic acids, and RNAs act as signaling molecules; these are referred to as pathogen- associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). A widely studied PAMP is lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a component of the Gram- negative bacterial cell wall. The PAMPs and DAMPs bind to pattern recognition receptors such as Toll- like receptors that are expressed by innate immune cells such as macrophages, mast cells, and bone marrow–derived monocytes. The DAMPs and PAMPs also bind to receptors present on nonimmune cells, including epithelial cells and fibroblasts.

The activation of Toll- like receptors stimulates second messenger signaling cascades that activate transcription factors, including nuclear factor kappa B and posttranslational modifications. These changes then trigger the production and release of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin- 1β (IL- 1β), interleukin- 6 (IL- 6), and tumor necrosis factor- α (TNF- α), as well as the activation of oxidative stress pathways. Numerous studies have demonstrated that increases in IL- 1β, IL- 6, TNF- α and other cytokine levels can be detected in human cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) after surgery, which suggests that neuroinflammation may also play a role in PNDs in humans.56–62 Furthermore, the time course of changes in plasma and within the brain in animal models often mimics that of changes that occur in human plasma and CSF after surgery.63

Surgery- induced activation of the immune system leads to an increase in monocytes within the mouse brain and human CNS (as measured in the CSF after surgery)57 ; however, it is unclear whether this increase results from an influx of monocytes from outside into the CNS or the mobilization of cells within CNS reservoirs, including those in the cerebrospinal brain–vascular interphase or the CSF itself. Current studies may shed light on what percent of older patients experience an increase in CSF monocyte levels and to what extent this is associated with PNDs.64

Although both preclinical and clinical studies have shown postoperative increases in the level of inflammatory cytokines in the CSF, relatively few studies have shown a clear association between increased postoperative CSF cytokine levels and PNDs.57,59 It also remains unclear the extent to which these postoperative increases in CSF inflammatory cytokines result from the passive flux of cytokines across a disrupted BBB,65 versus increased cytokine production within the CNS by microglia or other cell types. More studies are needed to determine the extent to which neuroinflammation plays a causal role in PNDs; however, a substantial body of evidence from outside perioperative medicine has shown that central neuroinflammation plays a causative role in multiple other disorders that involve cognitive dysfunction.66–68

Impaired resolution of inflammatory responses has also been theorized to contribute to PNDs. Molecules leading to the resolution of inflammation in laboratory animals (such as lipid resolvins, protectins, and maresins) have also been detected in human CSF. Changes in levels of some of these factors after surgery (as well as proinflammatory lipid mediators such as prostaglandins) have been detected in older surgical patients.69 Further, exogenous treatment with antiinflammatory lipids related to resolvins, such as maresin- 1, ameliorate postoperative memory deficits in mouse models.70 Even so, it remains unclear whether a hypoactive resolution response and lower levels of pro- resolving lipid mediators in the human CNS contribute to PNDs. Results from preclinical animal models raise the possibility that exogenous supplementation of specific pro- resolving mediators may reduce neuroinflammation and improve cognition after surgery in older adults70,71 by promoting synthesis of pro- resolving lipid mediators from omega- 3 fatty acids or enhancing cholinergic signaling, since vagal nerve stimulation reduces cognitive deficits following injection of lipopolysaccharide in rodent models.72 Additional studies of modulating the inflammatory response to surgery to reduce

human PND rates are underway.73 Interestingly, nonselective immune suppression with drugs such as dexamethasone or statins have not proven beneficial in humans,74 although many researchers hope that fine- tuning exaggerated or unresolved inflammatory responses will be beneficial for preventing PNDs (reviewed in reference 55).

Disruption of the Blood–Brain Barrier ​

The BBB is a highly organized biological system that forms a physical barrier between the brain and the circulatory system. It is composed of endothelial cells connected by tight junctions, and it is ensheathed by pericytes and foot processes of astrocytes. Collectively, the tight junctions, astrocytes, and pericytes regulate the permeability of endothelial cells and the BBB. In animal models, an increase in proinflammatory factors in the peripheral circulation leads to increases in the permeability of the BBB, which allows inflammatory factors, including monocytes and cytokines, to access the brain parenchyma. Similarly, in humans, BBB dysfunction following surgery has been assessed in pilot studies using gadolinium- enhanced magnetic resonance imaging.75 BBB function can also be studied in humans by measuring the CSF to plasma ratio of albumin levels (referred to as Qalbumin). Normally, there is much less albumin in the CSF than in the plasma, so an increase in Qalbumin indicates BBB dysfunction. Qalbumin increases after major aortic and cardiac surgery. The release of brain-derived factors such as YKL- 40, S100β, and plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 into the plasma may also indicate increased permeability of the BBB.65

Immune cells from the periphery, including bone marrow–derived monocytes, can gain access to the brain via a leaky BBB, where they can release factors that further disrupt the integrity of the BBB. Further, macrophages and other leukocytes located in the circumventricular organs and perivascular structures express Toll- like receptors that trigger cytokine release, which can then enter the brain. The interplay between peripheral and central inflammation in PNDs is an area of intense research, and a great deal more about this topic is likely to be learned in the near fut ure.65,76,77

Modulation of Microglia and Astrocytes ​

Glia, which are also referred to as gliocytes, glial cells, and neuroglia, form most of brain and spinal cord parenchyma. These non- neuronal cells include microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and ependymal cells. In the peripheral nervous system, glia subtypes include Schwann cells and satellite cells. The glia serve multiple functions in the CNS, such as supplying nutrients, oxygen, trophic factors, and neurotransmitters to the neurons. They contribute to the structure of the parenchyma, insulate neurons from one another, and help to maintain the BBB in response to injury by destroying pathogens and damaged cells.

Microglia are resident phagocytes of the brain, where they account for 10%–15% of all cells. Microglia serve a range of functions, including supporting blood vessel and BBB integrity, neurogenesis, tissue repair, and synaptic remodeling. They also play key roles in surveillance of the surrounding area, and their cellular morphology (and that of their processes) can change dramatically in response to extracellular stimuli or threats. In response to activating factors, microglia can release soluble factors such as

cytokines, and they play important roles in phagocytosis of synapses in both normal development and disease states. Interestingly, studies have shown that microglial activation is necessary for the development of cognitive dysfunction following surgery in normal and obese mice.48,78 The most recent nomenclature refers to microglia as residing in either a homeostatic baseline state or a surveillance state of their local environment. Microglia respond to both local cues and peripheral signals, including those from the gastrointestinal tract via the gut–brain axis. Several studies have suggested that activation of microglia is necessary and sufficient to cause PND in animal models.48,79 Changes in microglial function lead to the robust production of inflammatory cytokines, including IL- 1β. For example, Kv1.3 potassium channels that are expressed in microglia have been proposed as drug targets. However, the responses of rodent and human microglia may differ.78 Early human studies using positron emission tomography (PET) imaging with the peripheral benzodiazepine receptor ligand [11 C] PBR28 (which is thought to preferentially bind to activated microglia) suggests that microglia exhibit a suppressed, rather than reactive, phenotype after surgery.80 Furthermore, no correlation has been found between microglial activation (as measured by [11 C]PBR28 PET) and delirium in older surgical patients.80 Overall, the utility of targeting microglia for preventing or treating human PNDs remains uncertain.

Astrocytes are the most abundant cells in the brain, contribute to the formation and maintenance of memory,81 and may also play a role in PND. Astrocytes form direct contact with neurons, where they regulate the homeostatic environment, synaptic structure, neurotransmission, and the function of neuronal networks. Astrocytes release soluble factors known as glio- transmitters, including γ- aminobutyric acid (GABA) and glutamate, as well as paracrine factors, such as the neurotrophin brain- derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF).82

Cross- talk between microglia, astrocytes, and neurons may contribute to both the onset and resolution of PNDs. For example, microglial activation in the hippocampus generates activated, potentially neurotoxic astrocytes, which mediate etomidate- induced cognitive deficits in animal models.83 Furthermore, astrocytes release GABA, which reduces neuronal excitability.84 Conversely, dexmedetomidine, which has been shown to reduce the incidence and duration of delirium in the intensive care unit (relative to benzodiazepine- based sedation),85,86 stimulates astrocytes to release neurotrophic factors (such as BDNF) that support normal neuronal function. The role of astrocytes in PNDs is an area of emerging interest (reviewed in reference 87).

General Anesthetic Drugs ​

Although the contribution of general anesthetic drugs to cognitive outcomes in human patients remains controversial, animal studies have more consistently shown that memory deficits can persist after drug exposure. A scoping review recently summarized articles that addressed the question of whether general anesthetic drugs cause cognitive deficits in adult animals that persist after the drugs have been eliminated from the brain.47 The review assessed articles indexed in the MEDLINE database from 1953 to 2021. Overall, 106 preclinical studies met the study

inclusion criteria, which included a single exposure to a general anesthetic drug (other than ketamine or nitrous oxide) and one or more assessments of cognitive behaviors at least 24 hours later. All of the included studies involved rodents, and 77% reported postanesthetic deficits in at least one cognitive domain. Deficits were detected in young adult and aged animals, but were more frequently reported in studies of older animals. Cognitive deficits were also more frequently reported in studies that involved longer anesthetic treatment durations, shorter postanesthetic recovery intervals, and administration of inhaled versus intravenous anesthetic drugs.

Several reasons might account for differences in cognitive outcomes after general anesthesia in humans and laboratory animals. For example, the timing of cognitive testing after anesthetic drug treatment often differs between preclinical and clinical studies. Also, certain cognitive domains such as hippocampal- dependent spatial memory might be particularly vulnerable to persistent anesthetic drug effects in animals, but may be less frequently assessed in human PND studies. Notably, cognitive deficits in one specific domain are often not generalizable to other domains.

The scoping review summarized above found that the quality of many preclinical studies of PND was lacking. Many studies included in the scoping review failed to use blinding, did not use randomization in assignment of animals to treatment groups, failed to report mortality, almost exclusively studied male animals, and rarely studied aged animals. Further, many PND animal model studies have not reported physiologic variables during anesthesia and surgery. This raises the question of whether effects seen in these PND animal model studies may be due to significant physiologic derangements, such as hypotension or hypothermia, rather than more general aspects of anesthesia and surgery that occur in humans. This is an important caveat to these studies, since avoiding blood pressure values below the individualized lower limit of cerebral autoregulation has been demonstrated to significantly reduce postoperative delirium rates in humans.88 Overall, to improve the overall quality of future animal PND model studies, recommendations for preclinical studies of postanesthesia outcomes have been proposed.48

In future preclinical studies, the effects of anesthetic drugs should be studied in a more realistic context, including assessment of the influence of cofactors such as inflammation and surgery. Several studies have shown that anesthetic drugs and surgery cause cognitive impairment in laboratory animals, but only when combined with neuroinflammation.89 For example, an intravenous anesthetic drug caused prolonged cognitive deficits, but only in mice that had been pretreated with the proinflammatory agent lipopolysaccharide.89 In contrast to the results summarized above, the contribution of different classes of drugs (inhalational vs. injected) to human PNDs has remained controversial. Several studies have examined whether propofol, compared with inhaled anesthetics, is associated with lower rates of delirium or improved postoperative cognitive trajectories, but these have produced mixed results.29,90–94b

One approach that has been used in clinical studies to assess the effects of anesthetic drugs on PNDs is to compare cognitive outcomes after regional and general anesthesia. Large prospective randomized trials, including the REGAIN

trial, showed no difference between general anesthesia and regional anesthesia with intravenous sedation in the incidence of delirium among older patients undergoing hip fracture repair.95 However, the intravenous anesthetic dosage in the non–general anesthetic arm of the REGAIN trial was not reported, and many centers typically provide “sedative” doses of propofol suitable for general anesthesia (i.e., up to and above 100 mcg/kg/min) to orthopedic surgery patients receiving regional anesthesia. Thus, although the REGAIN trial clearly shows no benefit of spinal anesthesia over general anesthesia when the spinal anesthesia is administered with intravenous sedation (i.e., the common current practice), it did not answer the question of whether avoiding CNS- active anesthetic drugs altogether would reduce the incidence of delirium or improve other outcomes. This latter question was addressed by the RAGA trial in China, in which patients undergoing hip fracture repair were randomly assigned to receive spinal anesthesia without sedation or general anesthesia.96 The study found no difference in rates of POD between the two groups. However, delirium rates in the RAGA trial were less than half those seen in REGAIN and many other North American, Australian, and European studies, raising questions about the generalizability of the RAGA trial outside of China. Nonetheless, overall, the RAGA and REGAIN trials together suggest that anesthetic drugs are unlikely to be major causal factors in the development of PND.

Aside from RAGA and REGAIN, other studies have addressed the general question of whether anesthetic drugs contribute to PND risk by examining whether EEG- guided anesthetic drug delivery reduces PND rates by allowing anesthesiologists to administer lower doses of anesthetic. These studies have yielded similarly mixed results. Two randomized controlled trials showed that titrating anesthetics to a bispectral EEG index (BIS) value of 40–60 resulted in significantly lower delirium rates.97,98 In contrast, in the ENGAGES trial, patients were randomly assigned to BIS target or standard care, and the incidence of delirium did not differ between the two groups, although the difference in anesthetic dosage between the two groups was only ∼0.1 MAC (minimum alveolar concentration) and unlikely to represent a clinically significant difference.99 More recently, the BALANCED anesthesia delirium substudy found a significant reduction in the incidence of delirium after major noncardiac surgery in patients randomized to a BIS target of 50 (light anesthesia) compared with a BIS target of 35 (deep anesthesia).100 In the BALANCED delirium substudy, the difference in anesthetic dose between groups was considerably greater than that seen in ENGAGES and represented a statistically and clinically significant difference. The BALANCED study results suggested that a greater depth of anesthesia associated with higher doses of anesthetic drug(s) influenced postoperative cognitive outcomes. However, all of the studies addressing this question have had limitations, and the contribution of anesthetic agent dosage to PNDs remains uncertain at this time.

Altered Inhibitory and Excitatory Neurotransmission ​

One potential mechanism underlying PNDs is a disruption of the delicate balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission, the so- called E/I balance, which is required for normal cognition. The major excitatory

neurotransmitter is glutamate, whereas GABA mediates most inhibitory neurotransmission in the brain. General anesthesia and surgical trauma may disrupt the E/I balance and thereby disrupt synaptic plasticity and impair learning and memory processes. Numerous studies have shown that anesthesia and surgery alter neuronal excitability and neurotransmitter release; more specifically, they increase inhibitory neurotransmission and disrupt long- term potentiation of excitatory synaptic transmission, which is thought to be a cellular correlate to memory.84,101–104

Excess activity of GABA receptors, of which GABA type A (GABAA) is the most prevalent type in the brain, may contribute to PNDs. Preclinical studies have shown that anesthetic drugs, surgery, and the administration of a proinflammatory factor, lipopolysaccharide, impair synaptic plasticity, possibly by increasing GABA- ergic inhibition.84,103–105 As an example, many anesthetic drugs and inflammation trigger sustained hyperactivity of α5 subunit–containing GABAA (α5- GABAA) receptors, which regulate memory processes.103,106 Drugs that preferentially reduce α5- GABAA receptor function (so- called negative allosteric modulators or NAMs) prevent memory deficits after general anesthesia and surgery in animal models.84,104,107,108 NAMs that preferentially inhibit α5- GABAA receptor–mediated function are being developed by large and small pharmaceutical companies as cognitive enhancing or “smart” drugs. These include L- 655,708, α5IA, Ro15- 4513, MRK- 016, RO4938581, and RY- 80.109–111 Drugs that inhibit α5- GABAA receptors are attractive, as they do not have convulsant or proconvulsant properties, they have good oral bioavailability, and they easily cross the BBB.110 Pharmacologically inhibiting α5- GABAA receptor–mediated tonic current enhances long- term potentiation, reverses anesthesiaand scopolamine- induced impairment of spatial working memory, and enhances performance on an object recognition memory assay.111 In studies involving human volunteers, these drugs have also been effective in the treatment of memory deficits after ethanol and depression, although NAMs were not effective in the treatment of cognitive deficits associated with Down syndrome.112–116

Future studies are also needed to determine whether α5- GABAA receptor activity increases in humans after surgery and whether NAMs mitigate postoperative cognitive deficits. Selective PET radioligands that preferentially target α5- GABAA receptors are available, such as [11 C]Ro15- 4513 and [3 H]L- 655,708 and the less selective PET ligand [11 C] flumazenil.115,117 One particular NAM, [11 C]Ro15- 4513, shows in vivo regional specificity and target engagement in humans.113 Studies to determine whether α5- GABAA receptor expression increases after anesthesia and surgery in patients, and whether negative allosteric modulators mitigate postoperative cognitive deficits will be of great interest. In support of the α5- GABAA receptor hypothesis of PND, cotreatment with the α2 adrenergic receptor agonist dexmedetomidine prevents overactivity of α5- GABAA receptors and also prevents cognitive deficits in animal models.118,119

Exposure to anesthetic drugs and surgery is also associated with a reduction in excitatory glutamate receptor function. In particular, the N - methyl- d- aspartate (NMDA) glutamate receptor subtype has been implicated in a variety of mood and cognitive disorders, including those

associated with aging.120–123 Anti- inflammatory interventions reduced NMDA receptor hypofunction and rescued deficits in spatial memory in animal models. Neuroinflammation triggered sustained downregulation of NR2A and NR2B subunits in the hippocampus and selective spatial memory deficits in older rats,124 and anti- inflammatory treatment reversed altered dendritic spine structural plasticity and ameliorated learning deficits. Thus, drugs that increase glutamate receptor expression and function may have utility in the treatment of PNDs.

Production of Neurotoxic Molecules Associated with Neurodegenerative Disorders ​

Increases in plasma neurofilament light chain and tau, two molecules associated with neuronal injury and neurodegenerative disorders, have been identified in human plasma following anesthesia/surgery.125 However, it remains unclear to what extent these plasma biomarker changes reflect de novo CNS injury versus either BBB dysfunction and increased efflux of these proteins from the CNS into the plasma, or peripheral production of these proteins, although a close correlation between plasma and CSF levels has been demonstrated.126a Other human studies have shown inconsistent results. Two studies have demonstrated postoperative increases in CSF tau levels following neurosurgery and/or ENT surgery that involved crossing the dura62,126b and some (but not all)58,61 studies have found increases in CSF and/or blood- based neurodegenerative disease biomarkers after major vascular127 and cardiac surgery.128,129 In contrast, a recent study in over 100 patients found no correlation between postoperative changes in cognition and postoperative changes in CSF Alzheimer disease–related biomarkers (including amyloid beta 1- 42, tau, and p- tau- 181p) at either 1 day after or 6 weeks after surgery.130 However, some studies have only examined CSF biomarkers one day after surgery, which may have been too early to observe any significant increases in biomarkers or associations of such increases with PNDs, since the peak rise in plasma neurofilament light (NFL) does not occur until 48 hours or later.125,131 Neuroimaging studies with PET ligands have not definitively shown a relationship between CNS amyloid pathologic changes and PNDs or an effect of surgery on brain amyloid deposition.132,133

Future Directions ​

Overall, mechanistic insights gained from both animal and human studies may help identify biomarkers for stratifying patients according to their individual/personalized PND risk, and which may serve as diagnostic and/or prognostic markers. Future research priorities of preclinical studies include developing standardized animal models to study cognitive deficits, such as delirium, after anesthesia and surgery. Studies that directly investigate the effect of different concentrations of intravenous and inhaled anesthetics, administered at equipotent concentrations and for similar durations, are also needed. Importantly, such studies should follow rigorous best practice guidelines designed to ensure that their findings will be relevant to the care of older surgical patients.48 Research is also needed to determine the role of neuroinflammation, and to identify pharmacologic strategies to prevent and treat PNDs. Robust evidence is needed to support novel interventions to reduce or prevent PNDs.

Finally, insights gained from the study of PNDs may inform investigations of other forms of cognitive impairment, such as traumatic and neurodegenerative brain injury, which share some similar contributing factors (such as neuroinflammation). Such insights may identify novel opportunities to treat and/or prevent both PNDs and other disorders.
