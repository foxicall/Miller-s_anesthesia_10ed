第 7 章 麻醉照護的倫理議題：重點精華總整理 ​

Ethical Aspects of Anesthesia Care

主題索引： 醫學倫理、知情同意、病人自主

本摘要由 Gemini 依本章原文整理，尚未經臨床專家審閱。引用頁碼為各章 PDF 頁序；用藥與處置請對照原文及院內現行規範。

1. 核心觀念精要 (Core Concepts) ​

  • 現代醫學倫理的四大支柱為尊重病人自主權、行善原則（Beneficence）、不傷害原則（Nonmaleficence）以及正義原則（Justice）。（原文第 2 頁）
  • 知情同意（Informed consent）與知情拒絕（Informed refusal）是尊重病人自主權的核心實踐。麻醉醫師有法律與倫理責任確保病人獲得充分資訊，此責任不可委託他人。（原文第 3 頁）
  • 醫療決策能力（Capacity）是功能性的，與特定任務相關，且不應僅因病人拒絕治療或存在精神疾病診斷而自動否定。即使在緊急情況下，若病人具備決策能力，其拒絕治療的權利仍應受到尊重。（原文第 3 頁、原文第 4 頁）
  • 醫師參與死刑執行被美國專業醫學組織視為不道德。美國麻醉專科醫師委員會（American Board of Anesthesiologists）明確指出，參與死刑執行可能導致醫師喪失專科認證資格。（原文第 1 頁、原文第 16 頁）

2. 臨床指引與處置決策 (Clinical Guidelines & Management) ​

術前評估與禁忌症 ​

  • 常規術前妊娠檢測並非醫學必要，且可能對病人造成嚴重的社會與經濟傷害。麻醉醫師應提供病人選擇權，解釋風險與效益，並取得知情同意或拒絕。（原文第 7 頁）
  • 術前給予鎮靜劑或止痛藥並不自動導致知情同意無效。對於因疼痛或焦慮而無法專注的病人，適度給藥反而可能改善其決策能力。（原文第 4 頁）

術中監測與藥物劑量建議 (Dosing / Monitoring) ​

  • 本章未提供可安全擷取的藥物劑量或術中監測建議，請核對原始圖表與相關臨床章節。

關鍵介入步驟 (Step-by-step Interventions) ​

  1. 若病人具備決策能力，其拒絕輸血或特定治療的權利應受尊重。若醫師因個人價值觀無法配合，應盡可能尋求替代照護者。（原文第 5 頁）
  2. DNAR（拒絕心肺復甦）指令在術中不應自動暫停。麻醉醫師應在術前與病人討論手術期間復甦的風險與效益，並記錄雙方達成的共識。（原文第 10 頁）
  3. 在臨終照護中，使用高劑量止痛藥或鎮靜劑以緩解痛苦（如呼吸困難、疼痛）在倫理與法律上是被允許的，即使其副作用可能加速死亡（雙重效應原則），但若目的是為了加速死亡則屬於安樂死，是不被允許的。（原文第 11 頁）

3. 重要併發症與危機處理 (Complications & Crisis Management) ​

  • 醫療錯誤發生時，醫師有倫理義務告知病人事實。研究顯示，誠實揭露錯誤能增進病人信任、改善醫病關係，且並未增加醫療訴訟風險。（原文第 9 頁）

4. 臨床重點口訣 / 高頻考點 (Key Takeaways / Board Exam Focus) ​

  • 醫療決策能力評估應採用「滑動量表」方式，僅在病人處於功能表現曲線最底端時才判定為無能力，而非採取全有全無的判斷。（原文第 3 頁）
  • 在處理母胎衝突時，醫師應尊重孕婦的自主權與價值觀，並在法律與倫理衝突時，優先考量專業倫理價值。（原文第 8 頁）
  • 對於心臟植入式電子裝置（如 ICD），在臨終照護階段應與病人討論是否停用，DNAR 指令不應被視為自動停用裝置的請求。（原文第 12 頁）

英文原文對照 / 原始內文 (Original Text) ​

JENNIFER LUCERO, CLARENCE BRADDOCK III, and GAIL VAN NORMAN

KEY POINTS ​

  • Medical ethics emphasize principles of respect for patient autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice.

  • Medical ethics principles also prioritize justice, equality, diversity, and inclusion (JEDI) in considering how to address specific ethical dilemmas.

  • Adult patients with decisional capacity have the right to consent to—and to refuse—medical therapy, even if such therapy would be lifesaving.

  • Minor patients have varying degrees of capacity, and have legal rights to make some medical decisions.

  • Routine preoperative pregnancy testing has potential for significant patient harm, is not medically indicated, and requires the patient’s informed consent.

  • The US Supreme Court ruling in Dobbs v. Jackson has had major impact on how individual states and the legal system address reproductive health issues and maternal–fetal conflicts, presenting physicians with dilemmas of whether to follow the law, or adhere to ethical practice.

  • Advance directives have both ethical and legal weight in allowing patients to express choices; Do Not Attempt Resuscitation (DNAR) orders should not be automatically suspended for anesthesia and/or surgery.

  • Medical care at end- of- life requires special medical knowledge and experience on the part of the physician; support and counseling of patients and families; understanding and respect for the patient’s autonomy; and respecting religious and cultural practices, and beliefs.

  • Research in human subjects balances the needs and rights of the research subject and the hypothetical interests of future patients, and can create physician conflicts of interest that must be acknowledged and addressed.

  • Advances in understanding animal cognition have led most biologists to believe that many, if not all, animals are capable of feeling pleasure, pain, anticipation, and fear – placing special obligations on the part of the researcher to reduce suffering.

  • US professional physician organizations consistently state that physician participation in executions is unethical, and the American Board of Anesthesiologists states that an anesthesiologist’s participation in executions may result in loss of board certification.

  • “Conscientious objection” by physicians to providing legally allowable medical care is ethically limited.

Introduction ​

Modern medical practitioners have tremendous power, recognized social import, and powerful financial interests that touch nearly everyone’s lives – and the medical profession is one guided by codes of behavior and defined rules of ethical conduct. The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) has established guidelines for the ethical practice of anesthesiology,1 and this chapter examines the underlying principles upon which to provide ethical anesthesia care.

Ethical Theory ​

UTILITARIANISM, DUTY- DRIVEN ETHICS, AND INCLUSIVE VALUES ​

Many different ethical frameworks are applied in modern medicine, and two of the most prominent frameworks relevant to Western medicine are utilitarian ethics and deontology.2

In utilitarian ethics , actions are judged right or wrong based on the balance of their good and bad consequences,

generally for populations rather than individual patients. A “right” action produces the most good, based on a perspective that gives equal weight to the interests of all affected parties. Utilitarian theory is compelling but falls short in defining which benefits are most important. Is it the “good” that all reasonable people want or the “good” defined by individual patients? What if the only way to maximize good is to commit an entirely immoral act? For example, what if the only way to prevent a terrorist attack is to torture children? Outcomes of actions accumulate over time – when on that continuum should it be determined that an action was right or wrong? The “good” of saving an individual’s life today may be viewed through a completely different lens when 20 years from now that same individual is revealed to be a mass murderer.

Utilitarian theory is best when applied to analyzing broad- based policies, in decisions regarding rationing of resources, and when attempting to resolve conflicting ethical obligations between several equally interested parties.

The premise of Kantian - based ethics (aka deontologic —or duty- based —theory) is that features of actions other than their consequences make them right or wrong. Intention is more important than outcome. Furthermore, no person should use another exclusively as a means to an end, because each person is the end for which we should act. Kantian philosophy would forbid killing one innocent person to save another innocent person, for example.

Individualism and autonomy are valued highly in Western society, and ethicists tend to turn to Kantian philosophy when questions arise that balance the authority of the physician against the goals and values of individual patients.

Some of the toughest ethical questions in medical practice occur when the rights and desires of individual patients conflict with society’s interests and policies. Clashes between deontologic and utilitarian principles are common in the intensive care unit (ICU), managed care settings, endof- life care, transplant medicine, triage during civilian mass casualty events, and in the care of poor and older patients whose medical management is funded by government funds, for example. In each, the will of the individual patient may conflict with broader principles of minimizing expense, fairly allocating scarce resources, protecting broader interests of patients in general, and determining how society’s health care dollars are spent.

Four basic principles form the “pillars” of Western medical ethics and are expected in the professional behavior of all physicians:

  1. Respect for patient autonomy

  2. Beneficence (doing “good”)

  3. Nonmaleficence (avoiding “harm”)

  4. Justice (equal treatment and equal distribution of resources)

PRINCIPLES OF JUSTICE, EQUALITY, DIVERSITY, AND INCLUSION (JEDI) ​

With wide recognition of health disparities and the impact of race and racism in medicine, the concept of justice in medical ethics has become more expansive than ever. There have been and continue to be profound health disparities within the United States – clearly demonstrated during the

recent COVID- 19 pandemic. African- American, Hispanic, and American Indian/Alaskan native people experienced higher rates of COVID- 19 infection and death compared to White people when considering age differences across racial and ethnic groups.3 Prior to the pandemic many medical institutions had already adopted principles of JEDI—promotion of justice, equity, diversity, and inclusion—as a core part of the ethical principle of justice. Since 2020 there has been more visible and widespread advocacy, with more intention toward addressing health disparities and the role health care providers play in acknowledging and reducing them. Such changes invite us all to recognize that characteristics such as race, culture, religion, and gender identity impact health and health care decisions, and are therefore ethically relevant.4

Historically, medicine has too often taken a front seat in inequitable care and unethical research based on racism, ableism, anti- LGBTQ+, and other discriminatory practices. Physicians and medical scientists led the eugenics movement of the 20th century, and carried out human experimental atrocities during World War II, for example. Some of these unfathomable practices continued until at least 2010 in the United States,5 where despite its illegality, forced or coerced sterilization was practiced on incarcerated women until at least 2010 – a practice that is not only discriminatory against the reproductive rights of women as a whole, but which also disproportionately targeted African- American, Indigenous, and Hispanic women.6,7 Laws providing both repudiation of the practice and victim reparations were only just passed in the state of California in the summer of 2021 and implemented in 2022.8

Such injustices are at the heart of mistrust of the health care system by individuals who belong to historically marginalized groups. To ethically practice medicine, physicians must understand the history of medical practices in the United States, structural racism, and the mechanism by which bias in the form of aversive racism affects patient care. One example of the changing practices which follow JEDI is resulting in adoption of nomenclature to represent a range of sexual and gender identity, and normalizing the appropriate use thereof. We are now at a critical transition point in medicine, with future physicians being educated on JEDI principles in school, while those already practicing are still striving to catch up to the rapid changes taking place in addressing these inequities.

Common Ethical Issues Confronting Anesthesiologists ​

INFORMED CONSENT AND INFORMED REFUSAL – RESPECT FOR PATIENT AUTONOMY ​

The political tradition of the United States provides a clear underpinning to individual freedom,2,9 and the most frequently encountered situations in which respect for the autonomy of patients is practiced, demonstrated, and documented are through informed consent and informed refusal of medical care. Informed consent/refusal serves many important interests in medical practice: it provides transparency, allows patients more control over their health and treatments, respects patients’ values, promotes trust

between the doctor and patient, and promotes integrity in medical research. Informed consent is required by law with only rare exceptions.

Legal and moral imperatives for informed consent are based on the ethical principle of respect for patient autonomy. Autonomy refers to the ability to choose without controlling interferences by others and without personal limitations that prevent meaningful choices, such as inadequate information or understanding. In 1914, the case of Schloendorff v Society of New York Hospital established that it was the right of every adult person with capacity to determine what can be done to their own body. In 1957, the term informed consent was first used in the case of Salgo v Trustees of Leland Stanford Hospital , which established that it is not sufficient for physicians simply to secure consent; they have a duty to inform patients about risks and benefits to treatment, in addition to alternative forms of treatment.

Respect for patient autonomy not only requires physicians to respect decisions made by patients with decision- making capacity, but also to promote autonomy by removing barriers to competent participation in decisions. Such obstacles range from incomplete or inaccurate information to reversible medical conditions that impair a patient’s ability to understand the information given to them.

Colleagues, physician assistants, residents, fellows, nurse anesthetists, and others can assist in the informed consent process, but the duty to assure that the patient is properly informed rests ethically and legally with the attending physician providing the therapy, and this responsibility cannot morally or legally be delegated.10–12 While consent for the surgical procedures must be obtained by the surgeon, the informed consent process related to anesthesia care must be obtained by the anesthesia provider.

Competence and Capacity ​

Autonomy to make medical decisions does not exist in the absence of capacity. In the United States, competence is a legal determination, and capacity describes the minimum necessary skills to participate in medical decisions.13 The general assumption by courts in the Unites States is that an adult (e.g., over age 18 years) is competent to make medical decisions unless proven otherwise, and a minor is incompetent to make medical decisions, unless proven otherwise. However, age alone is not a determinant of competence.

Capacity is the ability to reach reasonable conclusions based on rational thinking. Capacity is both relative and task specific. Patients may be able to understand and make decisions about medical issues while being unable to manage their financial affairs, for example. Capacity can also vary over time.

Agreement or disagreement with proposed treatment is not necessarily evidence of capacity nor lack thereof.14 A patient may disagree with physician recommendations due to personal or cultural values or beliefs. Patients may agree to, or at least not object to, treatment yet still not have the capacity to understand the treatment and its goals. Furthermore, patients are vulnerable and highly susceptible to coercion.

Impairments of capacity can be temporary or permanent. Examples include some mental illnesses, dementia, immaturity, anxiety, and pain. Medications can variously impede competence, or improve it, depending on their effects and

the context in which they are given. Older patients, patients suffering from mental impairment, and children are particularly vulnerable to having their participation in medical decisions inappropriately curtailed or denied because their capacity to participate is underestimated. Hearing loss, expressive aphasia, and other neurologic impairments can create false impressions that capacity is impaired. Many minors have the capacity to make medical decisions but may be mistakenly excluded from the decision- making process because of their age. Language barriers can present significant challenges to communication.

Prejudice and paternalism permeate physician behavior in the informed consent process. Studies have demonstrated that tests routinely employed by mental health care specialists in assessing patient capacity are more likely to classify a female or non- White patient as lacking capacity – suggesting that the tests, their application, and/or the examiners themselves carry implicit bias against females and non- Whites.15,16

Challenges to a patient’s competence frequently occur because the patient and the physician have a difference of opinion or values,13,17,18 yet the majority of patients referred for evaluations are found to have decision- making capacity.13 Such challenges may, intentionally or not, serve to justify the physician’s decision to dismiss rather than solve a dilemma regarding a difficult patient’s wishes. Treatment refusal may simply reflect a patient’s prioritization of other values (e.g., dignity, privacy, independence) above medical outcomes, and not represent a problem with capacity – or it might reflect manageable emotions and values that disproportionately affect a patient’s choices, but do not necessarily rule out capacity.

Functional capacity for decision- making must be judged separately from the perceived quality of the decision itself. Patients have the right to make “bad” decisions (i.e., decisions that the physician feels are less than optimal) if they have capacity and appropriate information.17 Otherwise, the physician would merely prevail whenever a disagreement occurred, and patient autonomy in medical decisionmaking would not exist.

Capacity is functional, and no medical diagnosis, other than a profound change in consciousness itself, is sufficient to determine the ability to make medical decisions,19 although some conditions such as dementia may justify further evaluation of capacity. Furthermore, evidence of impairment is not sufficient to prove incapacity. With such high value placed in the United States on autonomy and self- determination, incapacity is and should be a difficult bar to leap over, requiring that only persons with impairments “that place them at the very bottom of the performance curve” be found to not have capacity.20 A “sliding scale” approach to capacity determinations, rather than an all- or- none approach, is endorsed by a Presidential Commission,21 and generally reflects how courts deal with competency cases.20

Anesthesiologists frequently ask whether valid consent can be obtained after the patient has received premedication. Claims that medications automatically invalidate consent demonstrate a lack of understanding of the concept of capacity. In one study, 37.5% of geriatric patients presented for surgery having taken self- prescribed medications and over 25% of these were analgesics and benzodiazepines.22

If benzodiazepines and narcotics automatically invalidate consent, then the consent of almost all chronic pain patients would be invalid, and this study suggests that we would need to drug test all preoperative patients before obtaining consent.

Premedication can sometimes interfere with a patient’s capacity, but in some circumstances patients cannot give consent without premedication. A patient in severe pain, for example, may not be able to focus on detailed risks and alternatives of a proposed procedure without first receiving treatment for pain. In such cases studies have shown that administration of a narcotic can improve capacity to give informed consent.23,24 Similarly, anxiety can decrease concentration, understanding, and capacity to give informed consent25,26 and judicious administration of anxiolytic medications can improve capacity.

Multiple reviews by consultants who assess capacity agree that there are several functional elements to competence: (1) Can the patient receive and understand treatment- related information? (2) Does the patient have insight about the disorder and its consequences, the potential treatment options, and that treatment could be beneficial in some way? (3) Is the patient able to logically compare the risks and benefits of treatment alternatives? (4) Can the patient communicate a choice?13,20,21

Several approaches to assessing medical decision- making capacity have been suggested. The “cognitive approach” (the most widely used) focuses on whether a patient can understand and reason logically about their choice.27 However, cognitive abilities may be neither necessary nor sufficient for adequate decision- making. Some patients may not be able to argue logically about their decision, even though the decision is congruent with their lifestyle and goals. An “emotional” or “value- driven” approach includes more about the patient’s non- cognitive perceptions: passions, values, motives, beliefs, culture, and desires, all of which are essential to a person’s “inner life” and concept of self, and are intricately involved in patient perceptions of quality of life and health care goals.28

In respecting and promoting patient autonomy, the anesthesiologist has an ethical duty to ensure that reversible conditions that interfere with medical decisions are addressed when doing so will not delay urgent or emergent medical treatment. Elective surgery should be postponed until expert consultation for a determination of mental capacity or treatment of reversible conditions can occur. When surgery in an impaired patient is urgent, the anesthesiologist may have to rely on a surrogate decision maker or proceed with the best determination of the patient’s interests in mind. However, the mere presence of an emergency does not invalidate the rights of a patient who has capacity to consent to or to refuse treatment. Overriding such a patient’s wishes is unethical, even in an emergency.29

Disclosure ​

US courts generally rely on two standards of disclosure, termed the reasonable person (or objective person) standard and the subjective standard.30,31 In the reasonable person standard , the physician must disclose any information that a theoretically reasonable person would want to know, and the risks to which a reasonable person in the patient’s position would attach significance in deciding whether or not

to forgo the proposed therapy.30,31 This standard does not require an exhaustive recitation of facts and acknowledges that not all information related to the procedure is integral to making a decision about whether to consent. The subjective standard recognizes that individual patients may have special needs for specific information, and when that need is obvious or has been brought to the attention of the physician, the information must be disclosed. An opera singer may need to know that intubation may adversely affect the voice, for example. The anesthesiologist should always ask the patient if there are any special concerns regarding the anesthetic, or anything the patient wants the provider to know. In general, legal and ethical standards now require that physicians (1) accurately discuss the therapy and its potential alternative—including no therapy—and (2) disclose the common risks, because they are more likely to happen, and the serious risks, because the consequences are severe.

Therapeutic privilege is sometimes cited to avoid discussing risks, asserting that such disclosures can psychologically or physically harm the patient by increasing anxiety. Studies of patient anxiety during the informed consent process do not support the concept of significant harm, and often show that patient stress is generally reduced after risk discussions.32,33 Such omissions are contrary to US law. Although it is permissible to forgo or curtail risk discussions at the patient’s request, it is not ethical for the physician to decide to do so unilaterally. And even in situations where it might be legally allowed, therapeutic privilege can never be ethically justified on the basis that full disclosure might lead a patient with capacity to refuse treatment.

Coercion and Manipulation ​

The physician–patient relationship is inherently unequal because of the physician’s knowledge and authority and the patient’s dependence on the physician for information and care. Physicians have ethical obligations to avoid exploiting their influence for the purpose of accomplishing their own ends. Common methods of unethical influence in the informed consent process include coercion and manipulation.

Coercion refers to the presentation by an intentional agent (such as the doctor) of a real or implied threat of harm that the recipient believes and alters their behavior to avoid.2,9 “Threats” in health care, for example, can include both overt threats to refuse to provide all medical treatment to a patient who is refusing a specific therapy, or implied threats by suggesting through words or actions that the patient will lose the provider’s respect or best care. Manipulation involves the presentation of unbalanced or untruthful information to the patient – e.g., lies, deliberate omissions of key information, and over- emphasis or under- emphasis of information in order to sway the patient’s decision. Such intentional manipulations are morally equivalent to lying to the patient, are unethical, and invalidate the concept of informed consent entirely.34

Legal Implications of Informed Consent ​

Informed consent is cited in approximately 1% of closed claims cases, and cases with inadequate informed consent documentation are associated with larger monetary awards.35 Studies also show that the risk of a malpractice

claim is directly related to the patient’s perception of their relationship with the physician.36 The informed consent process provides one of the few opportunities anesthesiologists have to establish that relationship, however brief, and the medicolegal importance of that process should not be underestimated.

Informed Refusal ​

There is no “consent” if the informed, capable patient cannot refuse proposed medical therapy, because every informed consent process would then have to end with the physician getting their own way. Examples of informed refusals in anesthesiology include requests to withdraw or withhold life- supporting care in the ICU; Do Not Attempt Resuscitation (DNAR) orders in the operating room (OR); refusals of blood transfusions; and cases in which a patient refuses preoperative testing, such as pregnancy testing.

Informed refusal has concerns and requirements similar to those of informed consent. When patients refuse medical care or insist on what the physician believes to be suboptimal care, disclosure of the risks and benefits becomes even more important because these decisions may veer from widely accepted options for which the risks are believed to be lowest. It is easier to justify agreeing to the unusual preferences of a well- informed patient than to justify subjecting a poorly informed patient to unorthodox care.

Patients may sometimes request care that is unreasonable, either because it will adversely affect the performance of a surgical procedure or because it would be associated with unreasonably high risks. When a patient demands a technique that is inappropriate or outside of the realm of reasonable practice, the anesthesiologist is under no ethical obligation to provide that care. No physician can be compelled by a patient to practice negligently.

Special Issues in Informed Consent and Informed Refusal ​

Faith- Based Health Care Refusal. One example of a patient who refuses therapy in anesthesia practice on religious grounds is that of Jehovah’s Witnesses (JWs), many of whom believe that accepting a blood transfusion violates a Biblical injunction. While they are not the only group of patients to have religious objections to treatments, they serve as an instructive example of faith- based health care refusals, and principles learned from the JW example apply to informed consent in the setting of many different religious and cultural beliefs. The JW doctrine has changed over time, with resulting inconsistencies in religious practice regarding which, if any, blood components may be acceptable. Individuals interpret religious doctrines in light of their own spiritual contexts, and not all believers hold to the same tenets with identical fervor. Church doctrines, like medical practices, also evolve over time, and practices that are acceptable at one time may not be years later. Anesthesiologists and surgeons have cited individual and doctrinal inconsistencies to justify ignoring wishes of JW patients, but to do so is no more logical than assuming that every hypertensive patient needs or will respond well to identical treatment or that optimal treatments will not evolve over time. Moreover, any patient has the right to refuse blood transfusions, regardless of whether this desire is founded in any religious belief.

Because of differing beliefs among JW patients, the anesthesiologist must have a thorough and detailed preoperative discussion of possible therapies with the patient directly and not through surrogates when the patient is competent to make medical decisions, and conclusions should be documented in the patient’s chart. A physician who cannot comply with a competent adult patient’s desire to forgo transfusion has an ethical obligation to find an alternative caregiver whenever possible.37,38

Courts strongly support the rights of most adult patients to refuse blood products for themselves but have been inconsistent with regard to pregnant patients. Transfusion of pediatric JW patients by court order is common. However, this may become ethically and legally less acceptable as alternative bloodless therapies to maintain oxygencarrying capacity evolve, as the JW church further defines its doctrines with regard to children, and as the capacity of children to consent to or refuse therapy is better understood.

The Pediatric Patient, and Other Patients Without ​

Capacity. Children may or may not yet have the ability to make medical decisions. Laws in each state define the age at which children become legally competent to make medical decisions (usually 18 years), but many younger children have the mental and emotional capacity to make medical decisions. Forcing such individuals to undergo treatments they do not want is often unethical, and could be illegal as well.

Decision- making capacity in children is variable. Very young children are not generally able to make medical decisions, however, the range of capability in children as young as 7 or 8 years old is wide. In one study, children 6 through 9 years of age who were invited to participate in influenza vaccine research asked pertinent questions about individual risks and benefits and whether their community and other children would benefit.39 Studies show that the average 12- year- old has capabilities that are required in medical decision- making, but differing influences of the brain’s well- developed reward system coupled with underdevelopment of the brain’s control systems suggest that decisionmaking capacity may be diminished in specific contexts in this age group.40

Most states recognize “emancipated minor” status, in which a court determines that a minor can legally make medical decisions. Legal exceptions to the age of consent are recognized in most states when treatment is believed to be in the minor’s best interest and a requirement for parental consent would interfere with the child’s ability to receive medical help. The law recognizes that, tragically, some conditions for which a minor seeks therapy may even be the result of parental abuse and seeking parental permission for treatment may further endanger them. One quarter of pregnant teens are at risk for physical or sexual assault, and the most common perpetrator is a member of their family.41 Thus, minors are allowed in many states to consent for treatment for substance abuse, sexually transmitted disease, mental illness, and medical care affecting pregnancy, including abortion, without seeking parental consent. When a minor has decision- making capacity but is not “emancipated,” a judge may declare the child to be a “mature minor” with decision- making rights.

Ideally, individuals of any age should be involved in medical decisions to the degree that their capacity allows. Children ages 7 to 17 have been shown to desire comprehensive perioperative information, including details of the procedure and anesthesia, risks, and complications.42 A minor with decision- making capacity should not be coerced or restrained under most circumstances, particularly in non- emergencies.43 Determining whether a minor has such capacity may require formal consultation and assessment. The American Academy of Pediatrics has stated that physicians who care for children “should give serious consideration to each patient’s developing capacities for participating in decision- making, including rationality and autonomy.”44 The term assent rather than consent refers to agreement to treatment from children who do not automatically fall into legal categories, such as age, that award adult rights. Some experts suggest that informed consent rather than assent for medical research participation should be applied to children ages 12 and up.45

When children dissent from medical care, persistent refusals may be ethically binding, particularly in the case of clinical research. Medical personnel should respect the wishes of a patient who withholds assent and should try to gain a better understanding of the patient’s situation or deal with their fears. “A patient’s reluctance or refusal to assent should carry considerable weight when the proposed intervention is not essential to their welfare and/or can be deferred without substantial risk.”44

ETHICAL CHALLENGES OF PREOPERATIVE TESTING ​

Ethical implications of some medical tests, such as genetic testing, have been widely discussed in the literature, but ethical implications of routine medical testing have been largely overlooked. The performance of diagnostic tests has ethical dimensions, however. We generally undertake such tests precisely because we intend to help the patient (beneficence) or to use the information to minimize risks (nonmaleficence). Certain medical tests may carry implications for the patient’s autonomy, privacy, and even social justice.

Routine Preoperative Testing Protocols – General Principles ​

Preoperative testing may identify unrecognized or disguised conditions that could adversely affect anesthetic risk, but all such tests carry risks themselves. Falsepositive and false- negative results may label patients with a condition they do not have or inaccurately rule out a condition that they actually do. Erroneous results can lead either to further testing or to unnecessary therapy associated with further complications. Errors can cause patients to be deprived of important therapy they would otherwise receive. Tests sometimes have physical complications, and are certainly always associated with economic costs. Systematic over- testing increases costs of health care for entire populations, diverting critical funds to unnecessary enterprise. Medical tests can involve conflicts of interest if the ordering physician has an economic relationship with the entity that carries out the tests. Finally, not all medical tests are ethically equivalent. Certain tests, such as

pregnancy testing, genetic testing, and drug testing, can have complex legal and social consequences and cause serious, avoidable harms.

Modern medicine incorporates theories that are expected to be consistent and generalizable. Although all data are deeply theory laden, the practice of evidencebased medicine (EBM) is founded on the concept that conscientious, judicious, and explicit use of the best available medical evidence should be integrated with clinical experience and systematic research in making decisions about the medical care of individual patients. In general, nonsystematic clinical experience, anecdote, and untested theory are not sufficient grounds for clinical decision- making.

EBM and medical ethics share the common goals of maximizing benefits and minimizing risks, as well as involving the patient in shared decision- making. Many traditionally accepted therapies subjected to evidence- based review, such as administration of human albumin in treatment of shock46 and routine screening mammography,47 have been shown to have questionable benefits, and sometimes serious harms.

Using evidence- based principles of preoperative testing not only serves the ethical principles of beneficence and nonmaleficence, but also permits us to provide patients with accurate and up- to- date information on the potential benefits of tests to aid their understanding and input in their medical care, thus honoring the principle of respect for the patient’s autonomy.

EBM also presents ethical concerns. In relying on traditional medical experimentation, EBM may not sufficiently account for the role of social and cultural factors (e.g., poverty, ethnicity, spirituality, and gender) in health, and may rely too heavily on a narrowly drawn, biomedical model of the patient’s experience of health and disease. In the words of Rogers, “those with the greatest burden of ill health are disenfranchised, as there is little research that is relevant to them, there is poor access to treatments, and attention is diverted from activities that might have a much greater impact on their health.”48

Despite limitations, it nevertheless seems reasonable to believe that EBM provides at least an improvement in the search for a rational, cost- effective approach to medical testing over simply accepting “traditional” therapies or protocols without evidence that they will accomplish those goals. Applying medical tests inappropriately can cause concrete harm to patients. Simply stated, if medical care is not valid, then it is not ethical. At the same time, it is critical to assess current evidence in making decisions about medical care since standards of clinical practice evolve based on new findings.

Evidence that routine tests or traditional patterns of preoperative testing enhance perioperative outcomes is scant. Large population studies of many routine preoperative tests, such as coagulation screening,49 chest radiography,50 and electrocardiography,51 have been shown to increase costs without positively affecting outcomes, and to even lead to significant harms. The ASA Task Force for Preanesthesia Evaluation acknowledges that most routine testing is not necessary.52 When evidence- based algorithms for preoperative testing are available and have been

appropriately validated, they should be used to guide clinical decision- making.

Routine Preoperative Pregnancy Testing ​

Social risks of preoperative testing may not be as obvious to the physician as medical risks, but can be the source of significant harm. One example is preoperative pregnancy testing. Requiring pregnancy testing prior to anesthesia carries serious ethical implications and has limited, if any, beneficial impact in the setting of surgery, but can have serious social and economic consequences for the patient beyond the OR. It therefore requires the patient’s informed consent.

Despite pervasive beliefs to the contrary, studies do not conclusively demonstrate that anesthetic agents lead to early fetal loss, and no anesthetics have been clearly associated with teratogenic effects.53–55 Legal ramifications of not routinely testing for pregnancy preoperatively have been virtually nonexistent, and fewer than one-third of practices in the United States were found to require such testing.56 A positive pregnancy test may have profoundly negative consequences for a vulnerable patient in a social situation where pregnancy is not accepted. Up to two- thirds of sexual assault victims are minors, and some are the result of child rape within the home. Abandonment, negative family interactions, and violence to the patient or their fetus, may all occur as reactions to revelation of the pregnancy, attempts to hide a sexual domestic crime, or even in some cases as “honor killings”57 (a phenomenon that, contrary to popular portrayal, occurs across all race and cultural lines58 ). Adolescent victims of homicide are three times as likely to be pregnant compared to adults, and homicide against pregnant persons of all ages is double that of nonpregnant persons.59 When unexpected pregnancy is discovered, a referral of pregnant adolescents to child protective services may be required in some states. In many states, the law requires strict confidentiality of pregnancy status regardless of age. Therefore, the anesthesiologist who discovers an adolescent patient’s pregnancy has few comfortable or legal options.

Patients may choose not to undergo an elective surgical procedure if they know they are pregnant, but many other patients will avoid even necessary medical care if a pregnancy test is required.60 Culture can influence a patient’s desire to know about their pregnancy status, and affect medical decision- making. Coercing a patient to have a test against their wishes explicitly violates the principle of the patient’s autonomy.

Anesthesiologists who do not require patients to undergo pregnancy testing prior to anesthesia have not experienced heightened medicolegal risk. In 2021, the ASA closed claims database included just ten malpractice cases related to undetected pregnancies out of 10,500 claims. Successful claims occurred in three cases – in each one the anesthesiologist ordered a pregnancy test and then failed to check the results.61 The ASA Committee on Quality Management concluded that the medicolegal risk from preoperative pregnancy testing is greater from failure to check the results or failure to document disclosure of the risk of miscarriage after elective surgery, than from not performing a pregnancy test.61 The ASA Task Force on Preoperative Testing and the ASA Committee on Ethics jointly recommended that anesthesiologists offer the choice of preoperative pregnancy

testing to any “female” patient who may desire it, explain the potential risks and benefits, and obtain informed consent or refusal for the test.52 We affirm that, ethically, anesthesiologists should offer preoperative pregnancy testing to any appropriate patient in whom pregnancy is possible, which may include transgender men.

Ethics of Anesthesia Involving Pregnant Patients ​

INCLUSIVE LANGUAGE ​

The language health care providers use to discuss pregnancy and reproductive rights is changing to accommodate the shifting understanding of sexual and gender identity and roles. Up to 30% of transgender men experience unplanned pregnancies,62 and in the United States it is estimated that up to 530 trans and nonbinary people have undergone abortions.63 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and other medical entities are increasingly opting for gender- neutral terms, such as “pregnant people” and “birthing parent” over use of the term “women” when discussing pregnancy, fertility, abortion, and maternal– fetal conflicts. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists endorses a policy of inclusive language for all patient interactions, and encourages physicians to transition to inclusive language in publications and communications.64 These efforts acknowledge and respect the changing landscape of reproductive health care for all persons.

Gender- neutral terms in the setting of pregnancy and birth are not yet widely accepted, and may be awkward for many providers until they become fully transitioned into common usage, but the authors acknowledge and emphasize that the binary terms female and male are not inclusive. Because medical research and legal writings are still expressed in binary terms, for the purposes of the following sections we will use binary language when resources refer to such writings for the sake of clarity. Ethically, however, transitioning to more inclusive language is an obligation of physicians, since it reflects a core ethical value of respect for persons.

MATERNAL–FETAL CONFLICTS ​

Until June of 2022, the rights of pregnant patients to refuse therapy, even if it would be detrimental to their fetuses, were generally protected under right- to- privacy provisions in the US Constitution, and a pregnant patient’s rights were weighed against potential harms to the fetus in a decremental fashion as the fetus approached and surpassed viable age. When the fetus was of nonviable age, the mother’s rights usually prevailed. Court decisions consistently supported the rights of pregnant women to have abortions (when allowed by the laws of the state), to not be subjected to drug testing, and to forgo transfusions early in pregnancy. Attempts to charge women with child abuse, child endangerment, drug trafficking, murder, and attempted murder for activities during pregnancy that are deemed dangerous to their fetuses are decried, and have been shown to place unequal burdens on the poor, medically indigent, and minorities.65

In 2022 the US Supreme Court ruling in Dobbs v. Jackson returned determinations regarding abortion rights, including relevance of fetal viability, to the states. As a result, the relevance of fetal viability regarding the law and women’s reproductive and other health care rights depends on the specific state in which the patient resides or is seeking medical care, and is not consistent across the United States. This ruling presents serious challenges for health care providers, when ethically upholding patient choices comes into direct conflict with legal statutes regarding fetal “rights” and protections.

More than 75 health care organizations released a joint statement after Dobbs opposing legislative interference in medical decision- making and the patient–clinician relationship, and reaffirming the primary values of medicine in dedication to patient care and public health. The challenge of the recent ruling will impact clinicians who are considering the impact on their patients of continuing the pregnancy, including patients in the ICU, who are experiencing medical emergencies, or whose lives and health are at stake as a direct result of the impact of their pregnancy. In labor and delivery units across the country postpartum tubal ligations have not been prioritized even in the most high- risk patients who have been advised to not become pregnant again, given their complex high- risk medical disease. Lack of resources is cited as a reason for only 50% of the requested postpartum tubal ligations being completed, and with the Dobbs ruling those resources will become even more limited, given an expected increase in pregnancies. In resource- limited areas, it will be critical to place safeguards to prevent ethical lapses on the part of health care providers that impact patients with limited resources and who are from historically marginalized communities – these have been the communities who experience more breeches of ethics in areas with resource- limited care. Maternal mortality is expected by some to increase as a result of abortion bans by as much as 21% overall, but the racial disparity is predicted to be 13% among White versus 33% among Black patients.66,67

Professional societies have guidelines that address the ethical (versus the legal) treatment of patients when maternal–fetal conflicts arise. The American Academy of Pediatrics Committee on Ethics outlined conditions that in their view are necessary to override a pregnant patient’s refusal of care: (1) the fetus will suffer irrevocable harm without the treatment; (2) the treatment is clearly indicated and likely to be effective; and (3) the risk to the pregnant patient is low.68 The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists condemned the use of coercion in pregnant patients and advocated counseling the patient carefully about risks, as well as consultation with an ethics committee.69

It is clear that while the impact of the Dobbs ruling plays out in the states and the courts, health care providers, including anesthesiologists, will face situations in which adherence to traditional ethical values of the medical profession—such as respect for persons, and for patients’ values and choices—will force the provider to choose between ethical obligations of physicians and the law. But recent history provides stark lessons about the costs to patients and society of systematically sacrificing those medical professional values,70 as well as the benefits of insisting that

they be upheld in the face of “unhappy consequences” and pressure from colleagues.71

INFORMED CONSENT IN LABORING PATIENTS ​

Anesthesiologists periodically ask whether laboring patients are able to consider and weigh the risks of labor analgesia sufficiently while they are in pain. Although ideal conditions for informed consent are often lacking during labor, the ideal is seldom achieved even in elective surgical patients; therefore, it is crucial to distinguish between conditions that are not ideal from conditions that are not adequate.

Studies show that laboring patients believe they are capable of consent during labor,72 that they recall information presented during labor as well as other patients and long after labor is finished, that they have the same capacity to give informed consent as the general surgical population, and that labor did not alter their decision- making.73,74 Some experts argue that it is only after the patient enters active labor and can assess for themself the severity of pain and consequences of proceeding without analgesia that they can be fully “informed” and contextualize their decision.75

Ethical conflicts can arise in the cases of so- called Ulysses directives in which, before labor, a pregnant patient executes an advance directive that both refuses epidural analgesia and instructs doctors to ignore her pleas for epidural anesthesia at the time of labor should she change her mind. Some experts suggest that ignoring the Ulysses directive disrespects a pregnant patient’s long- term preferences, but others argue that “information and valid experience are critical prerequisites for autonomous decision- making” and that only the current wish (for an epidural) is ethically relevant.76 Such cases appear to have no clear and unequivocal ethical ground on which to proceed, and circumstances must guide the physician. However, if an anesthesia practice decides to routinely disregard Ulysses directives, this should be disclosed to patients in advance of labor whenever possible.

Truth Telling – Disclosure of Errors and Apology ​

The principle of nonmaleficence in medicine draws no distinction between deliberate and unintended harms. Medical care is fraught with uncertainty, risk, and error. Harms resulting from unexpected complications, accidents, systems issues, and medical mistakes are to be avoided with equal diligence whenever possible.

Seventy- six percent of physicians admit to a serious medical error that they did not disclose to the patient,77 and 22% of physicians indicate that they would not disclose an error that caused a patient’s death.78 Reasons cited for failure to disclose included personal shame, fear of loss of prestige, fear of direct reprisal, lack of experience in disclosing uncomfortable information, fear of causing further harm (emotional or psychological) to the patient and family, and fear of litigation. In many cases, legal advice discouraged disclosure and apology in the erroneous belief that these tactics decrease medicolegal liability.

Obfuscating the root cause of a patient’s complication when it is a medical error is not difficult, and a minimum standard of disclosure does not exist. The Code of Ethics of the American Medical Association (AMA) states that patients have a right to be free of misconceptions about their medical condition and that physicians have ethical obligations to “inform the patient of all the facts necessary to ensure understanding of what occurred.”79 However, the Code addresses “harms” and not “errors,” implying there is no duty to disclose medical errors that do not cause harm. It is arguable, however, that they should do so. The physician has little to lose, and such disclosures may enhance discussion of medical care with the patient, resulting in a strengthened doctor–patient relationship. From a legal perspective, some experts consider full disclosure of medical errors to be an extension of the legal principle underlying informed consent: “Clearly, if the patient is entitled to know the risks of a procedure and what could go wrong prior to giving their consent, it follows that they would be entitled to know if something has in fact gone wrong, regardless of whether it was unanticipated.”78

By disclosing mistakes that harm patients, providers release the patient from misconceptions about their medical past and enhance their ability to make decisions about their medical care. Disclosure prevents mistaken attribution by the patient of adverse consequences to noncontributory causes. Disclosure is usually necessary for informed consent for treatments to address complications of the error. Disclosure may enhance patients’ trust in physicians and facilitate a patient’s obtaining just and fair compensation for economic consequences of the injury, such as lost work and pay.

Concerns that disclosures will increase litigation or decrease patients’ trust, either in the doctor involved or in doctors in general, has not been borne out. Studies suggest that disclosure of a medical error reduces the likelihood that patients will change doctors, improves patients’ satisfaction, increases trust in the physician, and leads to a more positive emotional response.80 Studies show that patients take legal action because they want more honesty from their physicians and assurances that the physician has learned from the mistake so that future patients therefore are less likely to suffer.81

An often- ignored aspect of medical errors is the effect they have on physicians and other health care workers, who experience anxiety, fear, guilt, shame, self- doubt, anger, and disappointment. Damage to physicians, nurses, and others can be long- lasting and severe, particularly following serious errors, manifesting as substance abuse and even suicide. In a survey of anesthesiologists, 84% had been involved in at least one unanticipated death or serious injury, 88% stated they needed extended time to recover, and 19% stated they never fully recovered after the event. Two- thirds felt their ability to provide care was compromised in the first four hours following the event, but only 7% were relieved of duty to recuperate. Five percent admitted to using drugs or alcohol to cope.82 Most anesthesiologists report inadequate support from colleagues or their institution following such events.83

Physicians benefit from a sense of relief after disclosure and, in many cases, the forgiveness of the patient.84 Disclosure helps physicians learn and improve their practice.

Failures to report errors, learn from errors, and communicate errors and their potential solutions within a health care system are major causes of medical errors themselves. One could argue that a physician who does not disclose a preventable error, such that it is repeated, bears responsibility for harm to all future patients in whom the error could have been prevented had it been disclosed.

Legally, some North American courts have held that physicians do not have a duty to disclose the errors of others,78 and social norms about “tattling” on others are powerful deterrents. A reporting physician may hesitate because of a lack of definitive information, potential accusations of interfering in the doctor–patient relationship of another, worry that professional interactions such as patient referrals and performance evaluations may be affected, and fear of libel suits. When a physician observes a medical error committed by another physician, the options include nondisclosure, recommendations to the involved physician to disclose the error, disclosure of the error to a third party such as a risk- management group, or direct disclosure to the patient. Although no strict legal guidelines are in place, ethical principles favor actions that lead the patient to have a full understanding of what has occurred during their medical care.

Apology remains a controversial aspect of communication following medical errors, out of fear that it could be interpreted as an admission of negligence in subsequent litigation. A provider can express concern that a complication occurred as opposed to taking responsibility for causing the complication. In either case, apology appears to decrease the risk of subsequent litigation, and lack of apology is a commonly cited reason among malpractice plaintiffs for their legal action. Many states have enacted “apology laws” that prohibit use of various types of apologies in court against physician defendants in malpractice cases.85

Advance Directives and Surrogate Decision Makers ​

Critical health care decisions often arise when patients are too ill to formulate or express decisions about their care. Advance directives were developed when several legal precedents affirmed that patients can refuse even lifesaving medical care and that allowed surrogate decision makers to request withdrawal of life- sustaining therapies on behalf of patients.86 An advance directive is a document executed by the patient before they become unable to make decisions about their care to provide the patient’s physicians with guidance when the patient has lost capacity or cannot communicate. The advance directive allows the patient to clarify preferences regarding medical interventions.

A surrogate decision maker is someone whom the patient has appointed to make health care decisions for him or her (e.g. a durable power of attorney [DPOA]) for health care, or an individual with other legally recognized authority by virtue of their relationship with the patient. A DPOA need not be a family member, and the authority vested in a DPOA supersedes most other decision makers, including family members, except a court- appointed guardian.

When the patient has not designated a DPOA, doctors rely on family members to make decisions for the patient.

Many states legally define the hierarchy of family decision makers. Commonly, the surrogate hierarchy is the spouse or legally recognized domestic partner followed by the children, parents, and then siblings. Anesthesiologists should familiarize themselves with the specific laws of the state in which they practice.

Surrogate decision makers are explicitly trusted to act in “substituted judgment” to state what the patient would have wanted, and should not merely express their own preferences. However, surrogate decision makers at best only approximate the patient’s decisions because their interpretation is subject to their own biases, values, and psychological agendas. Incapacitated patients can be emotionally and financially burdensome, and decision makers may have conflicts of interest that distort their beliefs and testimony about what the patient would have wanted.

Patients and their proxies only infrequently discuss decisions involving life- sustaining technologies and neither physicians nor proxies consistently accurately predict the patient’s preferences for life- sustaining therapies.87,88 Often the specific situation and its potential impact on quality of life or other issues impact an individual patient’s decisionmaking. These situations make it challenging for a surrogate to consider all options in representing the wishes of a patient who no longer has decision- making capacity. Nevertheless, proxy decision- making may be the only option if a patient has not left specific directives.

MEDICAL DECISIONS THAT REQUIRE A COURT ORDER ​

Some medical treatments have intense cultural connotations, may involve limitation on private freedoms such as reproduction, or may have historically been subject to abuse. Decisions regarding these interventions legally cannot be made by a surrogate decision maker (even if one is available) and require court review. Examples of such treatments in many states include sterilization and electroshock therapy.

The anesthesiologist should review the patient’s record before anesthetizing them to ascertain (1) whether the patient has advance directives, (2) who the patient’s surrogate decision makers are, (3) whether the procedure is legally allowed with consent from a surrogate decision maker or instead requires a court order, and (4) in the latter case, whether an appropriate court order has been obtained.

DO NOT ATTEMPT RESUSCITATION ORDERS IN THE OPERATING ROOM ​

Up to 60% of anesthesiologists erroneously believe that DNAR can be automatically suspended during anesthesia and surgery. The ASA,37 the American College of Surgeons,89 the Association of Operating Room Nurses,90 and The Joint Commission91 all have practice guidelines or standards requiring reconsideration, not abandonment, of the DNAR orders in the perioperative period.

The causes and outcomes expected with a cardiac arrest in the OR are different from what may happen elsewhere, although they are still poor, with a “viable” survival of only about 25%, and a poor quality of life in many survivors.92 It is important that patients be informed of the risks and

benefits of intraoperative resuscitation so that they can best determine whether to ask for their DNAR order to be suspended in the perioperative period.

Although the patient’s primary care physician may have introduced the idea of DNAR orders to the patient or the surrogate decision maker, the anesthesiologist has a duty to discuss the risks and benefits of resuscitation in the setting of anesthesia and surgery before undertaking the patient’s care. The discussion should include the following steps: (1) determine the patient’s goals regarding surgery and resuscitation; (2) establish exactly what is meant by “resuscitation,” in contrast to routine anesthetic care; (3) educate the patient about the risks and benefits of resuscitation in the OR setting; and (4) document the agreements reached with the patient about which interventions commonly associated with resuscitation are acceptable to them. Such interventions could include, but are not limited to, intubation, administration of vasoactive drugs, administration of direct current countershock, and institution of chest compressions. Many patients who express reluctance about resuscitation during surgery actually fear more the burdensome collateral outcomes, such as permanent neurologic impairment, rather than resuscitation itself. Education and discussion may reassure patients about outcomes of intraoperative resuscitation and can establish guidelines for discontinuing interventions postoperatively if chances of meaningful recovery are minimal.

Resuscitation agreements must be discussed with other members of the OR team, including the surgeon, nursing staff, and anesthesia care team. In some cases, the decision to withhold resuscitation during a surgical procedure will have impact on the surgical plan of care that requires further consideration. This communication prevents crucial disagreements from occurring during a critical event when treatment decisions must be made quickly. It also allows “conscientious objectors” to withdraw from the health care team.

Advance directives are legally and ethically binding. Despite clear and consistent legal decisions regarding DNAR orders, physicians have continued to hold the false assumption that the legal authority of advance directives and living wills is not binding in the OR, or that the physician has discretion in deciding to ignore such directives. Families have received significant monetary damages for the costs of continued health care and punitive damages for the pain, suffering, and mental anguish of the patients’ survivors when DNAR instructions are ignored.93,94

In 1990, the US Congress passed the Patient SelfDetermination Act recognizing a competent patient’s right to refuse any medical therapy, including life- sustaining therapy, and requiring medical institutions and providers to advise patients of these rights and ask about their preferences. Compliance with the act is required in order to participate in Medicare and Medicaid reimbursements, and in 2006 a group of orthopedic surgeons was successfully sued for requiring patients to rescind their DNAR as a requirement of surgery.95

Finally, DNAR orders must never be construed as an excuse to not “care ” for the patient. A patient’s decision to forgo resuscitation does not imply a wish to avoid other beneficial interventions. Placement of a pulmonary artery catheter, for example, may help ensure optimal management of

a frail patient who has a DNAR order and enable the anesthesiologist to avoid situations in which the patient’s DNAR status becomes pivotal.

END- OF- LIFE DECISION- MAKING ​

Reviews have shown that the following end- of- life issues are of foremost importance to patients: control over the timing and location of death; management of symptoms such as pain, dyspnea, anxiety, and depression; financial management of medical care; and maintenance of therapeutic options, including physician- assisted suicide (PAS).96

Withdrawal or Withholding of Medical Therapies ​

Before the mid- 20th century, the concept of beneficence in the eyes of physicians was strongly tied to preventing death. Ethical distinctions between acts of omission (“letting die”) and acts of commission (“killing”) were and remain confusing at best. Worse, physicians faced threats of criminal punishment if a patient’s death resulted from withdrawal of medical treatments.97,98 In 1976, the case of Karen Ann Quinlan established that patients have a right to forgo invasive treatments, even if lifesaving, and that surrogate decision makers could ask for withdrawal of lifesaving therapies if the surrogates could show that the patient would not have wanted them. The right to forgo lifesaving treatments was later extended in the cases of Claire Convoy and Nancy Cruzan to include any treatment if the patient refused it or if clear and convincing evidence indicated that the patient would have refused therapy if he or she could speak for himself or herself.86 These decisions were revisited and reconfirmed in the case of Theresa Schiavo in 2005.

Arguments that withdrawing/withholding life- sustaining therapy does not violate rules against killing patients are based on ethical differences between killing and letting die, and between acts of commission (e.g., lethal injection) and omission (e.g., withdrawal or withholding of ventilator therapy).99 Because such distinctions are confusing to both physicians and patients, a principle of proportionality is often applied when withholding or withdrawing treatments. In this principle, treatment is indicated to the extent that it is likely to present more benefits than burdens to the patient based on the patient’s perception of such medical, social, and psychological benefits and burdens. Of course, patients with decision- making capacity still always have the right to refuse therapy, even if lifesaving and otherwise indicated.

Two settings in which anesthesiologists may be involved in withdrawing/withholding life- sustaining interventions are the ICU, and the OR before organ donation after cardiac death (DCD). In both cases, the issues and principles of withdrawing or withholding treatments are the same.

Withdrawal of life- sustaining interventions heralds the final phase of end- of- life care, and it is a socially profound phase in the life of the patient and family. Terminal care requires special knowledge, experience, and expertise in medically supportive therapy, management of problematic symptoms, physiologic changes in the dying patient, support and counseling of patients and families, as well as understanding and respect for the patient’s autonomy and religious and cultural practices and beliefs, the ability to

work within complex health care teams, the ability to communicate well, and empathy. Anyone involved in care of the dying patient should be intimately familiar with ethical and legal standards.

Withdrawal of life- sustaining treatment begins with assessment of the individual patient’s physiology, level of dependence on therapy, degree of consciousness, preferences regarding sedation and analgesia, and preferences regarding privacy and level of involvement of family and other loved ones. Treatments promoting the patient’s comfort should generally be continued, whereas those directed only at physiologic maintenance may all be withdrawn. Family members and others at the bedside should be educated about physical and mental changes they can expect to see as treatments are scaled back, including the possibility that death may not occur imminently once support is withdrawn.99

Several interventions have ethical implications and deserve special consideration: fluid and nutritional management; the administration of sedatives and narcotics that have the potential to hasten death; the administration of neuromuscular blocking drugs; and deactivation of pacemakers, ventricular assist devices, and implantable cardioverter–defibrillators (ICDs).

Burdens associated with continuation of fluid and nutritional support include prolongation of the dying process, and complications and suffering from placement and maintenance of intravenous or enteral access (or both). However, feeding and hydration may have important connotations for the patient, family members, and members of the health care team by allowing a sense of nurturing and by mitigating feelings that they are “abandoning” the patient.99

Pain, dyspnea, and depression are all common symptoms that cause suffering in the dying patient. Alleviating pain and dyspnea carry the risk of hastening death. Medical, legal, and religious authorities accept the principle of “double effect,” in which an action intended to produce a benefit for the patient unintentionally produces significant harm. It is ethical and legal to administer high doses of pain medication and sedatives with the intent to relieve symptoms, even if they have the unintended side effect of hastening death. However, to administer medication intending to hasten death is euthanasia, and not medical therapy.99 Orders for sedatives and analgesics should specify the clinical goals.

Terminal Sedation ​

“Terminal sedation,” or “deep continuous sedation” (DCS), is an ethically controversial end- of- life strategy for patients suffering intolerable symptoms in the last stages of dying, who do not respond adequately to sedatives or pain medications that are being administered “as needed.” With DCS, a decision is made by the patient or their surrogate to forgo reactive treatment of symptoms and to employ intravenous sedative/analgesic infusions to render the patient unconscious until death occurs, but without the intention of causing death itself. While the intention of DCS—relief of suffering—is a laudable one, ethical questions nevertheless persist: the practice is plagued by vague, non- standardized terminology and practice, a lack of outcomes research, an uncomfortable confusion with PAS and euthanasia, misunderstanding of the principle

of double effect, and culturally diverse philosophies about the role of suffering and transcendence in the meaning of human life.100 Opponents argue DCS is merely euthanasia in disguise, to circumvent legal sanctions and moral objections. Some ethicists argue that DCS permanently deprives the patient of morally relevant aspects of “personhood” and therefore represents a form of killing. Disturbing studies show that many physicians do employ DCS with the intention to hasten death.101,102

A particularly controversial aspect of DCS is whether it can and should be allowed when the patient is suffering from severe and intractable existential suffering: e.g., fear, loneliness, anxiety, or spiritual crises. Professional guidelines for DCS in adult patients for existential suffering are not consistent.103–108 Even more controversy arises when DCS is instituted in pediatric patients.105

Neuromuscular Blocking Drugs ​

Neuromuscular blocking drugs have no anesthetic, analgesic, or sedative properties, and should not be initiated if withdrawal of ventilatory support is anticipated. Paralyzing the patient to comfort the family so that they see no disturbing movements or respirations as the patient dies is not justifiable. Worse, it can mask symptoms and signs of distress and thus prevent relief of suffering during the dying process.99 When withdrawal of ventilator support is anticipated in a patient already receiving such drugs, such agents should be withheld in all but extraordinary cases.109,110

Implantable Cardiac Devices ​

When a patient who has a cardiac device requests withdrawal of life- sustaining interventions, questions arise about whether it is ethical to deactivate or explant such devices at the patient’s request. In the case of ICDs, the question might well be whether it is ethical to continue such therapy in a patient who has opted for palliative care. In the MADIT II trial, only 15% of patients who died had their ICD deactivated, and received “many” shocks at the last days of their lives.111 Interrogation of ICDs postmortem shows that up to 31% of patients receive shocks in the last 24 hours of life.112 Such patients may have been aware and unable to ask to have it stopped. Only 27% of patients discuss deactivation of their ICD at end- of- life, raising concerns that physicians fail to consider it.113,114

The ethical distinction between disabling a pacemaker in a pacer- dependent patient and turning off a ventilator for a ventilator- dependent patient is minimal if the request comes from a patient with capacity, or their surrogate decision maker. Both actions involve discontinuance of artificial therapies that the patient no longer desires, and both may be followed by rapid death.

Consensus statements and guidelines by the multiple physician professional organizations recommend discussion with the patient of deactivation of implantable cardiac devices in end- of- life care.115–117

The presence of a DNAR order should not be taken as an automatic request by the patient to discontinue implantable device therapy. As with other end- of- life therapies, discontinuance of such devices should include due consideration of whether the decision is made by a patient who has capacity and with full informed consent. Management of discontinuance of device therapy should include planning for

treatment of distressing symptoms and administration of appropriate comfort measures.

Physician- Assisted Suicide and Euthanasia ​

Physician- assisted suicide (PAS) is defined as provision of medication or prescriptions to a patient at their specific request for the explicit purpose of ending their life. PAS requires a patient who has capacity, can communicate the request, and is capable of self- administration. Euthanasia is defined as the administration of medication by someone other than the patient for the express purpose of causing death, with or without specific request of the patient. Both practices differ ethically from withdrawing/withholding life- supporting medical treatments. In withdrawal/withholding of life- supporting treatment, the primary intention is to discontinue treatments that are causing suffering, with an understanding that death may or probably will result. In PAS and euthanasia, the primary intention is to cause death, which secondarily ends suffering.

As of 2022, euthanasia is legal in the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Columbia, New Zealand, Spain, and six states in Australia (not to be enacted officially in three until 2023). In the United States, euthanasia is illegal regardless of circumstance, but PAS was legal as of 2021 in the states of California, Colorado, Hawaii, Maine, Montana, New Jersey, New Mexico, Oregon, Washington, and the District of Columbia.

Supporters of PAS argue that the right to privacy and respect for autonomy support a patient’s right to determine the time, location, and circumstance of his or her own death. Patients consistently rate loss of autonomy and control, inability to pursue valued activities, and loss of dignity as the major concerns at the end of life. Adequate control of pain, anxiety, dyspnea, and other symptoms at the end of life remain a challenge to the medical profession, fueling the desire for a means to end life when suffering cannot be controlled. Opponents argue that PAS “medicalizes” death, overly idealizes the physician–patient relationship, ignores possible personal and professional conflicts of interest, and leads to erosion of trust between dying patients and their doctors. Although many ethicists acknowledge that individual circumstances may make assisted suicide an ethically permissible action, most express concerns about potential abuse. Vulnerable members of society, such as the poor, old, and handicapped, could be pressed by financial and social factors into a suicide option in preference to palliative care. An additional argument against PAS or legalized euthanasia is that these approaches could provide solutions to the medical, social, and economic problems common to older and poor patients, that are simpler and less expensive than seeking and requiring more difficult but definitive remedies. Twenty- four years have passed since Oregon legalized and enacted PAS in 1997 and the Netherlands legalized both PAS and euthanasia, and data now provide some insights into whether these concerns about PAS and euthanasia are materializing. More than two-thirds of the US population polled favor legalizing PAS, and approximately 70% favored legalizing euthanasia.118 Sixty- five percent of US oncology patients favor a PAS or euthanasia option in end- of- life care,119 although physicians frequently involved in end- of- life care, such as ICU specialists, oncologists, and palliative care specialists, are the most uniformly opposed

specialists.120 This finding represents a significant and concerning discordance between physicians and their patients.

In Oregon, where PAS has been legalized for the longest period in the United States, data from 2021 (the most recent year available) show that patients who requested PAS were overwhelmingly likely to be White (95%) and had generally higher economic status and education, More than 99.2% were insured, and 98% were enrolled in hospice care. These figures raise questions of whether economic and/or healthaccess inequities or cultural issues prevent a more broad representation from other ethnic and educational classes. Most were more than 65 years old (81%), and 61% were suffering from cancer. As of 2021, one- third of patients receiving prescriptions for PAS (3280 patients over 24 years) never used them and ultimately died of their underlying disease – and many survived significantly longer after diagnosis than predicted. More than 93% of patients enrolled in the Oregon program cited loss of autonomy as a primary reason for pursuing PAS.121 Some authors have suggested that the possession of a means to end their lives legally and humanely prevented “preemptive” suicides that could have occurred earlier and thus may have prolonged lives.122

Concerns regarding abuses of PAS and euthanasia will undoubtedly continue, but currently, evidence of systematic abuse is lacking in places where PAS is legalized and regulated. As the population ages and patients desire greater control over end- of- life care and circumstances, debate over PAS and euthanasia in the United States can be expected to continue to grow.

ETHICAL ISSUES IN ORGAN TRANSPLANTATION ​

Two issues critical to vital organ transplantation that confront anesthesiologists are the concept of death by neurologic criteria (aka brain death) and the linkage of withdrawal of life- sustaining therapies with organ retrieval and transplantation after cardiac death (DCD).

Death by Neurologic Criteria ​

Advances in cardiopulmonary resuscitation and mechanical ventilation in the middle of the 20th century made it possible to postpone death, seemingly indefinitely. In 1968, the Ad Hoc Committee of the Harvard Medical School, under the leadership of Henry Beecher (recipient of the first endowed Chair of Anesthesiology in America) proposed redefining death as the point at which all cardiorespiratory function had irreversibly ceased, or all function of the whole brain had irreversibly stopped.123 The committee gave two explicit reasons for redefining death. The first was to allow patients to be declared dead and not maintained on machines, thus limiting expense and reallocating medical resources to other, salvageable patients, and allowing the social rituals surrounding death to occur. The second was to allow the donation of vital organs before circulation stopped.

Declaration of death has many nonmedical consequences, such as the process of mourning, initiation of any criminal prosecutions, inheritance, taxation, and burial concerns. It was thus important that determinations of death follow consistent rules across state boundaries, and the National Conference on Uniform State Laws issued

the Uniform Definition of Death Act in 1980, which was adopted by the states.124

Public acceptance of the concept of death by neurologic criteria has been slow, in part because it requires complete trust in physicians and ignores indicators of death that people already understand. Court cases challenging declaration of death in patients who clearly did meet standard neurologic criteria, such as that of Aden Hailu in Nevada, illustrate continued public uncertainty.124

Medical standards in diagnosing death by neurologic criteria are relatively straightforward, although variations in the definition of death itself persist throughout the United States. US medical standards require demonstration that, in the absence of drugs, paralytic agents, hypothermia, and other reversible conditions that mimic loss of brain function, no cortical or brainstem function is present. The diagnosis is made clinically, by physical examination demonstrating that cortical activity and brainstem reflexes are absent, or in cases where the clinical picture is uncertain, by adjunct testing demonstrating the complete absence of cerebral blood flow.

Death by neurologic criteria is a social and not a biologic definition of death, but medical, ethical, theological, and legal experts generally agree that it adequately defines a condition in which a person with ethical and legal rights and moral standing need no longer be treated as though alive. However, cultural, religious, and individual concepts of death are often ignored or disrespected in the setting of a diagnosis of neurologic death, leading to conflicts with families and loss of trust in physicians. This is particularly problematic when the diagnosis of death by neurologic criteria occurs in a potential vital organ donor, leading to fears that doctors have abandoned the patient in order to obtain organs for transplantation.

Despite assertions of organ procurement organizations to the contrary, death by neurologic criteria is not universally accepted among the world’s major religions, although organ transplantation itself is more widely approved. While Judaism and Christianity tend to accept cessation of organ function as a definition of death, for example, in one survey over 90% of Islamic jurists disagreed with a concept of neurologic death, and many other major religions (e.g. Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism) tend to view cessation of organ function as merely the beginning of a longer process of death involving consciousness and the soul. Even within some religions, beliefs can vary: Roman Catholicism accepts the concept of neurologic death, while the Orthodox Christian Church is silent, and many Protestant churches default to the individuals’ beliefs.125 And it bears emphasizing that individual patients and their families need not have specific religious affiliations in order to have significant and meaningful personal beliefs about what defines death.

Several legal cases such as those of Aden Hailu126 and Jahi McMath127 have highlighted a growing discordance between physicians and families, in which a perceived lack of respect by physicians for families and/or their religious values have led to passage of laws that recognize a religious exemption to neurologic death (New Jersey) and laws that require family informed consent prior to testing for neurologic death (New York, Kansas, Missouri). Other states have dealt with the issue through legislation specifying that the physician can proceed without consent (California,

Virginia, Nevada), although a “reasonable accommodation” of family preferences may be recommended.127,128

Before assuming care of a donor who has been declared dead neurologically, the anesthesiologist should be aware of the applicable laws in their region of practice, and sensitive to individual preferences of patients and their families. They should also review the chart for documentation of the declaration of death and the criteria on which it was based. If any questions exist about the diagnosis, organ donation should be postponed until the anesthesiologist is satisfied that these concerns are addressed.

Donation after Cardiac Death ​

DCD occurs when a patient wishes to have life- sustaining medical therapies withdrawn and also wishes to proceed with vital organ donation after death. Controlling the time and place of death so that timing of organ donation can be optimized has obvious medical and ethical advantages. The decision to donate organs is made before death, allowing time for discussion and informed consent. Organ ischemia time can be minimized. The dual decision to withdraw life- sustaining interventions and donate vital organs after death can create ethical conflicts, however. When a dying patient becomes an imminent organ donor, the risk is that the patient’s interests will be minimized or ignored in favor of the organ recipient.

National Academy of Medicine reviews of DCD in 1997129 and 2000130 found serious ethical questions, such as determining how quickly after asystole organ donation can begin and whether medications can ethically be administered to the donor before death that are solely for the purpose of organ preservation.

Ethical, theological, and legal principles prohibit us from killing one person to benefit another, but the point at which actual death has occurred in DCD donors is unclear. Although expedient donation is the very purpose of DCD, doctors must never sacrifice any living patient in the process or even take significant risks of doing so; the mistrust this would engender among the public could place the entire concept of DCD at risk by reducing potential donors and ultimately harming future potential organ recipients.130

Loss of consciousness appears to occur quickly after asystole, but brain function can continue for some time, and irreversible brain injury may not occur for many minutes. Yet some protocols call for organ retrieval to begin only two minutes after circulation has stopped, and in at least one institution allowed it to begin within seconds of cardiac arrest.131 Scientific and philosophic uncertainty exists about when death is complete; this could even lead to accusations that physicians deliberately kill patients to obtain organs for transplantation. Mistakes have occurred in some cases of brain- dead organ donors for which clear clinical criteria exist132 ; DCD organ donation is potentially even more prone to error because no clinical guidelines are universally accepted.

Donation of vital organs after brain death and DCD are both ethical and legal, but the interests of the dying patient must be absolutely protected until after death has occurred. Anesthesiologists can play a vital role in the organ donation process by helping hospitals develop reasonable and ethical policies for the management of brain- dead donors and DCD donors. It is inappropriate for any anesthesiologist to

be involved in organ DCD unless they have special expertise in the relevant ethical, legal, and medical issues and are experienced in end- of- life care.

Research Ethics ​

HUMAN SUBJECT RESEARCH ​

The premise that the physician always puts the best interests of the patient first can be jeopardized when research objectives enter the doctor–patient relationship. Human subjects are asked to put aside their own interests to benefit a future, hypothetic group of patients. In extreme cases, the patient becomes a research “object” who will not benefit personally from the experiment at all. Two examples include experiments in healthy subjects and phase I cancer trials in terminally ill patients in which the goal is to determine the toxicity of treatment – not remission, palliation, or cure.

Human subject research must balance many conflicting interests, such as the needs and rights of the research subject, the hypothetical interests of future patients, and the physician’s financial, professional, and personal goals. Academic or corporate advancement, personal prestige, and financial incentives may be disincentives to researchers who diligently protect patients’ interests or who remain objective in designing protocols and analyzing and reporting their findings. Thus, human subject research is more closely regulated, supervised, and controlled than any other medical endeavor.

Regulation of research began after World War II, with the Nuremburg Code and the Helsinki Declaration outlining the ethical obligations of physicians engaged in human research. The United States was slow to awaken to the parallels between the concentration camp experiments and the sometimes gruesome treatment to which they subjected their own subjects in similar trials.133 In the years that followed the Doctors’ Trial at Nuremburg, Beecher and others134,135 found that researchers were aware of the standards set at Nuremberg but regularly violated them. In 1974, the National Research Act established the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research, out of which the modern institutional review board was born.

The ethical conduct of human subject research follows three principles: (1) respect for autonomy and the obligation to protect subjects with limited autonomy; (2) beneficence, with obligations to minimize risks, maximize benefits, and ensure that the research design is scientifically sound; and (3) justice, the obligation to treat each person with regard to what is morally right and to ensure fair distribution of benefits and burdens.

In addition to the comprehensive presentation to research subjects of the risks and benefits of procedures or medications to which they will be subjected, disclosure must include the possibility of commercialization of the results, financial interests of the researchers, and any other actual or perceived conflicts of interest on the part of researchers and their institutions and sponsors. Subjects must be free to refuse or end participation at any time without penalty. Situational coercion , in which subjects feel that they are not truly free to refuse, should be avoided or

mitigated. Examples of situational coercion include prisoners whose terms and experiences of incarceration may be affected by their decision to participate or refuse, and hospitalized patients who may believe that their care could be compromised if they do not cooperate with researchers.133

Monetary or other inducements to participate in research are probably permissible if they do not undermine the freedom of the subject to refuse under reasonable circumstances. Significant monetary awards may have adverse effects on the autonomy of subjects and may negatively affect the scientific quality of the research. If remuneration is high, for example, subjects could conceal factors that would otherwise disqualify them from participating, thus compromising the research results and exposing themselves to greater risks. The determination of whether a remuneration is “high” varies with the subject: a person suffering from homelessness or hunger may find even a very modest compensation difficult to refuse.

Researchers are obligated to maximize benefits and minimize potential harms, including physical, psychological, social, legal, and financial harms. The research must address a question of significant value to justify the level of risk and must follow the approved protocol. Findings must be promptly and accurately reported. The research must be terminated immediately if it is suspected to be harmful to the participants.

Anesthesiology research often involves the treatment or prevention of unpleasant symptoms, such as pain and nausea. When effective treatments are well established, such studies should be restricted to comparison of treatments with known efficacy, not placebo- controlled trials, and “escape” analgesics or antiemetics must be provided on the patient’s request.

No population group should be unfairly subjected to research without having equal access to its benefits. Finally, the interests of the individual must always prevail over the interests of society.

Children as Research Subjects ​

Children are particularly vulnerable as research subjects because they may lack the ability to make mature decisions, are subject to the authority of others, may defer to their parents and others in ways that mask underlying dissent, and may have conditions requiring immediate decisions not consistent with informed consent.136 Children’s rights are frequently undervalued, whereas parental authority may be overvalued. Studies show that even children with decision- making capacity are often excluded from the consent process by both parents and physicians.137

If a minor child is “able” to assent, then assent must usually be obtained in addition to the consent of any legal surrogate decision makers. In the United States, federal law requires assent of any minor age 7 years and older to participate in medical research.138 Particularly for research in which no substantive individual benefit is expected, many ethicists believe that a child’s dissent must always be honored.136,137

ETHICS OF ANIMAL RESEARCH ​

In 1959, William Russell and Rex Burch published The Principles of Humane Experimental Technique , introducing the

concept that humane treatment of animals was not merely an ethical imperative, but absolutely necessary to highquality research.139 Federal legislation protecting animal welfare began with the Laboratory Animal Welfare Act of 1966. In 1985, the Health Extension Act and amendments to the Animal Welfare Act required the establishment of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees, which oversee conditions of laboratory animals; review and approve animal research protocols; educate and train investigators in ethical issues and aspects of animal handling such as anesthesia, analgesia, and euthanasia; and act as community liaisons.

Some researchers deny that animal experimentation could be subject to any moral reservations and assert that medical advances have been and continue to be completely dependent on continued animal research. Many animal welfare activists insist on the moral equivalence of animal experimentation to that of human experimentation and accuse researchers of being blind to or, even worse, actually unmoved by the suffering of animal subjects. The simplicity of these polarized views does not do justice to the complexity of the issues.

Advances in the understanding of animal cognition led most biologists to believe that many, if not all, animals are capable of feeling pleasure, pain, anticipation, and fear, and thus experience both enjoyment and suffering. Many bioethicists state that the higher animals therefore have adequate awareness to possess moral standing, although how much moral standing is intensely debated.140,141 Allowing animal suffering as a result of pain, fear, sickness, or poor standards of care is a moral harm that must be avoided, mitigated, and weighed heavily against the benefits it produces. Ethicists maintain that cruelty to animals is immoral and that animals should be protected from it, not merely because they have moral standing, but because he who is cruel to animals is more likely to be cruel to humans.142

Researchers have obligations to provide clean and humane conditions and appropriate veterinary care for animal subjects. Researchers should mind the “Three Rs”— replacement, reduction, and refinement—that is, use animal subjects only when necessary, minimize any suffering incurred in the study, and seek non- animate replacements for animal subjects. Mediocre or repetitive research using animal subjects should not be allowed. It is the responsibility of the medical and scientific community to continue aggressively to seek and promote alternatives to the use of animal subjects.

Physician Participation in Executions ​

US professional organizations for physicians have consistently stated that physicians’ participation in executions is unethical, yet many physicians admit that they would agree to be involved. The role of physicians in euthanasia and executions is of particular concern to anesthesiologists, who have been identified as ideal candidates for duties that involve killing because of their particular professional skills. Arguments in favor of physicians’ involvement in executions usually cite the principle of beneficence in allowing a humane death. Evidence for the belief that lethal injection

prevents suffering during execution is lacking, and many experts have provided scientific evidence that it may be associated with awake paralysis and suffocation143 and other forms of protracted suffering.

Historically, such beneficence arguments have also led to “slippery slope” justifications for physicians to be involved in the killing of persons who have never faced an accuser or had a fair hearing – such killings have included persons with physical or mental handicaps and other “social flaws” for the “benefit” of the individual and of society as a whole.144 Once physicians accept a beneficence argument for participating in executions, it is difficult ethically to draw the line at participating in other state- sponsored activities, such as torture, coercion, and “medical incarceration,” because these activities are also usually defended as being beneficial to society.145

When physicians agree to participate in an execution, they act as agents of the state while appearing to act on behalf of the “patient.” This situation can lead to eventual erosion of public trust and respect. It also at times undoubtedly leads to physicians’ participation in the killing of innocent persons.146

To avoid intolerable self- condemnation, virtually all executioners undergo “moral disengagement” in which they dehumanize the convicts and devalue their lives, thereby deflecting moral responsibility for the execution away from themselves by blaming juries, judges, governors, and “the law” rather than accept the responsibility they share in ending the prisoner’s life.147 It is difficult to reconcile the medical profession’s overt ethical imperatives of valuing human life, respecting individuals, and accepting personal moral responsibility with participation in a process that requires a rejection of these very values.

In 1980, the AMA issued an opinion that physicians’ involvement in capital punishment is unethical and defined “participation” broadly as including not only actions that themselves would lead to the death of the condemned but also any activity that assisted, supervised, or contributed to another’s being able to do so.148 However, no direct sanctions of any participating physician were addressed. In 2010, the American Board of Anesthesiology became the first organization of physicians not only to condemn participation in capital punishment as unethical but also to state that physicians who were certified by their board and who participated in lethal injection would be subject to disciplinary action that could include revocation of their diplomate status.149

Acceptance of moral objections to certain hotly contended issues such as abortion or PAS may be reasonable, but objections to well- established standards, such as informed consent, are not. Moral objections of physicians are also likely to carry more weight if they involve concepts that the physician believes supports him or her as an ethical doctor, and not just as an ethical person, because these concepts are more likely to be founded in professionally established standards than in personal beliefs. When physicians’ personal religious and other moral beliefs are allowed to supersede those of patients, the result has almost uniformly been barriers in health care to vulnerable groups, such as women, adolescents, and the elderly.151

In early 2018, the US Department of Health and Human Services moved under conservative political leadership to strengthen doctors’ rights to refuse to refer patients for legal medical treatments that they personally object to, such as abortion, transgender treatments, and birth control, for example. The AMA has raised concerns that the move both combines and illegally broadens laws that have not been subjected to public vote. Dr. Lainie Friedman Ross, physician and ethicist at the University of Chicago, points out that “it is very problematic because it forgets that doctors are powerful individuals and patients are vulnerable. The law is all about protecting doctors, not the individuals who need the protection – those who are sick and frightened.”152 It remains to be seen whether the new rules will withstand inevitable legal challenges. In the meantime, a Canadian court ruled in the opposite direction, that the requirement to refer patients to legal medical treatments in Ontario was “a reasonable limit on the religious freedom of doctors, necessary to prevent harm and inequitable access for patients.”152
